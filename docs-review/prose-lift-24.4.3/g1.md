## 1. The Blank Slate

**Whose story:** Solvei Brask, born in the policy's first year to a mother in −2. She is raised in a facility, placed in Main at eighteen, meets a family she has no template for, and becomes the caregiver who takes her Sanctuary friend's baby at intake.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Blank Slate
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Child Protection Divergence · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.3
Outcome: Universal autoparenting eliminates inherited disadvantage and destroys the family as a civilizational institution
```

Solvei Brask was born in the first year of the policy and went from the delivery room to the Brenn Hill facility, where she lived until she was eighteen. Every child in the nation was raised in a centralized autoparenting facility like it from birth, whatever layer their parents occupied. Her file named a mother in -2. The Sanctuary children slept in the next dormitory, and all of them were fed, housed, educated, medically covered and behaviorally monitored identically.

Their nation was VMSS-adjacent and ran a five-ring gradient model, and the founders' argument, cut into the atrium wall, was the logical extension of VMSS's own child protection doctrine. *If children born in lower layers deserve the right to relocate to Main,* the wall read, *why leave the burden of exercising that right on the child? Why should a seven-year-old in -2 need to choose to leave their parent? Give every child the same start and let their own conduct sort them.* The terms ran underneath in smaller letters. Parents in all layers kept visitation rights, custody did not exist, and at eighteen each citizen would be placed in the layer their own behavioral profile warranted.

The facilities were well-designed and well-funded. The caregivers were AI-assisted and worked to strict developmental protocols, and the children's nutrition, education, socialization, medical care and emotional support were calibrated by developmental psychology rather than by the variable quality of individual parents. Solvei learned cooperation and the behavioral norms early. At bedtime a caregiver read her dormitory one story, as the protocol set. When she ran a fever at six, the night caregiver took her temperature on the hour, logged it, and moved on to the next bed.

The founders' hypothesis was that removing parental variability from child development would produce more consistent outcomes, and every year the cohort's published figures confirmed it. On every measurable institutional metric, from literacy and health to behavioral stability and social development, the autoparented generation outperformed both Earth children and VMSS children raised by lower-layer parents.

At eighteen the whole cohort's placements came through on one morning. Ninety-four percent went to Main Layer, Solvei among them. Three percent qualified for Sanctuary on early behavioral trajectory, and Marit, who had slept in the next bed for twelve years, was one of them. Three percent went to -1, almost entirely for conduct during their institutional upbringing itself; Rian from the east wing went for the fights. Nobody went to -2 or -3. It was the most uniformly well-behaved generation in the civilization's history, and the founders cited it as definitive validation.

In Main Layer she met families. Dain, from the next bench at the survey office, had been raised by his parents. The first time he took her home, his mother Edda argued with him about his job through two courses, told the story of the winter he was four and cut his own hair, and brought up something from when he was twelve that sent him out of the room. He came back for dessert.

"What were you like at four?" Edda asked Solvei.

Solvei thought about it. "I was in the top decile for language."

Dain laughed, and then saw that she meant it.

Later, at the sink, Edda handed her a plate to dry. "He was my favorite from the day he was born," she said. "Don't tell his sister."

They lasted two years. In the second spring the founders published the cohort's first adult figures: her generation formed romantic partnerships at a lower rate than the parent-raised and reported difficulty with attachment. The report found them psychologically sound, with no template for what unconditional attachment feels like. That summer Dain said he couldn't tell whether she would notice if he left, and she had no answer for him. He moved out in the autumn.

Solvei took a caregiver's post at Brenn Hill at twenty-six. Between twenty-five and forty her cohort reproduced at about 60% of the rate of the parent-raised population. The shortfall was behavioral, since people raised without the experience of family were less likely to create families, and the nation placed no limit on reproduction.

Marit's daughter was born in the thirty-first year of the policy and came to Brenn Hill the same day. Marit had a perfect behavioral record, a stable partnership and every institutional qualification for parenthood, and she carried the baby to the intake desk herself.

"Corin's mother still calls him every morning," she said. "To ask what he's eating."

Solvei took the baby and checked the band on her wrist.

"I want to raise her," Marit said. "I don't know how. I'd learn."

"You can visit her."

"I know I can visit her."

Five years later a caregiver from VMSS came to Brenn Hill on a visit: Kasimir Lund, from one of the autoparenting facilities in VMSS's Main Layer. The packet he left had VMSS's clause on its first page: children born in lower layers remain with their parents, and the right to relocate to Main Layer is a standing human right, exercisable at any age. The page called the weight this put on a child a real cost and accepted it as the price of keeping the bond between parent and child. Solvei read it at the intake desk while he waited.

"So yours come to you," Solvei said.

"When they decide to." He had grown up in -2 with his father. "I was seven when I understood I could go. I thought about it most nights for a year."

"Why didn't you?"

He took a while. "I liked him."

Each year fewer of the caregivers on Solvei's floor had been raised by parents, and more had come out of the facilities, as she had. The older ones still did the voices when they read; Solvei read the words as written. The children on her floor were healthy and well taught, and Brenn Hill ran as well as it ever had.

That night Marit's daughter asked for another story. Solvei checked the protocol and said goodnight.

Key lesson: In VMSS the choice to move to Main belongs to the child, so that no parent in any layer loses a child by rule. Raising every child in an institution improves health, schooling and conduct, and weakens the family.

Footnote — The Self-Correcting Policy: The 60% reproductive rate sets the policy's own expiration date. A nation that reproduces at 60% of replacement rate faces population contraction within two generations. The autoparented cohort's reluctance to form families is a demographic trajectory, and it forces the nation either to modify the policy or to import population from allied nations whose citizens were raised by parents. The Blank Slate repeals itself through demographics. The question is whether the charter committee recognizes this before the contraction becomes structural, or whether the policy's institutional momentum outlasts the population it was designed to serve.

## 2. The Equal Floor

**Whose story:** Dara Kolvey, a Main Layer bookkeeper. A colleague's message tells her −1 is the same money, she commits the fraud that sends her there, finds it comfortable, and later reads that she is part of the rising reassignment rate.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Equal Floor
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Economic Gradient Divergence · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.3
Outcome: Equal UBI across all layers removes the economic dimension of consequence and produces lower layers that are materially indistinguishable from Main
```

Dara Kolvey drew $10,000 a month, the same UBI as every citizen in every ring of her nation. It was a five-ring ally of VMSS, and its founders had equalized UBI across all layers on the principle that dignity should not be graduated. VMSS's halving cascade, $10,000 to $5,000 to $2,500 to $1,250, treated the reduction as part of the consequence. The founders held that the consequence was the layer: the environment, the enforcement posture, the institutional withdrawal, the social texture. A -2 resident already lived among people reassigned for predatory violence, with partial institutional presence and elevated revival failure rates, and halving their UBI on top of that would change their purchasing power without changing their behavior. The founders called that redundant punishment. From the first year it did what they intended: no resident of any layer experienced economic deprivation as part of their consequence.

Dara kept the accounts at a freight broker in Main. In the nation's twelfth year her colleague Wenna was reassigned to -1, and her first message came back within the week.

*Same money,* Wenna wrote. *Same shops, same food. The clinic's farther and everyone on my street did something.*

The next spring Dara began moving small sums out of client accounts. She was caught in the fourth month and reassigned to -1.

Her UBI stayed $10,000, paid in -1 currency. Currency siloing held, so money did not convert across layers, but within each layer the economic floor was identical. What changed for her was social and institutional: her institutional services were reduced, the enforcement posture on her street was different, and her neighbors' behavioral histories were the reasons they were there. She ate the same food, bought the same goods and used the same consumer economy as in Main. She ate well and lived comfortably, and when she took mornings at a bakery it was for something to do.

Mateo, who had grown up in VMSS, came down to see her in her second month. He walked her street twice.

"Where is everybody?"

"Home, mostly. They don't have to work."

"In VMSS's -1 people are running things," he said. "Stalls, repair shops, delivery crews. They're on five thousand, half of Main, and it's its own place."

"Do they starve?"

"Nobody starves in any ring." He looked at her door, the bakery bag, her new shoes. "There you'd have gone down four ways. Different ring, different people, different services, half the money."

"This is Main with a different AR color scheme," Dara said.

Her brother Joren was in -2, reassigned for violence years before, and in her third year in -1 she went down to see him. The economic constraint on VMSS's -2 was severe and deliberate, so -2 felt harsh in a way that reinforced the severity of the conduct that put people there. Joren got $10,000. His building was full of people reassigned for predatory violence, and a man followed her up two flights of stairs before he stopped. Joren's flat had a new kitchen and three locks on the door. He locked all three behind her without looking and went back to the stove.

"In VMSS I'd be on twenty-five hundred," he said. "A quarter of Main. What's -1 like?"

"Like moving to a worse neighborhood."

In the twentieth year the founders published two decades of behavioral data. -1 reassignment rates ran about 6% higher than VMSS's: the marginal population that the full four-dimensional consequence would have deterred was not deterred by the three-dimensional version. The increase was modest, and the founders called it acceptable variance. The rate was not stable, though. It rose about 0.3% a year, as the population came to see -1 as Main Layer with less institutional presence but equal money. Dara read it on her front step. Wenna's message was still on her handset.

Key lesson: UBI in every layer already guarantees dignity; VMSS lowers it at each ring so that a reassignment is felt in money as well as in surroundings. Without that, the step down to -1 deters fewer people each year.

Footnote — The Tax Fork: The simulation leaves out a second-order problem that equal UBI creates for the tax gradient. VMSS's lower layers pay reduced tax rates because they receive fewer institutional services, partial taxation for partial institutions, and -3 Terminal sits at the bottom of the exact cascade (6.25% top marginal) as a civilizational membership acknowledgment rather than a redistribution mechanism. ## 3. The Spending Ceiling

**Whose story:** Hester Quill, a fabrication specialist in −2 of the Equal Floor nation. She watches the tax fork decided, hears the infrastructure gap from her sister in −1 and her old apprentice in −3, and becomes the monopolist her neighbor cannot afford.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Spending Ceiling
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Economic Architecture Divergence · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.3.3
Outcome: Equal UBI in degraded institutional infrastructure brings inflation, artisan monopolies and unequal purchasing power
```

Hester Quill ran a fabrication shop in -2 of the Equal Floor nation, where every citizen in every ring drew $10,000 a month. The nation had kept VMSS's graduated institutional withdrawal: full services in Sanctuary and Main, partial in -1, reduced in -2, and the federal floor only in -3. It had kept the graduated tax structure as well, heavy and progressive in the upper layers to fund the full institutional apparatus, and reduced in the lower layers to reflect their reduced services. It had changed the income. Every layer now drew Main-Layer money, and the infrastructure had not changed with it. The economy of -2 had been built for $2,500.

The tax question reached the founders in the first fiscal year, and it had no clean answer. VMSS reduced lower-layer tax rates because it reduced lower-layer institutional services, partial taxation for partial institutions, down to -3 Terminal at the bottom of the exact cascade: a 6.25% top marginal rate, kept as a civilizational membership acknowledgment rather than a redistribution mechanism. The founders had equalized income without equalizing institutional presence, and the tax gradient now sat on a contradiction. Hester watched the hearings on the screen above her bench.

Option one kept the reduced lower-layer rates, and at -2's rate Hester would keep significantly more of her $10,000 than a Main Layer resident kept under heavy progressive taxation. Option two put every citizen on Main-Layer progressive rates, whatever ring they lived in. The financial inversion would disappear, and disposable income would come out equal. But residents of -1, -2 and -3 would be taxed at rates for institutional services they did not receive, and Hester would pay Main's rate for a fraction of the infrastructure that Main's taxes funded: full taxation for partial institutions. Option two would swap one injustice for another, economic deprivation as redundant punishment for taxation without corresponding services, and that had historically produced civic resentment faster than almost any other policy failure. One founder stood and read from VMSS's FAQ.

"It would not be equitable to tax citizens at rates corresponding to services they are not receiving."

"Keep the old rates," another founder answered, "and a -3 resident under Terminal's minimal effective taxation keeps nearly all of their ten thousand."

The founders chose option one. They reasoned that taxing citizens for services they did not receive was a more visible injustice than a tax-rate differential most citizens would never calculate. The calculation took about one news cycle. A Main commentator ran it on air the week the rates were published: the upper layers paid heavy taxes for the whole institutional apparatus, while the lower layers drew the same income with a fraction of the tax burden and none of the institutional overhead. The consequence architecture had inverted. The punitive layers were now the most economically advantageous layers in the civilization, and reassignment was a financial upgrade.

"The founders set out to equalize dignity," the commentator said. "They've equalized income and built a tax shelter in the punitive rings."

Hester's cousin in Main sent her the clip with one line: *Room down there?*

Main Layer ran a fully serviced post-scarcity economy, built to absorb $10,000 a citizen at full institutional density. Hester's sister Fenna lived in -1, where about 60% of Main's institutional infrastructure was present and the economy had been designed for people spending $5,000 a month. Demand doubled overnight and supply did not change, so Main-Layer money was chasing -1-Layer goods. For two years Fenna's messages were cheerful. The shops were absorbing the extra spending and expanding their stock, and the one below her flat had hired two more people. In the third year the constraint turned structural. The automated medical clinics, fabrication-grade consumer goods and high-density entertainment infrastructure that Main had were not in -1 at all, because VMSS's graduated withdrawal had removed them.

*I've got ten thousand a month,* Fenna wrote, *and I can only spend six before the shops run out of things to sell me.*

In -2 the gap was severe. Institutional presence there was so reduced that private enterprise filled most civic functions, and a partially withdrawn hybrid economy calibrated for $2,500 now took in $10,000. In the service categories that needed institutional infrastructure to deliver, medical services, fabrication access, entertainment systems and automated retail, demand ran four times supply. Everyone on Hester's street could afford all of it, and the layer could not provide it. Her waiting list ran into the next season.

In -3 the gap was total. Emrys, who had learned the trade at Hester's bench, maintained power and water lines there. VMSS's design had withdrawn daily governance from -3 and left the federal floor in place, and the economy was a capitalist frontier, with minimal institutional infrastructure scaled for $1,250 and no automated service delivery beyond basic utilities.

*Ten thousand a month,* Emrys wrote, *and almost nowhere to put it.*

The gap made the skilled artisan into essential infrastructure, at a scale VMSS's traditional architecture never produces. When Hester's cousin in Main wanted medical attention, he visited an automated clinic. In -2, anyone who wanted more than the reduced institutional baseline needed a private practitioner like Dr. Saye two streets over, one of a small number of qualified providers serving a population with $10,000 a month and acute demand. People like Saye, the pipe fitters, the engineer who kept the district's grid up and Hester herself had become the de facto economy of the lower layers. They were the only supply for institutional-grade demand, and every resident had Main-Layer money to pay them with. In VMSS's -2 economy a fabrication specialist like Hester would have earned a comfortable, unremarkable living. By the fifth year she was the most economically powerful person in her district. She set her prices and chose her clients, and she decided who received her services and at what quality.

That year the feed ran the comparison. Prices had risen accordingly: a consultation with a practitioner like Dr. Saye cost three to four times what one cost in VMSS's -2, because demand at $10,000 a month overwhelmed a supply scaled for $2,500, and fees had settled where the two met. The equal floor had produced radically unequal purchasing power. A Main Layer resident's $10,000 bought a Main-Layer life. After -2's inflated prices, a -2 resident's $10,000 bought purchasing power comparable to or worse than VMSS's $2,500 baseline in the same layer.

Odo, from the ground floor, brought in a cracked knee brace.

"Eleven weeks," Hester said. "Or this week at double."

"I get ten thousand a month, same as you."

"So does everyone on the list." She turned the brace over. "The grid engineer's paying double."

Odo took the brace back.

By the fifteenth year each lower layer had divided into three economic classes, a stratification the founders had not anticipated, and Hester's street held all three. Hester drew the same $10,000 UBI as everyone else, and her service income multiplied it several times over; the artisans were accumulating wealth at a rate no other part of the layer could match. Odo's sister cut hair, a marketable skill though not an essential one, and it earned enough to offset the inflated prices. Odo had no specialized skill. He spent his $10,000 on inflated goods and services, and it bought him a floor well below what $10,000 bought in Main.

The stratification was sharpest in -3, where, with minimal institutional infrastructure, virtually every service was private. Its private security operators, its medical providers and the engineers who maintained power and water, Emrys among them, had become a de facto governing class that controlled access to the services that made -3 livable. Every -3 resident's UBI was enough to bid for those services. It did not make the services exist in sufficient quantity to serve every bidder. The frontier capitalism VMSS's -3 produced under a $1,250 baseline now ran under a $10,000 one: the same economic dynamics at a higher price point, with the same winners and losers and the same stratification the founders had believed equal income would prevent. Emrys's neighbors had high nominal incomes and a low effective quality of life.

*I set the water price for the district now,* Emrys wrote. *Security and the doctors set theirs. Between us we take most of everybody's ten thousand.*

That spring Odo came up with another brace, cracked in the same place. Hester wrote his name at the bottom of the list.

Key lesson: VMSS matches each layer's UBI to its taxes, its institutions and what its economy can supply. Money beyond that goes to whoever controls the supply.
