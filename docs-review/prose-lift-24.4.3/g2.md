## 1. The Layer in Name Only

**Whose story:** Anja Teller, one of the ally's founders. She writes the +2 line and runs the differentiation roadmap, her own SAD thins behind the wall, and the decommissioning paper that is refused is hers.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Layer in Name Only
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Ring Resolution Divergence · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.4
Outcome: A sixth ring above Sanctuary offers nothing Sanctuary lacks, and the nation keeps it indefinitely because dismantling it would mean admitting that
```

Anja Teller was one of the founders, and the line describing +2 in their announcement was hers: *the recognition that sustained excellence deserves distinction beyond the Sanctuary threshold.* Her nation, an ally of VMSS, was launching a six-ring gradient model. Below Sanctuary it matched VMSS exactly: Main Layer, -1, -2 and -3, with the same enforcement, the same institutional withdrawal and the same UBI cascade. Above Sanctuary the founders added +2, a ring gated at 92 STI and reserved for the highest-trust citizens in the civilization. Anja put the treaty application together herself. It carried architectural drawings of a sixth mega-wall, a dedicated +2 infrastructure plan, and a population projection of approximately 50 million residents drawn from the upper end of Sanctuary's behavioral spectrum.

The alliance approved the application. Gradient governance with six rings still qualified, and the layer equivalence mapping was straightforward: for border protocol purposes, +2 mapped to VMSS Sanctuary. The nation built the wall.

The founders had understood that +2 could not offer less than Sanctuary, since a ring above the highest ring cannot withdraw services, so +2 launched with every feature Sanctuary offered. Anja crossed the new mega-wall with the first residents into a place that was, by every measurable metric, identical to the Sanctuary they had just left. The economics were full post-scarcity, and her first monthly UBI deposit in +2 was $10,000, in the same currency as +1 and Main. The services directory listed TIP, the pre-intervention enforcement that halted harmful acts before completion, then full augmentation and longevity access, best-tier revival fidelity and full institutional services.

The founders had expected that for year one. The plan was always to set +2 apart over time, and the differentiation roadmap was Anja's to run: five years to add exclusive research facilities, priority access to experimental technology, a governance consultation role and enhanced neural diving infrastructure. In year one +2 was Sanctuary with a higher number, and the founders called it *the foundation phase*.

She began with the exclusive research facilities. Sanctuary already had collaborative research facilities, and restricting research access by STI bracket went against the meritocratic principle that research capability is credentialed by competence rather than behavioral score. The first admissions list shut out Viggo Lemm, an 87-STI researcher with a century of materials science experience, and let in a 93-STI resident with no research background. Viggo called her from Sanctuary the week it was posted.

"What's behind your wall that I haven't got down here?" he said.

"Nothing yet."

"Then who is it for? The man you took has never worked a bench in his life."

The rest of the roadmap went the same way. Beside the experimental-technology item in her draft she wrote: *Meritboard already governs deployment, by competence and institutional need. An STI bracket has no relationship to who can use it productively.* Beside the governance consultation role she wrote: *Sanctuary already takes part in petition-based regulatory mechanisms. A 92+ gate makes an elite legislative class, gated by behavioral score instead of demonstrated policy competence.*

The 92 gate selected people who had scored seven points higher on the same metric, and by year five she had found no service, no facility, no institutional feature and no governance role that logically belonged behind it rather than behind the 85 gate Sanctuary already provided. The roadmap was quietly shelved.

+2 ran as Sanctuary with a higher STI floor and a smaller population. The wall had changed the residents' address, and they lived identically to +1's, with the same enforcement, economics, institutional access, services, augmentation and revival. The only measurable difference was population density: 50 million behind the +2 wall, 250 million in +1. That year's residents' survey reported satisfaction with the *intimacy* and *exclusivity* of +2.

By then the phrase had taken hold across the alliance as diplomatic shorthand: layer in name only. At a trade fair a buyer from another allied nation asked Anja how many rings her country ran.

"Six," she said.

"Five plus what?"

Anja read the VMSS analysts' review of the comparative data. On citizen satisfaction, behavioral stability, creative output, institutional trust, longevity outcomes, revival success or any other metric, +2 differed from Sanctuary by no more than statistical noise.

She felt it most in her own Selective Ascension Domain, +2's Cognitive Clarity Domain. The SADs were metric-gated micro-communities with functional lifestyle distinctions, the one mechanism that actually gave meaningful differentiation above Sanctuary, and the wall that was supposed to improve on them left them drawing on +2's 50 million instead of Sanctuary's full 300 million. A Cognitive Clarity Domain that sustained 40,000 residents from a 300-million base sustained approximately 6,500 from +2's pool. The micro-communities that had made the domains vibrant, with familiar faces within weeks, intellectual density and small-town cultural life, thinned toward the minimum viable population. Several SADs in +2 failed to reach critical mass and sat functionally dormant. In Anja's, the reading room kept its spare chairs stacked by the door.

By year fifteen the consensus inside the nation's governance apparatus was that +2 did not justify its infrastructure, and Anja wrote the paper that said so. The mega-wall cost maintenance. The administrative overhead of a sixth ring (separate population tracking, separate infrastructure budgets, a border protocol that processed citizens crossing between two identical environments) produced no governance return. The rational decision was decommissioning: dissolve +2, reintegrate its population into Sanctuary, and acknowledge that five rings exhausts the meaningful structural gradients. On the paper's last page she wrote:

*A sixth ring above Sanctuary is architecturally incoherent. The five-ring model already delivers everything the architecture can provide at the Sanctuary threshold. TIP is the enforcement ceiling, post-scarcity the economic ceiling, full augmentation the health ceiling. A ring above Sanctuary would need to offer something that requires ring-level infrastructure: its own mega-wall, its own enforcement posture, its own economic baseline, its own institutional character. Nothing qualifies. SADs are the differentiation mechanism for any population filter above the Sanctuary baseline, and they filter on a functional criterion such as cognitive clarity, non-attachment or creative output.*

Ivar Stenmark, who had signed the founding document beside her, brought the paper back to her office himself.

"Take the wall down and we tell everyone the founding architecture had a ring in it that served no function," he said. "That our signature divergence from the five-ring model was a wall between identical rooms, from the start."

"It was."

"Then that's what we'd be telling them." He left the paper on her desk.

The nation did not decommission +2. The political cost of that admission exceeded the maintenance cost of the wall. Twenty years after the founding, the founding document still described +2 in Anja's words.

That spring she went down to Sanctuary to see Viggo. At the crossing an officer ran her through the border protocol, logged her out of +2 and into +1, and asked the purpose of her visit.

"Lunch," Anja said.

Viggo was at his bench when she arrived, running a sample through the same furnace model the facility behind the wall had bought.

Key lesson: Above Sanctuary's threshold, enforcement, economics, infrastructure and institutional services are already at their maximum, so a higher trust score has nothing left to unlock. Any further distinction has to come from small communities that select their members for a way of living.

## 2. The Human Bench

**Whose story:** Tereza Holm, a judge on one of the first panels. The mercy case, the sympathy gradient and the geography of outcomes all pass across her own table, and the last file she reads is her first case decided the other way.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Human Bench
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Judicial Architecture Divergence · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.3
Outcome: Human judges produce just outcomes more slowly, less consistently, and with a sympathy gradient that corrodes categorical consequence
```

Tereza Holm was sworn onto one of the first judicial panels of the Human Bench, a VMSS-adjacent nation that operated a five-ring gradient model. The nation had kept the full VMSS technology stack: implants, STI, backup vessels, mega-walls, currency siloing, the UBI cascade, the kill switch. The implants still recorded behavioral data, the STI ledger still tracked trust, and the implant's non-repudiable record of what happened was still the evidentiary foundation. The founders replaced one thing, AI governance for consequence delivery. In VMSS the evaluation was mechanical and consistent: the AI governance system evaluated the behavioral data against established thresholds and produced a categorical determination, the same one for the same fully evaluated case in every district. Here a panel of three human judges reviewed the implant record, heard the citizen's context and rendered a determination.

At the swearing-in one of the founders read out a passage from VMSS's charter: *…the same consequence classification regardless of which district it occurs in… No judge weighs the case. No judicial discretion modulates the outcome. The absence of a judge is not a gap — it is the design.* A few of the new judges laughed.

"The implant tells you what happened," the founder said, "and you catch the nuance a mechanical evaluation flattens."

For the first years the panels' output was, by several measures, superior to VMSS's automated system. Human judges handled edge cases that automated thresholds evaluated crudely. In the first year Tereza's panel heard Matthias Coyle, who had discovered his partner's infidelity and, under extreme emotional duress, struck her once, causing minor injury. The act technically met the threshold for -1 reassignment. The panel weighed the provocation, the severity, the absence of any pattern and Coyle's full behavioral history, and determined that the act, while qualifying under strict threshold analysis, did not warrant reassignment. They imposed an STI consequence and monitored rehabilitation, and Coyle remained in Main Layer.

Afterward Benno Kray hung up his robe beside hers. "In VMSS he'd have gone to the AI," he said. "Article XII, multi-factor evaluation."

"Which weighs context, severity, pattern and cumulative history. What we just did."

"So it might have kept him in Main too. Or it might not." He buttoned his coat. "Put him in front of three panels here and you might get three different outcomes."

Over the next decade the panels produced a measurable pattern the founders had not anticipated, a sympathy gradient, and Tereza watched it form at her own table. One spring the panel heard Linnea Ostberg, a surveyor from a Sanctuary family, who arrived with a prepared statement and put her conduct inside a story the judges found compelling. She was articulate, remorseful and easy to sympathize with. Three days later it heard Hakon Dray, a dock loader with little schooling, whose implant record showed the same act.

"You've got the recording," Hakon said. "Watch it."

Ostberg received monitored rehabilitation, and Hakon was reassigned. Tereza voted with the majority both times.

After fifteen years the national data showed a statistically significant correlation between presentation quality and outcome, controlling for offense severity, behavioral history and all other variables. The gradient correlated with education, social class and articulacy. Citizens from upper-layer backgrounds who were evaluated for qualifying breaches presented more effectively: they were accustomed to institutional interaction, understood narrative framing and arrived at the panel better prepared. Citizens from lower-layer backgrounds, citizens with limited education and citizens who expressed themselves through anger rather than contrition presented less effectively. Tereza found Ostberg and Hakon in the appendix, two rows apart.

The variability compounded as the system scaled. By then the nation operated approximately 4,000 judicial panels processing consequence evaluations across five layers, each composed of three judges drawn from a qualified pool. Judge quality varied, and so did panel composition. Regional judicial cultures emerged: panels in Tereza's district developed a reputation for leniency, and panels in another district one for severity. A citizen's outcome depended on what they had done, and also on which district they lived in, which panel heard their case and which three judges sat on it that day. The implant record, the non-repudiable account of exactly what happened, was the same in every courtroom.

By year twenty-five, identical offenses produced reassignment in one district and monitored rehabilitation in another, at a rate that made the system's outputs partially dependent on geography. VMSS's automated, implant-level architecture made that structurally impossible. The nation had reintroduced every variable the charter passage read at Tereza's swearing-in was written to eliminate, down to how a citizen presents to the person evaluating them. That year's national report carried a case from the severe district in its annex.

A woman there had discovered her partner's infidelity and struck him once. The injury was minor, and her record showed no pattern. Her panel had reassigned her to -1. Tereza pulled Coyle's file from the archive and laid the two implant summaries side by side.

Key lesson: Human judges occasionally handle a single case better than automated evaluation. Their answers also shift with the panel, the district and how well the citizen speaks, so the same act can cost two people different amounts, which VMSS treats as unequal by definition.

## 3. The Living Laboratory

**Whose story:** Agathe Lind, a founder of the Open Ring. She assembles the alliance's datasets on her study wall, her own nation's cost is one of the rows, and Dagny brings her the VMSS review that cites her tables.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Living Laboratory
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Alliance Ecosystem & Structural Validation · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.3
Outcome: Every allied divergence from VMSS's model produces measurable costs, and together the variants' data validates the traditional architecture
```

In the alliance's sixtieth year Agathe Lind, one of the founders of the Open Ring, covered the long wall of her study with a grid. The treaty framework now encompassed over twenty sovereign civilizations operating gradient governance models, and the diversity had become extraordinary. She gave each major design choice in VMSS's architecture a row and each nation a mark. Every row showed the choice modified, removed or replaced by at least one allied or adjacent nation:

*Walls: kept / removed. Reassignment: permanent / recovery pathway. Leadership: merit-based governance / elected officials. Kill switch: kept / removed. Currency: siloed / single. Children: parental child-rearing / universal autoparenting. Judgment: AI governance / human judges. UBI: halving cascade / equal.*

No central authority had designed this diversity. It had emerged through twenty sovereign acts of founding, each led by a population with genuine philosophical commitments to its specific divergence. The Open Ring's founders, Agathe among them, genuinely believed walls were redundant. The Common Coin's founders genuinely believed currency siloing was punitive, and the Blank Slate's genuinely believed institutional childhood would outperform parental childhood. None of them had been cynical, and none had been foolish. They were civilizational designers who disagreed with specific VMSS design choices, adopted what they agreed with and modified what they didn't, and between them they had built an experiment nobody had set up with controls: a living laboratory of gradient governance variants at civilizational scale, running on real populations and producing real consequences and real data.

The bottom half of the wall was the data. After decades of operation, the comparative data across the alliance showed a pattern that no single nation's experience could establish. Every divergence from VMSS's traditional architecture produced measurable costs in the specific dimension the divergence modified, while the unmodified dimensions continued to function as designed. The costs were not catastrophic. Every allied nation was a functional civilization that outperformed Earth on every macro metric: lifespan, safety, material abundance, institutional trust. The costs were consistent, directional and compounding. Under each nation's name she wrote what its divergence had cost:

*The Softer Ring removed the kill switch. A defense gap: eleven deaths over fourteen weeks when conventional security could not match the capability remote neutralization provides.*
*The Elected Ring introduced democratic governance. Discretionary padding between event and consequence: a 14% higher breach rate over twenty years.*
*The Recovery Gradient allowed upward reassignment. Deterrent erosion, victim re-traumatization, and STI gaming for recovery eligibility.*
*The Drift Vessel achieved 100% revival. Progressive identity erosion in lower layers, where multiple revivals compound drift into de facto personality replacement.*
*The Glass Mind made cognition transparent. Civilizational cognitive suppression, creativity collapse, and an inverted layer gradient in which the upper layers became the most mentally constrained.*
*The Open Ring removed walls. Consequence became administrative rather than environmental, reducing deterrent weight.*
*The Common Coin unified currency. Cross-layer economic extraction indistinguishable from colonial economics.*
*The Blank Slate universalized autoparenting. The family as an institution, reproductive decline, and a generation that cannot love irrationally.*
*The Equal Floor equalized UBI. The economic dimension of consequence, producing lower layers materially indistinguishable from Main; compounded by the Spending Ceiling effect, where equal income poured into degraded institutional infrastructure produced inflationary spirals, artisan monopolies, and purchasing power that converged back toward the original baseline despite quadrupled nominal income.*
*The Human Bench replaced AI governance with judges. Inconsistent outcomes correlated with presentation quality, geography and panel composition.*
*The Compressed Ring dissolved the middle punitive layer into a four-ring model. Upward contamination of -1 with moderate-violence offenders, a downward cliff-drop of severe-violence offenders into terminal -3 with severed revival, and the loss of the architectural vocabulary for conduct between impulse and fatality.*
*The Layer in Name Only added a sixth ring above Sanctuary. Twenty years of maintaining a wall between identical rooms, kneecapped SAD populations, and a ring that could not be decommissioned because admitting it was empty was more expensive than keeping it staffed.*

Twenty nations had set out to improve VMSS's design choices. Each had independently identified features it believed were too harsh, too rigid, too automated, too secretive, too permanent or too punitive, and built a civilization that addressed those specific concerns. Every modification was principled, and every one was defensible. Every one produced the outcome its founders wanted and an additional outcome they did not want, with measurable downstream effects the traditional model did not exhibit. In the autumn Agathe added a right-hand column for the additional outcomes and headed it *A reduction in the clarity, consistency or weight of consequence*:

*Kill switch removal: reduced military deterrent. Recovery pathway: reduced reassignment permanence. Elected officials: reduced enforcement immediacy. Wall removal: reduced environmental separation. UBI equalization: reduced economic weight. Human judges: reduced categorical consistency.*

VMSS had not predicted these outcomes, and it had not designed the alliance to serve as a comparative experiment. But the alliance's diversity had produced something no internal simulation or pressure test could: civilizational-scale evidence that the traditional architecture's harshest features (permanence, walls, automated judgment, the kill switch, the UBI cascade, currency siloing, cognitive privacy, child autonomy) were load-bearing elements of an integrated system, each reinforcing the others, rather than independently harsh design choices that could be softened one at a time. Remove one and the system continued to function. The load redistributed. The remaining features bore more weight than they were designed for, and the system drifted, slowly, consistently and in the same direction every time: toward softer consequence, reduced deterrent, and measurable divergence from the model that modified nothing. Agathe had watched it happen at home.

Dagny Sorel, who had helped found the Blank Slate, came to see the wall that autumn week and read down the new column with her finger.

"Every one of them went softer," she said.

"Where each of us meant it to," Agathe said.

By spring the alliance's diversity was VMSS's greatest strategic asset and its most effective argument. It widened the emigration funnel, gave hesitant populations an on-ramp and collapsed Earth's counter-narrative, and at the same time it validated the specific model it had been created to improve upon. Twenty nations had tried to build a better version of the architecture, and between them they had shown that the original was better, by producing the specific costs its traditional features were designed to prevent. It was the most comprehensive validation of VMSS's design philosophy ever produced, and the nations that had tried to prove VMSS wrong had written it.

That spring Dagny brought over a VMSS review of the alliance, with Agathe's own tables in its citations. The traditional model's harshest features were validated by the measured consequences in allied nations that had tried operating without them. Every allied nation that had softened a specific feature had produced a published, verifiable, decades-long dataset showing what that softening cost, and the review used them. The review made no case of its own. For permanence it cited the Recovery Gradient; for cognitive privacy, the Glass Mind; for walls, the Open Ring.

"They cite your wall," Dagny said. Agathe pinned the review to the wall under the Open Ring's row.

Key lesson: VMSS's harshest features support one another, so a nation that softens one shifts the weight onto the rest and drifts toward weaker consequence over decades. The clearest evidence for this came from the allies who tried it.

Footnote — The Uncomfortable Corollary: If the alliance's diversity validates the traditional model through variant failure, then the variants are more valuable to VMSS as imperfect implementations than they would be as perfect copies. A world of twenty identical VMSS nations produces no comparative data. A world of twenty divergent nations produces the only evidence that could prove the traditional architecture correct, generated by civilizations that tried to improve it and measured the cost. The uncomfortable implication is that VMSS benefits from allied nations making choices it considers wrong, because without those choices the argument for the traditional model remains theoretical.

## 4. The Deathless Gold Rush

Brenna Kast worked the safe shafts in a -3 mining district, and she was on shift the week Kofi Asante arrived. He was a Main Layer industrial engineer on a six-month visitation contract, with skills the terminal economy needed: fabrication maintenance, power cell diagnostics, structural assessment for mining operations. He fixed the cage lift on Brenna's shaft in an afternoon.

Three months in, a tunnel collapse killed two native miners, and Kofi was buried with them. Brenna was on the crew that dug the three of them out. The two native miners were gone permanently, and she carried one of the coffins up to the ridge. Kofi revived in a Main Layer medical bay four hours later, filed a re-entry application and was back in -3 within the week: same district, same reputation, same employer, same knowledge of the tunnel system. He found her in the canteen at the end of a shift.

"I heard about the funerals," he said. "I'm sorry."

"You were under there with them."

"Four hours," Kofi said. "Then I woke up in Main."

Kofi, who meant nobody harm, was a competent engineer who had noticed that his backup vessel made him the most valuable miner in the district, and his employer had noticed too. Within a year Kofi was running the most dangerous shaft in the operation, the one that produced the highest-grade ore and killed a worker every few months. He earned four times what native miners earned on the safe shafts, Brenna among them, because he took the shifts nobody else survived. He died twice more and came back twice more. His employer's output doubled.

Word traveled up to Main. One evening Kofi showed her the figure on a Main Layer feed: within eighteen months, over twelve thousand Main Layer citizens had filed -3 visitation applications. The gold rush had begun.

The visitors fanned out across -3's economy. Mining, hazardous construction, deep-water salvage, chemical processing: every industry where mortality risk was a material cost discovered that visitors eliminated that cost entirely, and the economic distortion was immediate and structural. Dangerous-labor wages collapsed. Native miners who had once commanded premium pay for mortal risk found themselves competing against visitors who would do the same work for less, because the visitors carried no risk premium. Some operations fired their native crews entirely and replaced them with rotating visitor teams, and Brenna's was one of them. She tried a demolition crew, a salvage boat and a chemical works on the coast, and every hiring table quoted her the visitors' rate. Private security firms were recruiting visitors as enforcers who could not be permanently killed. On -3's wild side, whose entire economic logic depended on the finality of death, Colosseum-classified enterprises found that visitors would enter at any odds, because the odds did not apply to them; a visitor who died in the Saurian Park revived in Main Layer with a story and a re-entry application.

Her brother Cato fought on the district circuit, where the Colosseum gate money rested on the genuine scarcity of people willing to risk permanent death, and the Colosseum economy had flooded with visitors for whom the risk was theatrical. Before he fought a visitor, Brenna sat with him in the back room while he wrapped his hands.

"He told me at the weigh-in he's booked a table in Main on Friday," Cato said. "Win or lose."

Brenna paid at the gate that night, and the price had dropped again. The fight circuits had become exhibitions rather than existential contests, and Cato fought for his life against a man who faced, at worst, a four-hour interruption. She watched from the rail.

By then natives like Brenna could pick out visitors on sight. The confidence was different, and the risk calculus was visible in how they moved. Protests emerged in the mining districts. Someone painted DEATHLESS GO HOME along the ore sheds while Brenna held the ladder. Reputation-ledger associations began flagging visitor status, and some districts attempted to restrict visitor entry through cooperative access rules. Private security firms loyal to native employers threatened visitors physically, but every instrument -3's organic justice system had was calibrated for a population where death was final.

Sella, who ran nights for one of those firms, told Brenna about the visitor they had locked in the storeroom under the weigh station.

"Dead by morning," Sella said. "Did it himself. He's eating breakfast in Main." She drank. "Kill one and he's back inside a week."

Torture ended the same way: the visitor waited for the pain to kill him, and revived.

Brenna rated Kofi down on the public ledger, and she was not the only one. The hits accumulated on the visitors' records as -3 public rating, weighted against the ambient standard of the terminal layer, and cost each of them a few points of STI. On a Main Layer citizen's ledger that was visible and structurally toothless, because STI does not decide reassignment. Some visitors wore their low -3 ratings as a badge, proof they had operated in the terminal layer and survived the backlash.

Eighteen months after the gold rush began, -3 citizens expeditiously invoked Article XXVIII. The petition surfaced within weeks, and 1% of the district populations in the most affected mining and Colosseum regions signed within days, Brenna on the first morning. The expert panel drafted a layer-wide regulation mandating backup vessel disclosure, Colosseum exclusion for visitors with active continuity, and dangerous-labor restrictions. The population ratified it at 93%, the highest ratification margin in -3's regulatory history, and it was enacted within a month of the petition surfacing.

The regulation was advisory. That was the structural limitation of Article XXVIII in -3: its enacted regulations were advisory only, consistent with institutional withdrawal. The native cooperatives and reputation networks enforced it against their own population. Local operators who hired undisclosed visitors lost reputation standing, and Colosseum operators who admitted visitors lost gate credibility. The visitors themselves were impervious. Their institutional relationship was with Main Layer rather than with -3's organic enforcement apparatus, and the advisory regulation that bound the local population had no teeth against them. The gold rush went on in two tiers: compliant natives, and non-compliant visitors operating above the local rules. Kofi was back on the deep shaft the morning after the vote.

The small-town cooperatives that had carried the protest, Brenna's among them, discovered they could not solve this alone. They escalated to -3's largest private corporations, the territorial enterprises that controlled multi-district operations, logistics networks and the layer's most significant private security apparatus. The corporations recognized the threat: the visitor gold rush was destabilizing the labor market their own operations depended on, native workers were leaving hazardous industries because they could not compete with immortal visitors, and the corporations' own workforce was eroding. Brenna went with the delegation to one of them and sat across from its labor director.

"We can't lobby the Meritboard in the traditional sense," the director said. "It isn't a legislature."

"Then what can you do?"

"Send it numbers."

The corporations activated their cross-layer connections: business relationships with Main Layer suppliers, -1 logistics firms and, through the trade networks that currency siloing does not prevent, indirect contact with Meritboard-adjacent institutions. The director hired Brenna, who knew every crew in the district, to help compile the data that reached the Meritboard's economics division through institutional channels: labor displacement reports, wage compression data, Colosseum revenue collapse, native mortality rates in industries now dominated by immortal competitors.

The data told a story the Meritboard's structural-fit analysis could not ignore, a cross-layer externality that the advisory mechanism could not resolve, and a federal law draft entered the Article XXV.VI ladder. The ladder was deliberately conservative, with the same conservatism that protected the founding core, and it took eleven years from first draft to ratification. The information campaign was brutal. Main Layer citizens who had benefited from the gold rush funded opposition messaging, and libertarian advocacy within -3 argued that federal intervention violated the layer's founding autonomy. Two earlier drafts failed, one at the Meritboard floor with 58%, below threshold, and one at the Supreme Court with 5/10, below the 6/10 requirement. The third draft survived because the economic data had compounded over a decade. By then native dangerous-labor employment in -3 had dropped 40%, Colosseum revenue had collapsed by 60%, and three major -3 corporations, Brenna's employer among them, had filed formal cross-layer grievances through the Meritboard's economics division.

The Meritboard's own internal debate on the third draft ran for three years before the filibuster vote. It cleared the floor at 64%, above the 60% threshold, with significant dissent from members who argued that the layer system's institutional withdrawal should extend to this problem. The Supreme Court's constitutional review cleared it at 7/10: the majority found the exploit a cross-layer externality within federal jurisdiction, and the minority argued it was an internal -3 regulatory matter. The three-track population ratification was the hardest gate. Brenna voted in the lower-layer aggregate from the market steps. +1 Sanctuary cleared at 91%, Main Layer at 74%, and the lower-layer aggregate at 82%, driven by -3's overwhelming support and by -1 and -2 recognizing that the same exploit applied to them at a milder gradient. The presidential veto was not exercised.

Federal law enacted. Brenna's employer posted the summary on its depot board:

*Continuity parity: binding across all five layers. Backup vessel disclosure: mandatory. Colosseum exclusion for visitors with active continuity: federal, not advisory. Dangerous-labor restrictions: federal, not advisory. Violation: exploitation of a structural asymmetry, processed through the standard federal chain.*

The chain was the same enforcement infrastructure that handled clean energy mandates and implant hacking prohibitions. A Main Layer visitor who concealed backup vessel status in a -3 employment contract, entered a Colosseum with active continuity, or took mortality-dependent work without disclosure faced federal consequence, from the same consequence architecture that enforced every other Article XXV mandate, the nuclear prohibition among them.

The gold rush ended, though not overnight. Ratification opened a six-month compliance window: existing contracts were honored, and visitors in active dangerous-labor or Colosseum positions exited or restructured.
