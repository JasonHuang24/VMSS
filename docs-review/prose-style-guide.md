# Prose Lift Style Guide

This guide comes from the pilot (`docs-review/prose-lift-pilot.md`) and two outside critic reports (`prose-lift-critic-grok.md`, `prose-lift-critic-chatgpt.md`). Follow it as written.

## Frozen
- **Metadata.** Title, Type, Classification and Snapshot never change. An AI-written Outcome line becomes a short, plain statement of what happened, with the same facts. Flag each change as old → new.
- **Facts.** Keep every number, name, rate, threshold, layer fact, mechanism and outcome. Cut a fact only when the story no longer needs it, and ledger the cut.
- **Era.** The Snapshot pins its era's numbers and rates: never recalculate them. For non-numeric rules, the **current Charter wins** (Jason, 2026-09-27): a story that conflicts with a current non-numeric Charter rule gets fixed to match it. Examples: Art. VII bans upward visits; STI never sets placement (Art. II); Sanctuary eligibility at STI 85 is immediate; downward conversion runs only through authorized channels (Art. III.V). If fixing a conflict would break the story's premise, flag it for Jason.
- **Standing rulings for stories:** a single DUI goes to −1 (Art. I side of T1). A −3 visitor keeps the backup-vessel link, because only terminal reassignment severs it. A −2 resident may file voluntary permanent residency into −3, with the psychological screening Art. VII requires.
- **Also frozen:** hedges ("tends to", "usually"); R13 (no founder in-world); the layer stance (lower layers are not hell; -3 is chosen).
- **Canon.** Work from the original. Open the charter or whitepaper only for a fact the original lacks.

## The one rule that matters most
**Once an action, a number or a line of dialogue has made the point, stop.** Cut any sentence or clause that explains, translates or restates what the reader just saw. Both critics ranked this the biggest remaining problem.
"'Who to?' he said." + "He had no district and no vouchers, and nobody anywhere kept a column of numbers on him." → keep "'Who to?' he said." and cut the rest.

**Don't over-compress.** If a cut leaves a sentence crowded, or leaves a pronoun without a clear antecedent, clarity wins. Cutting is meant to remove repetition, not the connections the reader needs ("one reconstructed from the victim's" → "reconstructed from the victim's").

## Banned tells
1. **Paragraphs that end on a thesis, aphorism or balanced contrast**, including spoken ones. End on an action, an object, or a plain line of dialogue.
   "…careful had only ever meant lucky." → "…five people stood between her and her vehicle."
2. **Reversals:** "not X, but Y", "X is not A. It is B.", "They didn't… They did…". State the positive claim.
   "…is not capital suppression. It is capital circulation." → "Ines Aro's crew worked in her atrium all season."
3. **Maxims in dialogue.** People say what they would say to this listener right now, and different characters sound different. A speech never states the story's rule in general terms. The one exception: when the story turns on a law, one short line may say it in the speaker's own words ("Kill a trader and we're murderers."). Never stack two quotable lines in one speech.
4. **Briefing register mid-story:** rosters, tiered catalogues ("closest… deeper… deepest"), step-by-step procedures, compliance checklists, career-and-acclaim profiles. Doctrine facts that must stay arrive as one clause of what the viewpoint character knows, or as an in-world artifact they hold (a log line, an almanac entry, a page of figures).
5. **Doctrine footnotes.** Cut them, or turn them into action or dialogue.
   "the floor holds here too, formally: his UBI accrues in an account…" → "'Distribution point.' He lifted the chain an inch off the concrete and let it drop."
6. **Blanket assertions:** "nobody anywhere", "everyone on every side", "everyone else downstream", "no precedent". Name the particular people, or cut the line.
7. **Stock phrases and cinematic beats:** "in equal measure", "for a moment there is only…", "the way crews do", "moving before he had decided to", "the road doesn't ask", unnamed superlatives ("the most complex… they have encountered"). Also rhetorical tricolons, and more than one em-dash per paragraph.

## Every piece is a story
- **Whose eyes.** Tell the story through one person caught inside the mechanism, placed where every figure reaches them and the result lands on them. They are in frame from the first paragraph, and world setup attaches to what they see or do.
- **Framing is not a fix.** Attributing a briefing to a character ("Joss knows…", "Calla sorted them out:") doesn't make it their experience. Rules and rosters still have to arrive as things the character sees, hears or handles: "the two who played cards with him", not "three who had come down on ledgers".
- **Numbers.** Figures reach the reader when they reach the character: on a statement, in a call, on a screen at night. Keep them all, in causal order.
- **Explanation.** Allow at most one clause of narration or one line of dialogue per mechanism step, given at the moment the character needs it.
  "The SCM does not use a snapshot. It uses a 90-day rolling average…" → "Petra was looking at the line below it, the one the trigger actually read: the 90-day rolling average, $96.8 billion."
- **Scenes.** At least two scenes with dialogue, one of them the turning point. Keep one tense for the main action. Invented minor characters get no new powers or institutions.
- **Leave things unresolved.** Let a mistake stand uncorrected, and let a feeling go unnamed.
- **Paragraphs the critics picked as most human (the target):** "They fed her, mostly." · "Distribution point." · "So it's a field recording." · "Her first piece took eleven months." · "Nobody corrects him." · "I thought they wanted my pack." · "For two weeks she spent…" · "The next quarter, three members left…"

## Key Lesson and length
**Key Lesson:** 1–2 short sentences stating the general rule the story illustrates, in terms the body doesn't use. Each one should be sayable aloud in one breath. No metaphor, no balanced antithesis, no "exactly as… as" formula. Never re-narrate the story or repeat a line from the body.
**Length:** never longer than the original; aim for 10–25% shorter. A case study converted to a story may run to 1.5× the original; shorter is better if it holds. Count the body plus the Key Lesson, not the metadata.

## Self-check before the ledger
Read the last sentence of every paragraph. If it explains, summarizes or generalizes, cut it and see whether the paragraph still works. It usually does.

## Fidelity ledger (every session)
- Write one row per doctrinal or factual claim in the original.
- Each row holds either a verbatim rewrite quote (double quotes, 10 words or fewer) or `cut: <reason>`. The reason is one of: aphorism, reversal, restatement, stock phrase, or story no longer needs it.
- Put original wording in single quotes, so a script can check every double-quoted fragment.
- Flag reading decisions, invented texture, Outcome changes and Charter conflicts. Resolve none of them silently.

## No invented mechanism
- **Texture is encouraged:** a gesture, weather, an object, a line of dialogue, a minor character's name.
- **Mechanism is forbidden:** no institution, rule, rate, number, technology or procedure that is absent from both the original and canon. If unsure, leave it out.
- Write in your own voice, and imitate no named author.
