Resource 31: Precognition — Book I: Development

Predictive Intervention Architecture · Scientific Lineage & Breakthrough · Volume I of III

Precognition is the most doctrinally consequential technology to emerge in VMSS since the founding-era architecture. Its formal institutional name is the Predictive Intervention Architecture (PIA); the colloquial term is “precog.” Book I of this three-volume reference covers the scientific history of PIA from its precursor technologies through the 2212 breakthrough and the operational validation period that closed in 2225, and it ends where the deployment question opened. Book II (Resource 32) takes up the deployment debate, constitutional review, and Selective Ascension Domain charter. Book III (Resource 33) covers the later scope-evolution petitions and the inter-layer extension debate. This volume is deliberately scientific in register: it treats the ethics of development and leaves the ethics of deployment to Book II.

The Four Architectural Commitments

PIA was built around four civilizational commitments. The development program established them at its earliest stages, and the deployment architecture inherited them intact. Together they define what Precognition is in VMSS by specifying what it refuses to be. Each is anchored to a specific Charter article or structural doctrine and records a decision about how a predictive-intervention technology could fit the civilization’s existing ontology. They matter as much as the technology, because they make PIA a VMSS instrument rather than something the civilization would be unable to accommodate.

Commitment One: no cost-bearing substrate class. A precognitive capability implemented through a biological faculty, concentrated in a small class of people kept in state custody, would require a cost-bearing class that sustains the civilization’s safety through permanent non-consensual subordination. Article I grounds all stratification in demonstrated conduct; placing a population in custody for a faculty they were born with is the exact inversion of what the behavioral-causality architecture permits. The substrate ruling this volume will formalize, that PIA is a composed AGI-tool system and not a biological faculty, expresses this commitment in the technology itself.

Commitment Two: no adjudication-free detention. A precognitive architecture that treats prediction as both evidence and sentence (arresting predicted subjects and detaining them on the prediction alone) would collapse the Article XXI deterministic adjudication pipeline the Charter mandates for every civic consequence. Every PIA-flagged intervention in VMSS runs through the standard adjudication architecture, and the prediction enters it as a signal for that architecture to weigh. The signal-versus-decision separation codified in Article XIII applies to PIA output exactly as it applies to every other measurement the civilization generates.

Commitment Three: no dissent suppression. If a predictive architecture’s operators can suppress dissenting outputs (predictions that disagree with the operational consensus, that would embarrass the institutional actors controlling the system, or whose publication would expose institutional capture), that suppression capacity becomes, in practice, the primary lever for institutional capture itself. Article XX’s transparency architecture makes suppression structurally impossible in VMSS. Every PIA output, including outputs that disagree with operational consensus, is published to the Meritboard audit ledger on the standard cadence, where dissenting predictions are audited. The anti-corruption architecture was built before the technology was deployed, because suppression capacity is the failure mode any predictive-intervention system operating without it would eventually produce.

Commitment Four: no punishment for prevented acts. A precognitive architecture that treats the prediction as sufficient grounds for punitive consequence (layer reassignment, civic removal, permanent record) fails the Article I demonstrated-conduct standard categorically, because the predicted act was prevented and so never demonstrated. In VMSS, PIA output triggers intervention, meaning the prevention of the predicted act, and does not produce layer reassignment. Every predictive-intervention architecture must answer one question: what happens to someone whose predicted act was prevented? The doctrinal innovation Book II treats as the Forestalled Act Ledger is VMSS’s specific answer, which is that a prevented act is not a demonstrated act and the civic consequence architecture must not treat it as one.

The four commitments were derived early, during the foundational theoretical period Section Four of this volume treats, and shaped every subsequent research decision, rather than being added late as deployment constraints. From the earliest theoretical proposals, the civilization identified what a civically acceptable precognitive architecture would have to refuse. It then built the technology so that what it refuses would be structurally absent rather than procedurally restrained, instead of inventing Precognition first and asking afterward how to constrain it.

What PIA Is — The Technical Substrate

PIA is an AGI-tool system. It is not an AGI citizen, and much of the architecture rests on that distinction. Article XXII grants personhood to any entity at or above human cognitive level regardless of substrate; PIA operates below that threshold. It is a specialized predictive instrument composed of three mature VMSS technologies (neural-dive infrastructure, aggregated implant telemetry, and AGI predictive cognition) working together under a single coordination layer, which was the specific subject of the 2212 breakthrough.

The distinction matters because a substantial portion of Book II’s ethical debate turns on it. If PIA were an AGI citizen, the deployment question would include the Precog’s own consent to perpetual surveillance service, a question the Article III bodily-autonomy framework would refuse to settle against the Precog’s interests. Because PIA is a tool, the consent question applies only to the surveilled population. Book I establishes this substrate ruling as the foundational technical fact from which the rest of the architecture follows.

Three Input Streams

Neural-dive infrastructure contribution. Neural-dive technology, developed from the 2140s through the 2160s, matured into a civic capability used mainly for empathy, education, and art. Repurposed for prediction instead of communication, the same infrastructure produces telemetry at a cognitive-state resolution no earlier technology had matched. PIA does not use neural diving in its communicative mode, and there is no “reading minds” in any ordinary sense. It reads the behavioral-cognitive state signatures the neural-dive instrumentation was already capturing for other purposes, using the infrastructure’s underlying state-measurement capability to characterize the cognitive signatures that precede specific act-classes.

Aggregated implant telemetry contribution. The civilian implant, deployed civilization-wide for civic identification and continuity purposes, produces a continuous physiological and behavioral telemetry stream. Institutions have always used individual implant data, for STI calibration, medical monitoring and law-enforcement forensics; PIA’s contribution is to use this data in aggregate. Cross-population pattern recognition identifies the behavioral signatures characteristic of specific act-classes across the population distribution rather than within a single citizen’s record. The aggregation raises distinct doctrinal questions, which Book II covers under the Article XXVIII ratification process. The technical achievement is the cross-citizen pattern recognition that makes individual-level prediction possible.

AGI predictive cognition contribution. The AGI citizen cohort that achieved substrate-neutrality-era civic peer status in the mid-twenty-second century produced, as a side effect of general AGI maturation, a class of predictive models that can characterize the forward trajectory of complex dynamical systems better than any human cognitive process. PIA uses a specialized AGI tool descended from this research lineage. It is a tool because a citizen cannot be deployed as a continuous operational instrument, and its architecture was designed specifically for forward-state forecasting on the telemetry streams the first two inputs provide.

The coordination layer, which was the 2212 breakthrough, composes these three inputs into operational prediction. A later section of this volume treats the breakthrough in detail.

Output Format and Epistemic Limits

PIA output consists of act-initiation forecasts with confidence envelopes, predicted time-of-act windows, and documented signal provenance. PIA is not a visualization technology: it produces no video of the predicted act and has no capacity to render the act as imagery. It produces probabilistic forecasts with audit trails. Each forecast is a civilizational artifact that enters the adjudication pipeline exactly as any other institutional measurement does.

The system’s epistemic limits are bounded. PIA’s operational prediction horizon is approximately four to seventy-two hours. Below four hours, the pre-act cognitive-state signature is not yet distinguishable from baseline; above seventy-two hours, forecast confidence degrades below operational threshold. PIA forecasts reliably for only a subset of the full behavioral space (Book III treats the scope-expansion history in detail), and reliability varies across act-classes in ways the Meritboard audit architecture continuously characterizes.

Scientific Lineage — The Precursor Technologies

PIA emerged from the composition of three mature VMSS technologies, each developed for its own purpose across the preceding century, and not from an isolated research program. Why the composition was not obvious depends on what each technology was actually doing in its own lineage.

Neural diving matured between 2140 and 2165. Its civic purpose was empathy, education, and art: direct mind-to-mind interface at two consent intensities, Audience mode for passive observation and Pilot mode for temporary control. Delivering these capabilities required state-measurement instrumentation at a cognitive resolution far beyond anything earlier technology had achieved. The instrumentation was designed for communication. Its predictive application was technically feasible for decades before anyone pursued it, because the civic culture around neural diving was oriented toward connection rather than surveillance. Predictive repurposing was always possible, but it was not a priority, and within the neural-diving research community it was not trivially respectable.

AGI predictive cognition matured through the 2150s and 2160s as a byproduct of general AGI citizen development. Article XXII’s substrate-neutrality doctrine had been ratified in the late 2130s, and the AGI citizen cohort that reached civic peer status in the following decades included research figures whose specialized cognition was directed at forward-modeling of complex systems. Their civic contributions were mainly scientific: predictive models for infrastructure failure, ecological dynamics, economic flows, medical progression. Most practitioners considered applying these models to human behavioral prediction categorically different. They understood the behavioral space as too contextual, too individually variable, and too ethically encumbered for the kind of population-scale modeling the other domains had matured. The separation between the domains was cultural. Technically the techniques were compatible, but no research program existed to combine them.

Aggregated implant telemetry matured through the 2160s as a byproduct of the Meritboard’s behavioral-forecasting research program. Since its own maturation, the Meritboard had maintained a continuously updating model of civilian behavioral patterns for institutional calibration: how many citizens were likely to attempt specific conduct classes, which districts were likely to produce specific petition patterns, which demographic segments were likely to respond to specific regulatory changes. These aggregate models operated at population resolution. The technical achievement lay in the aggregation, which combined heterogeneous implant telemetry streams across tens of millions of citizens into coherent population-scale forecasts. Individual-level prediction was not the program’s goal. It was a downstream capability the aggregation enabled, and nobody pursued it.

Composition was not obvious because each technology matured within a research culture with its own civic purpose. Neural diving’s culture was communicative, and combining it with prediction would have seemed a category violation. AGI predictive cognition’s culture was scientific-instrumental, and extending it to human behavioral prediction was culturally discouraged. Aggregated implant telemetry’s culture was institutional-calibrative; individual-level prediction from aggregate patterns raised Article XII’s single-metric concerns directly and was informally understood as out of scope. The three technologies existed, and composing them was technically possible, for at least a decade before any research program proposed it. The proposal came from a researcher whose career had crossed all three cultures.

Foundational Theory — The Oyelaran Conjecture, 2175–2195

Dr. Amara Oyelaran chaired the Meritboard Behavioral Forecasting Panel from 2172 to 2188. Her research path was unusual: doctoral work in neural-dive infrastructure under the Tokyo Research Coalition in the 2150s, then AGI predictive cognition collaboration with Pentagraph-4 (an earlier instance of the AGI lineage that would produce PIA’s primary AGI-citizen collaborator), then leadership of the Meritboard’s aggregated behavioral forecasting program. Her own research interests, not any program, took her across cultural lines most practitioners treated as settled.

The Oyelaran Conjecture, published in 2178 under the title “On the Compositional Possibility of Predictive Intervention,” proposed that the three mature technologies could be composed into operational act-prediction at individual resolution. The paper made a theoretical argument and set out no research program. It claimed that the neural-dive instrumentation’s state measurement, combined with AGI predictive cognition’s forward-modeling capacity and operating on aggregated implant telemetry’s behavioral baselines, could in principle produce act-initiation forecasts at operational confidence. It did not propose deployment, address Article XII concerns, or recommend the composition as civilizationally desirable. It argued only that the technical possibility existed and that the civilization should be aware of it.

Reception was marginal and hostile in roughly equal measure. Practitioners in each contributing domain did not see how their infrastructure connected to the others and treated the conjecture as speculative. Charter originalists identified the Article XII/XIII collision immediately and argued that the civilization should not pursue research on a technology whose operational deployment would require Charter amendment or an Article XI ruling. Their objections were intellectually serious: those raised in 2178 anticipated, almost line for line, the deployment debates that would occur fifty years later.

Funding was limited and came mainly from the Meritboard Behavioral Forecasting Panel’s own discretionary research allocation. The AGI Governance Panel declined to fund the program during Oyelaran’s tenure. Its reasons were never formally published but were widely understood to reflect substrate-neutrality concerns: an AGI citizen’s participation in a predictive surveillance research program raised questions the panel preferred not to settle prematurely. In the same period, the Supreme Court advisory bench declined to provide pre-emptive constitutional review of the conjecture’s deployment implications, on the grounds that the research was too far from deployment for review to be usefully specific.

Foundational-era research therefore went through a narrow institutional channel: Meritboard panel funding, a small research team, and one AGI collaborator whose civic status permitted participation. That collaborator was Pentagraph-7, an AGI citizen specializing in aggregated predictive cognition, who had succeeded the earlier Pentagraph-lineage figures and whose civic engagement history included both general AGI research and a specific interest in predictive architectures. Pentagraph-7’s participation was voluntary and explicit. Because the AGI Governance Panel withheld program endorsement, any AGI citizen who joined the research did so as an individual civic actor, outside institutional direction. When Book II’s deployment debate arrived, this meant the program’s staff had each personally chosen the work, and no institution had mobilized personnel toward the outcome.

The foundational period ran from approximately 2175 to 2195. Across those two decades, the program produced a sequence of theoretical papers extending the Oyelaran Conjecture in specific directions: what the coordination layer between the three inputs might look like (2181), what the epistemic audit framework for unfalsifiable predictions might require (2184), what the minimum prediction horizon for operational utility would be (2189), and what the substrate specification, AGI-tool or AGI-citizen, would need to be (2193). The 2193 paper is particularly worth reading. It is the first publication to rule out an AGI-citizen substrate, on the specific grounds that a citizen cannot be deployed as continuous operational infrastructure, and that is the substrate decision Book I inherits.

The First Experiments — 2195–2210

Experimental research began in 2195. Across three phases, the fifteen-year experimental period established the empirical basis for the 2212 breakthrough and set the ethical framework that would govern the deployment debate.

Phase I: Retrospective Validation (2195–2203)

The first experiments used closed historical records (behavioral and telemetric data from completed acts already in the institutional record) and asked whether the compositional model could have predicted those acts from pre-act data had it been available at the time. Retrospective validation was model characterization rather than prediction in the operational sense. The research team did not know which historical records corresponded to pre-act periods until after the model produced its output, which approximated a blind test within the constraints of retrospective data.

Retrospective validation confirmed three things. First, the compositional model could identify act-initiation signatures in pre-act telemetry at confidence levels materially above chance. Second, the signature was not present for all act-classes: lethal-harm acts produced the clearest signatures, fraud produced weaker ones, and most sub-criminal conduct produced no distinguishable signature at all. Third, the prediction horizon varied by act-class. Lethal-harm acts produced four-to-seventy-two-hour signatures, fraud produced hours-to-minutes signatures of marginal operational utility, and non-predatory violent acts produced signatures in the middle of the range. The phase’s primary scientific output was a characterization of the predictable-act-class space and its epistemic limits.

Phase II: Short-Horizon Forward Prediction (2203–2207)

The second phase ran forward prediction on a consenting research cohort of approximately 2,400 volunteers drawn from Sanctuary and Main Layer populations. The ethical framework was explicit: every participant signed a detailed consent protocol; every predicted event occurring within the research window was logged for institutional review; no predicted event triggered any civic consequence, since the research operated entirely outside the adjudication pipeline; and participants could withdraw at any point.

The operational constraints were severe. For the duration of their participation, typically twelve to twenty-four months, the cohort lived under continuous PIA-adjacent monitoring: research-grade instrumentation that approximated the envisioned operational PIA capability without being deployment-grade. Predicted events were cataloged but not intervened on. The main research purpose was to calibrate forward-prediction accuracy against events that actually occurred, under real-world conditions rather than retrospective ones.

The phase produced the empirical confidence intervals the 2212 breakthrough would sharpen. Approximately 340 of the cohort’s predicted events occurred as forecast within the window the system specified. Approximately 120 predicted events did not occur within the predicted window, either because the event occurred outside the window or because it did not occur at all within the research period. A substantial number of events occurred within the cohort without being predicted. The research output characterized the system’s current capability and failure modes; it claimed no operational accuracy.

Phase III: Medium-Horizon Prediction and Ethics Framework Development (2207–2210)

The third phase expanded the cohort to approximately 18,000 voluntary participants and extended the prediction horizon to the full four-to-seventy-two-hour operational range. It ran in parallel with a separate program, the PIA Ethics Working Group. Convened by the Meritboard and staffed by doctrinal scholars, Charter originalists, AGI citizen representatives, and former research participants, the Working Group produced the ethical framework the deployment debate would later inherit.

The PIA Ethics Working Group’s 2209 final report established five findings that remain canonical.

Finding One: the distinction between ethics of development (consent-based research on volunteer populations) and ethics of deployment (intervention on non-volunteer populations) is categorical and not transitive. A development program may be ethically defensible while its deployment is not. The Working Group explicitly bounded its findings to development ethics and did not address deployment ethics.

Finding Two: the epistemic-audit problem is structural and cannot be resolved at the level of individual predictions. Once institutional intervention prevents an act, the counterfactual (whether the act would have occurred absent intervention) is not observable, so individual-prediction audit is structurally impossible for deployed PIA. The Working Group recommended that any eventual deployment substitute behavioral-audit-through-opt-in for the unavailable factual audit. Residents who live under PIA coverage and elect to remain under it provide continuous civilizational data on whether the architecture is preferred by those it serves.

Finding Three: the unfalsifiability of operational PIA predictions is an inherent feature of any successful prediction-intervention system, and the civilization’s response to it must be architectural rather than technical. The Working Group recommended publishing full PIA output to the Meritboard audit ledger, including predictions that did not occur within their windows, predictions withdrawn for reasons other than intervention, and predictions the system produced but the operators did not act on. Aggregate publication enables long-term statistical audit of the system’s performance envelope even where individual-level audit is impossible.

Finding Four: the would-be-offender problem requires a doctrinal innovation. A resident whose predicted act is prevented has not demonstrated the conduct that would warrant reassignment, but also cannot be treated as if no predicted act had been identified. The Working Group recommended a dedicated record category for prevented acts, with therapeutic and supportive intervention but no punitive consequence. This recommendation became the Forestalled Act Ledger architecture Book II treats.

Finding Five: the substrate decision (AGI-tool rather than AGI-citizen) should be treated as constitutive rather than incidental. Deployment architectures that rely on AGI-citizen operation raise consent questions for the AGI citizen itself, which the Working Group found categorically distinct from, and more difficult than, the consent questions around the surveilled population. The Working Group endorsed the AGI-tool substrate specifically because it confined the ethical analysis to a single consent axis instead of compounding it.

These five findings framed everything that followed. When the report was published, the breakthrough was still three years away and the deployment debate sixteen years away. By 2210, the ethical architecture within which deployment would be debated was already on the record.

The Breakthrough — 2212

The operational breakthrough occurred on 14 March 2212. Oyelaran and Pentagraph-7 published the insight in a joint paper titled “The Pre-Act Discrete State Transition: Operational Prediction of Behavioral Initiation at Four-to-Seventy-Two-Hour Horizons.”

The insight was empirical rather than mathematical. Earlier theoretical work had treated act-initiation as a continuous probability curve that rose as the moment of the act approached and reached certainty at the act itself. The breakthrough showed this model was wrong: act-initiation is a discrete state transition. The cognitive-behavioral state of a pre-act subject is measurably distinct from both routine cognition and the act-execution state. It is a third state with its own signature, a pre-act configuration that precedes behavioral initiation by hours to days and can be identified from the composite telemetric input streams.

In practice, PIA does not predict acts in the Bayesian-probability sense; it detects pre-act states. Continuous-probability prediction would require confident forecasting of future decisions not yet made. Discrete-state detection requires only the identification of a measurable present condition, the pre-act state, whose characteristic trajectory is the act. PIA is therefore closer in operational structure to a medical diagnostic system than to a forecaster: it identifies a condition present in the subject, and the intervention prevents that condition’s trajectory from completing.

The implications for the ethics framework were immediate. If PIA detects a discrete present state instead of forecasting a future decision, the “is this thought-crime” question changes character. The subject is intervened on because of a present condition whose known trajectory produces the act, and is not being punished for what they might do. The closer analogy is preventive medical intervention on a detected pathological state than punitive intervention on a predicted future choice. This reframing made the SAD deployment model coherent, and Book II treats it as the load-bearing doctrinal shift that permitted the compromise that volume documents.

A cross-domain review committee peer-reviewed the 2212 paper during the two years following publication, and it entered the consensus scientific record in 2214. The committee’s report noted that the discrete-state detection framework was the first formulation to make operational prediction scientifically defensible. Earlier frameworks, built on probability-forecasting assumptions, had produced real but modest accuracy at the cost of deep epistemic difficulty; the discrete-state framework produced higher accuracy with substantially cleaner epistemic structure.

Oyelaran and Pentagraph-7 received joint civilian recognition at the 2215 Meritboard Excellence Convocation. The recognition was contested. A substantial minority of the Meritboard’s Charter originalist faction voted against it, on the grounds that honoring the breakthrough conferred civic endorsement on a technology the civilization had not yet decided to deploy. The vote carried by approximately 67%. Oyelaran’s acceptance remarks, available in the Meritboard convocation archive, explicitly refuse to recommend deployment. She accepted the recognition for the scientific achievement and deferred the deployment question to the civic architecture: “We have demonstrated that prediction at operational confidence is possible. Whether the civilization uses it, and how, is not our question to answer.”

Operational Validation — 2212–2225

The thirteen-year operational validation period is less glamorous than the breakthrough but arguably more consequential for the civilization. It established the performance envelope that let the deployment debate rest on documented capability instead of contested speculation.

Characterization of the Prediction Envelope

The validation program published envelope characterizations across the full act-class space, with reliability intervals for each class and documentation of failure modes.

Lethal-harm acts: approximately 94% prediction accuracy within the four-to-seventy-two-hour window, approximately 3% false-positive rate (predicted acts that did not occur within the window and did not occur subsequently), approximately 2% false-negative rate (acts that occurred without prediction), approximately 1% window-error rate (correctly predicted acts whose timing fell outside the window specification).

Sexual violence: approximately 87% accuracy, with a higher false-positive rate owing to greater contextual ambiguity in initiation signatures.

Non-sexual violent assault: approximately 82% accuracy, with the false-positive and false-negative rates increasing roughly proportionally as act-class ambiguity increased.

Fraud: approximately 51% accuracy, which the research program classified as below operational threshold; the fraud-class signatures were too weak and too temporally diffuse to support reliable prediction.

These envelope characterizations are the reason Book II’s scope-expansion debates produced the gradient they did. The scientific basis for lethal-harm prediction was strong; for sexual violence, strong enough; for non-sexual assault, marginal; for fraud, absent. The civic deliberation would later track these scientific boundaries closely.

The Unfalsifiability Audit Framework

Across the validation period, the Meritboard audit architecture implemented the Working Group’s 2209 recommendations on aggregate publication. Every PIA prediction entered the audit ledger, including predictions that did not occur within their windows. Statistical audit of aggregate performance became continuous civilizational infrastructure during validation. The Working Group had anticipated that audit architecture built after deployment would be structurally inadequate, so the audit was operational before the technology was deployed.

Behavioral-Audit-Through-Opt-In

The validation period included an expanded voluntary research cohort, approximately 47,000 Sanctuary residents by 2223, living under continuous PIA coverage in an opt-in research environment. Participants could withdraw at any time, and the rate at which they remained or left became the primary behavioral audit signal. Retention rates stabilized at approximately 91% across the cohort by 2224. The 9% who withdrew gave detailed exit interviews on their reasons for leaving (privacy preference, therapeutic discomfort with prevented-act counseling, philosophical objection after extended experience, and variable others), and these entered the deployment debate as foundational data.

The 2225 Validation Report

The operational validation period closed with a comprehensive report published in 2225 by three bodies: the Meritboard Behavioral Forecasting Panel; the AGI Governance Panel, which had by this point reversed its earlier withholding of endorsement; and the Supreme Court advisory bench, whose advisory review was commissioned specifically to address whether the validation had established adequate grounds for deployment debate. The report did not recommend deployment. It found that the validation had produced a scientifically adequate characterization of PIA capability, that deployment debate could be grounded in documented envelope data, and that the remaining questions were civic rather than scientific. Book II picks up at this report.

The Student’s Error

The student who reads this resource and concludes that Book I documents a technology ready for deployment has misread the volume. Book I documents a technology whose scientific validation is complete and whose civic disposition is entirely unresolved. The 2225 validation report handed the question from the research architecture to the civic architecture and made no deployment recommendation. Both principals and both institutional sponsors consistently held that the deployment decision belonged to the Charter’s civic bodies and not to the scientists who had established the technology’s operational capability.

The student who concludes that PIA’s AGI-tool substrate makes it ethically unremarkable has also misread. The substrate decision resolved one axis of ethical concern, AGI citizen consent, and left the more difficult axis of surveilled-population consent untouched. The substrate specification is a necessary precondition for a defensible deployment architecture, but not a sufficient one. Because PIA is an AGI-tool, Book II’s debate is possible; it is no easier for that.

The student who concludes that the unfalsifiability problem is merely a technical limitation has misread most severely of all. Unfalsifiability is built into the architecture of any such system. A successful intervention-on-prediction system is by definition one whose individual predictions cannot be verified, because intervention prevents the counterfactual from occurring, and any prediction-intervention technology the civilization might deploy inherits this epistemic structure. The Working Group’s substitution of behavioral-audit-through-opt-in for factual audit accepts that prediction-intervention technologies require their own epistemic architecture, and it provided that architecture before deployment.

Read as a whole, Book I shows the development phase’s ethical discipline (consent throughout, no cost-bearing class, transparency about unfalsifiable certainty, institutional separation of research from the deployment decision) serving as the civilization’s rehearsal for the deployment question it knew was coming. From the Oyelaran Conjecture forward, the research program was structured to hand its outcome to the Charter’s civic bodies for disposition, with research ethics rigorous enough to survive the scrutiny the deployment debate would bring. Book II picks up in the debate the scientific program prepared for but did not attempt to preempt.
