## 1. Dax Orimoto

**Whose story:** Dax Orimoto. The deaths, the Seventh Ring Wall and the next wall are his, and the audience is felt through his stream.

**Rewrite**

```text
Dax Orimoto
Simulation Type: Lifestyle · Classification: Extreme Sports Pioneer · Doctrine Snapshot: v15.6
Outcome: Main Layer — Most-Watched Athlete in Civilizational History
```

Dax Orimoto has died fourteen times. He is a free solo climber, working without any safety equipment on vertical faces that would be classified as suicide attempts on Earth; in VMSS they are classified as sport. He doesn't describe his deaths casually. Each one is a data point he respects. He can describe each precisely: the specific sensation of impact, how long he stayed conscious after the event, the moment the implant's pain buffer activated and the experience ended.

He streams every climb in audience mode. Neural diving spectator integration carries his full sensory experience in real time to millions of viewers through their implants, and they watch every moment from inside his body. They feel his fingers on the rock and the wind at altitude. They feel the precise moment his grip fails, then the freefall, then the impact. The stream terminates cleanly at the moment of death, so they get the final seconds of sensation before the buffer engages and never the blackout.

His most-watched climb is the unassisted ascent of the Seventh Ring Wall's exterior face, a sheer 15-kilometer vertical surface never designed to be climbable. His first attempt ended in a fall at the 11-kilometer mark. More than 200 million people felt that fall in real time, and nothing in the history of broadcast media had produced a shared physical experience at that scale. They knew he would be revived. The sensory data was indistinguishable from firsthand experience, so their own bodies processed the genuine mortal terror as real. The cultural impact was the collective experience: for all of them at once, the same body hit the same surface at the same terminal velocity.

The backup vessel system revived him at full fidelity in a Main Layer medical facility within hours. The attendant asked how he felt.

"Can I have the recording?" Dax said.

He reviewed the fall from inside until he found the specific motor decision that had failed, a reach with his right hand before his left foot was set. Then he began planning the next attempt.

The second attempt ended in a fall at the 13.4-kilometer mark, in front of another audience exceeding 200 million. When he woke from that one, his bioaugmentation specialist, Renske Tal, was sitting by the bed. His competitors who had augmented their fear response away had smaller audiences, and Renske had the numbers.

"I'll ask once more," Renske said. "Neural augmentation for cognitive processing speed."

"They'd feel the difference," Dax said. "It would come through artificially calm. I'm scared up there, and that's the stream."

She let it go.

The third attempt drew 340 million simultaneous neural diving viewers, and he completed it. The completion reached them as collective euphoria: 340 million bodies at once feeling the summit contact, the grip holding, and the proprioceptive confirmation of arrival at a point that three attempts and two deaths had made feel impossible.

Dax is thirty-one, age-pinned at twenty-six for peak physical performance, a decision he and Renske made after analyzing the recovery data from his first eight deaths. His body modifications are minimal by VMSS standards: enhanced grip strength, accelerated lactic acid clearance, reinforced skeletal density at impact points.

His AGI manager handles the commercial infrastructure: sponsorship negotiations, scheduling, ImmersionTube distribution rights, and the audience liability waivers VMSS requires for neural diving streams that expose viewers to death experiences. The legal question was novel when his sport emerged: did streaming a death experience to 200 million viewers constitute harm? The Supreme Court ruled that it did not, provided the audience opted in with informed consent and the stream terminated at the pain buffer threshold, and the ruling became the legal foundation for the entire extreme sports streaming industry.

What Dax thinks about is the next wall. He has identified a route on the exterior of a -1 boundary wall that has never been attempted, a face with variable surface geometry designed to prevent exactly the kind of climbing he does. His training footage goes up on ImmersionTube as a serialised preparation diary and has already drawn 40 million subscribers. They are training with him. They feel his hands on the practice wall and his assessment of the route's difficulty through his own proprioceptive uncertainty.

"The climb is on the calendar for spring," his manager said. "How many attempts should I book?"

"Leave it open," Dax said. "I expect to die at least twice before I complete it."

Key lesson: Because revival reverses a death after the fact, a sport can keep lethal stakes and real fear and still be survivable. Streamed through the implant, it gives audiences something no Earth sport could.

## 2. Idriss Laâbi

**Whose story:** Idriss Laâbi. Recording dreams starts as his question about resolution, the archive grows out of his own collection, and the sealed personal archive is his instruction.

**Rewrite**

```text
Idriss Laâbi
Simulation Type: Lifestyle · Classification: Dream Curator · Doctrine Snapshot: v15.6
Outcome: +1 Sanctuary — Founder of the Civilizational Dream Archive
```

Idriss Laâbi was a lucid dreamer before he was anything else. At fourteen he could sustain awareness inside a dream for twenty minutes without the state collapsing. At twenty he could re-enter the same dreamscape on consecutive nights and revisit the environments he had constructed, with enough fidelity to map them, refine them and build on what he had placed the night before. He considered it practice.

When he received his implant at VMSS entry and learned that neural diving could record dreams, he had one question for the technician.

"Does the recording have the resolution to keep what I build? I've spent fifteen years learning how."

"Neural state capture runs at the same fidelity asleep as awake," she said.

He dove his first recorded dream the next morning. The full sensory recording was there, with the emotional tone, the proprioceptive data and the entire phenomenological stream of the dreaming mind, in a format other minds could play back through neural diving. A recording of waking experience gives its audience a coherent sensory environment: physics that behave, consistent spaces, cause and effect operating normally. His recording delivered the dream's own logic, and the playback lost nothing to it. Anyone who dove it would experience the dream as he had, on rules entirely different from the ones they lived by. A corridor shifted into a stairwell he had never built. A cup turned to sand in his hand. A grief he could not account for attached itself to a green door that meant nothing in the waking world.

His first published dream ran seventeen minutes. It opened in a cathedral made of water, a structure whose walls, ceiling and floor were flowing liquid that held architectural form. The audience walked through it, felt the temperature of the surfaces, and heard their footsteps resonate through a medium that should not have been solid. Midway through, the space inverted, and the cathedral became the inside of a bell. They felt the harmonic vibration of a tone that had no audible frequency but registered as physical pressure across the entire body. The dream ended in a garden that smelled like his grandmother's house in Marrakech, a specific olfactory signature that carried its emotional weight to the audience with no context for why.

The reviews were unlike anything the sensory art community had produced. Audiences described the experience as "being inside someone else's subconscious."

The archive grew out of his personal collection. Within two years of publishing that first piece, Idriss had received over four thousand dream submissions from citizens who wanted their dreams preserved and made available, and he curated them. Most were fragmentary, incoherent, or emotionally flat once separated from the dreamer's personal context. His curatorial framework was a card propped against his playback rig: *Structural coherence: does it sustain an environment long enough to inhabit? Sensory density: does it engage more than two senses? Emotional legibility: can an audience access the emotional content without the dreamer's biography? Novelty: does it offer an experience unavailable through waking-world recording?* Roughly one in twelve submitted dreams meets the archive's standard. The collection now holds over fourteen thousand curated dreams spanning sixty years of submissions.

Idriss phased into Sanctuary at fifty-one. By then his STI had held above 90 for nine consecutive years, on the curation work, the teaching, and the careful institutional relationships he had built with the Memory Library system that houses the archive's physical infrastructure. The move had been his to make whenever he chose. The Dream Archive is now a formal division within the Sanctuary Memory Library network.

He runs it with eleven curators, in a structure that emerged from the work, unplanned. Six are human and three are AGI, and the other two are collaborative teams that operate as merged-consciousness units during evaluation. The merged teams catch what the individual curators miss: emotional undertones too subtle for a single mind to isolate, which become visible when two or more perspectives process the same recording simultaneously. Last year one of them caught a thread of dread in a dream that three curators had each called flat.

He still dreams every night, and he still records every dream. His personal collection, the uncurated, unfiltered archive of every dream he has had since receiving his implant, is the largest single-dreamer dataset in the civilization. To him it is the most honest record of his mind that exists, more revealing than his STI ledger and more complete than any autobiography he could write. Researchers in cognitive science, sensory art and consciousness studies request access regularly, and he grants it selectively.

When he filed his continuity instructions, the clerk read the clause back to him: *Personal archive to be sealed for fifty years after final death, then released in full to the public Memory Library.*

"If you ever choose one," she said. "That's generous."

"It's obvious," Idriss said. "I won't be using it."

Key lesson: Dream recording is a trivial extension of neural diving, and an art form, an archival institution and a field of consciousness research still grew from it. The capability was never designed for any of them.

Her body was discharging a state of alertness it had maintained continuously since the moment the link severed, and nine days of accumulated mortal vigilance unwound in a single moment. She sat on a bench inside the boundary checkpoint and cried for ten minutes. She was not sad.

A checkpoint attendant stopped beside the bench. "Do you want me to call someone?"

"No," Yael said. "Give me a minute."

She has not gone back, and she does not plan to. The nine days gave her what she went for: the felt knowledge of genuine mortality, experienced firsthand. She describes the trip to friends carefully, without romanticizing it, and tells them that -3 taught her something about Main Layer that Main Layer cannot teach about itself: the safety she grew up inside is a specific condition, and it shapes every decision, every risk assessment, every relationship, every moment of every day. She had never known, because she had never experienced its absence. She lives the way she lived before.

"Should I go?" a friend asked her once.

"I can tell you what it was like," Yael said.
