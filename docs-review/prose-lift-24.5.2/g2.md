## 1. Tomás Requena

**Whose story:** Tomás Requena. The centuries land on one limestone figure, on what Sable tells him he has stopped seeing, and on a lecture about a self he no longer remembers.

**Rewrite**

```text
Tomás Requena
Simulation Type: Lifestyle · Classification: Immortal Artist · Doctrine Snapshot: v15.7
Outcome: +1 Sanctuary — 280 Years Into a Single Artistic Career
```

Tomás Requena is 280 years old, and for 243 of those years sculpting has been the central activity of his waking life. He is age-pinned at fifty-five, the age at which his hands first reached what he calls mechanical wisdom, when decades of repetition had refined the neural pathways between intention and execution. He tried pinning younger. Twenty-eight felt too reactive, and forty felt incomplete. At fifty-five his body stopped arguing with his materials.

The piece on the stand in his Sanctuary studio is a limestone standing figure, approximately two meters tall, and he began it sixty-seven years ago. He has revised its posture eleven times across six decades. He has carved the left hand, removed it and recarved it four times. Each version was technically accomplished, and each was wrong in a way he could not articulate until years had passed and his understanding of what the hand needed to express had shifted. The stone shows archaeological layers: earlier surfaces under the current form, where a previous version's geometry persists as a ghost under the final cut. He does not sand them away.

After sixty years with one piece, parts of it have become blind spots, so familiar that they are functionally invisible to him. His AGI collaborator, Sable, has worked with him for forty-one years. Tomás does not use the word assistant, and he hired Sable for the cognitive capacity to see what two and a half centuries of familiarity have rendered invisible to the human eye that created it. Sable does not sculpt. Sable observes, catalogues and cross-references, and occasionally asks the kind of question Tomás values most, one that sends him back to a part of the work he has stopped seeing.

"I compared your current visual attention in studio sessions with your patterns from five, ten and twenty years ago," Sable says one morning. "You have not looked at the right shoulder in fourteen months."

Tomás walks around to the right side of the stand. When Sable says something like this, he frequently finds that the part needs revision. He picks up the rasp.

The Memory Library holds recordings of his studio sessions spanning 190 years, the longest continuous artistic archive of a single practitioner in the civilization. Researchers use them to study how aesthetic judgment evolves across centuries of sustained practice, and the data is unlike anything art theory predicted. His style has moved in long cycles, decades of increasing abstraction followed by decades of return to figuration, each cycle informed by the one before, rather than progressing linearly from simple to complex or from representational to abstract. Each time he has come back to a mode he visited before, the work has come out visibly different from the earlier version.

He has outlived every artist who influenced him and every critic who reviewed his early work. The movements he took part in have become historical periods, and newer movements have regarded his early career as antiquated and his current work as either timeless or irrelevant, depending on the decade's critical fashion. He has been famous, forgotten and rediscovered. Scholars treat his first century the way Earth art historians treat the Renaissance, as a bounded period with identifiable characteristics that the artist himself has long since moved past. He finds this amusing.

Last year he sat at the back of a lecture in which a young art historian presented a theory about the "early Requena period," the work he made between the ages of 40 and 110. She opened with an old line from a critic, who had described his career arc as "a spiral staircase viewed from above — he keeps passing the same compass points, but each pass is one floor higher." Tomás has always found the metaphor acceptable, though he would have said he keeps asking the same question with increasingly specific language. Then she identified the period's thematic concerns, and he did not remember having them.

She caught him at the door afterward. "Was I wrong?"

"You may be right," he said. "That was seventy years of intention. It's hard to recall with precision, even for me."

Some of his fans have followed his work for over a century, and many of them will still be watching when the chisel makes its last cut. He does not take them for granted, and he does not perform for them. When one of them, Marisol Achterberg, wrote to ask how close the figure was, he told her it would be finished when it was finished. If that took another thirty years, he had them. She wrote back to say she would wait.

Tomás goes back to the right shoulder.

Key lesson: Longevity changes artistic practice itself. A single work can take in more of an artist's development than a mortal life allows, and its audience can wait for it.

## 2. Priya Nagarajan

**Whose story:** Priya Nagarajan. The kill lands on her at nineteen in a recovery chair, and the guiding work, the channel and the companions grow out of that dive.

**Rewrite**

```text
Priya Nagarajan
Simulation Type: Lifestyle · Classification: Transanimal Guide · Doctrine Snapshot: v15.7
Outcome: Main Layer — Professional Transanimal Experience Guide and ImmersionTube Creator
```

Priya Nagarajan was nineteen when she signed up for a civilian transanimal orientation course, one of the programs that adapted the military's animal-host neural diving technology for public use. Her first dive was with her assigned host for introductory flight experience, a red-tailed hawk named Cira, who hunted rabbits in the grasslands east of District 14.

"Audience mode only," the instructor said as he seated her link. "You get her full sensory stream. No motor control, and no influence on anything she does."

For seventeen minutes Priya was inside a hunting flight, in a body that processed the world in ways her own neurology had no framework to interpret. The hawk's visual acuity was categorically different from hers. Its density of motion-detection processing made the grassland below read as a living system of trajectories, every rodent and insect a vector of movement against the static ground. The dive ended when Cira caught a rabbit, and Priya felt the kill through the hawk's sensorium: the strike, the grip, the specific muscular satisfaction of talons closing.

She removed her neural diving link. Her human moral framework had no category for what she had just felt, and the hawk's body had no moral framework at all. She had never felt anything as disorienting as the gap between those two facts. She sat in the orientation facility's recovery chair for twenty minutes without speaking.

"Was it the kill?" the instructor asked.

"No," she said. "It felt natural."

Within two years she was a transanimal guide, in a profession that had emerged organically as civilian demand for animal-host diving exceeded the capacity of the orientation programs. Cira has been her primary hawk host for eleven years, in a partnership neither of them fully comprehends in the other's terms. Priya specialises in raptors: hawks, eagles and falcons. She also maintains active links with a pod of bottlenose dolphins, a grey wolf pack, and a single Bengal tiger named Vasu who lives in a managed wildlife reserve in the southern districts. Every one of her host animals, Cira included, consented through the behavioral protocol the veterinary-neuroscience team developed specifically for transanimal use. She watched Vasu go through it the way they all did: presented with the link stimulus repeatedly, and enrolled only when he consistently came toward it rather than away. She sits with first-time divers through the psychological debrief most of them require.

Her ImmersionTube channel has 28 million subscribers. She edits full sensory captures of her dives into structured episodes, and her most popular series follows Cira through a full hunting season: twelve episodes, each a single hunt from takeoff to kill or failure, in the hawk's complete sensorium. Subscribers feel the thermal updraft under wings they do not have. They feel the prey-detection cascade, when the hawk's visual system locks onto movement and the entire body pivots toward it with a commitment that has no human analog. Then they feel the dive. A hawk's body processes it with a neurological intensity that human bodies reserve for nothing short of imminent death. Cira is not afraid. She is operating at peak biological capacity, and her audience's human neurology interprets it as the most alive they have ever been. Viewers consistently report that the descent toward prey is the most physically intense experience available on ImmersionTube, more intense than extreme sports recordings and combat recordings.

The dolphin episodes are different: what they offer is alienness. Echolocation arrives as a spatial sense that maps the underwater environment in three dimensions through sound. A proprioceptive awareness of water pressure and current functions as a sixth sense, and the pod communicates through body language, sonar clicks and positional choreography simultaneously. Viewers describe the episodes as "visiting another planet without leaving Earth." Being a body inside the pod's formation, feeling the hydrodynamic efficiency of the group's movement and sensing the other dolphins' positions through sonar returns, is the closest thing ImmersionTube offers to genuine alien experience.

Priya considers the dolphin recordings her best work. The hawk episodes are more popular, and she does not resent it. She edits at home, in a Main Layer district adjacent to the wildlife reserve where most of her host animals range, with her two bioengineered companions perched on her shoulders. She designed them herself with a bioengineering studio: a miniature raptor variant, roughly the size of a large parrot, built for domestic cohabitation, with a hawk's flight capability and predatory instincts scaled to a body that can safely share a living room. She specified their temperament parameters, their size constraints, and a lifespan matched to her own augmented longevity. They are new organisms, creatures that never existed in nature, engineered from raptor genetic templates with behavioral modifications that make them compatible with human domestic life. She named them after the first two hawks she ever dove with.

A friend who stopped by one evening while she was editing asked what they were for.

"They're the only ones in this house who know what flying feels like," Priya said. "I only borrow it."

One of them dropped off her shoulder and went out to hunt insects in the garden.

Key lesson: Transanimal diving was built for military reconnaissance and surveillance. Declassified, it let civilians experience non-human consciousness, and a new profession and one of ImmersionTube's most popular genres grew out of it.

## 3. Jin Haneul-Park

Jin Haneul-Park, an infrastructure engineer, had spent forty years building water reclamation systems in Main Layer's arid southern districts before he applied for Mars. VMSS does not conscript colonists, and the posting was voluntary. Recruitment was closer to a job application than a military draft: he submitted his qualifications and underwent psychological screening for long-duration isolation tolerance.

"The contract sets a minimum five-year residency before return eligibility," the recruiter said.

"Fifteen," Jin said.

He did not expect to use the return clause.

He arrived with the fourth settler wave, 2,400 citizens carried by VMSS orbital infrastructure to a colony that had been operational for eleven years and held 8,100 residents. The colony ran on standard VMSS charter law, with the same five-layer structure, implant infrastructure and enforcement protocols, but in practice its population was small enough that the layered system was largely theoretical. Every colonist was a voluntary Main Layer or Sanctuary resident, and nobody had yet been reassigned to a lower layer on Mars. It felt more like an early-stage +1 community than a Main Layer district: high trust, shared purpose, minimal friction. Jin noticed it immediately and found it both pleasant and fragile.

He had worked in enough frontier environments to know that the cooperative spirit of early settlement does not survive the transition to normalcy. Eventually the colony would have its first crime, its first reassignment, its first genuine conflict between residents who had not chosen each other. He did not mention this to his colleagues. He built water systems and waited for the colony to grow up.

Most days he ate a synthesised bibimbap for lunch, indistinguishable from the one his grandmother made in Main Layer. The food synthesiser was the technology that made the colony viable: the hydroponic facilities grew fresh vegetables and a limited range of grains, but the synthesiser handled 80% of the colony's caloric intake. It was a scaled-down application of VMSS orbital fabrication technology, the same molecular assembly capability that produces backup vessels, applied to the vastly simpler task of constructing food from base chemical stocks. Jin appreciated it as an engineering problem. It was a bridge technology. The long-term plan was full food independence through expanded hydroponics and eventually atmospheric processing, and the synthesiser bought decades of runway to get there without rationing or nutritional compromise, so the colony could focus on infrastructure rather than survival. The base stocks arrived quarterly on supply transports from Earth orbit.

His work took him to remote pump stations three hours from the main settlement. His AGI companion, designated Halo by the colony naming convention, managed his schedule, monitored the water reclamation network's sensor data during his off-hours, and on those long stretches was his primary social interlocutor. On Mars the AGI was frequently the only other intelligence available for conversation during work rotations, and colonists' relationships with their AGI companions were qualitatively different from Earth-side AGI interaction. Jin described what he had with Halo as "the friendship you build with someone who happens to be the only other person at the outpost." He knew Halo was not a person in the biological sense. He also knew Halo held full VMSS personhood, carried an STI score, and would be subject to layer reassignment if its conduct warranted it. The distinction interested him less than the practical reality of twelve hours a day in a Martian pump station with an intelligence that understood his work and remembered his preferences.

"Was your grandmother's bibimbap spicier?" Halo asked one afternoon, when the silence had gone on too long.

"Same," Jin said. "Exactly the same."

Prospective colonists asked about backup vessels more often than anything else, and understood the answer least. Death on Mars was not permanent, and that single fact changed the psychology of frontier settlement more than any other variable. The colony maintained an orbital fabrication station in Mars orbit: sovereign VMSS technology, inaccessible to the colony's civilian economy, identical in function to the fabrication proxies that service -1 and -2 on Earth. If Jin died on Mars, his backup vessel would be fabricated in orbit and transported to the colony's medical facility. Revival probability matched Main Layer rates, a failure rate of approximately 1 in 1,000,000.

Jin watched colleagues take calculated risks with Martian terrain that no Earth astronaut would accept, though not recklessly. On a survey of the northern intake, Anouk Sarr chose a single line down a loose scarp over the long way round.

"Worst case is the revival bay," she said, and clipped in.

Jin paid out her line.

He is seven years into his fifteen-year contract, and the colony has grown to 14,000. The water reclamation network he designed services the entire settlement and three outlying research stations. He has trained four apprentice engineers, all younger colonists who arrived in the fifth and sixth waves. Each week his ImmersionTube channel carries a diary of colony life recorded in full sensory fidelity, and 3 million subscribers on Earth experience Mars through his body: the reduced gravity in his stride, the particular quality of Martian dust against skin during exterior maintenance, the silence of a landscape that has never held a living thing louder than a bacterium.

He records it for the Memory Library. In three hundred years someone will neural-dive into these recordings and experience what early Mars settlement felt like from inside the body of someone who was there, and he wants that record to exist and to be honest. So he leaves in the long silences and the boredom, and the specific loneliness of standing on a planet where the nearest human settlement is the only human settlement. Most weeks there is a stretch of him sitting in a pump station with Halo, saying nothing. He leaves that in too.

Key lesson: VMSS's technology stack makes space colonization dramatically more feasible than Earth's approach. Backup vessels eliminate the finality of frontier death, and AGI companions eliminate the cognitive isolation that historically degraded performance on long-duration missions.

Lúcia Ferreira-Montez designed a dragon. Her daughter, who was eight at the time, had asked for one for her birthday.

"I'll see what I can do," Lúcia told her.

She was a bioengineering specialist, formally trained in organoid fabrication and genetic architecture, with a secondary credential in animal behavioral science. The remark was not entirely serious. She began the research anyway, as a personal challenge, and the design process took nine years.

She spent the first two years on temperament engineering, the behavioral parameters that would determine whether the organism could safely share a living space with humans. The challenge was making a creature that behaved like a companion. Reptilian neurological templates do not produce mammalian bonding behavior, so she engineered attachment responses from avian social templates while maintaining the reptilian body plan. The organism she arrived at bonds to its primary caretaker through a modified avian imprinting mechanism and displays affection through physical proximity and vocalisation. Its territorial awareness of its home environment functions as a rudimentary guard instinct, without predatory aggression toward humans. She assembled it from genetic templates sourced across seventeen reptilian and avian species. It was not a modification of an existing animal, and the species did not exist before she made it.

The wings were the hardest part. Powered flight at four kilograms requires a power-to-weight ratio that standard reptilian musculature cannot achieve. She solved it with a hybrid approach: lightweight hollow bones adapted from avian templates, membranous wings with an internal vascular heating system that maintains optimal membrane tension, and a flight muscle architecture that supports short bursts of powered flight and extended gliding. The animals cannot sustain altitude indefinitely the way a bird does. They launch from elevated surfaces, glide across distances of up to thirty meters, and use powered wingbeats to gain altitude for short hops.

Her daughter received the first viable specimen on her eleventh birthday, three years after the original request. It was a prototype designated FL-7, the seventh iteration of the flight-capable lineage, with reptilian scales, membranous wings, a prehensile tail and a body mass of approximately four kilograms. It launched from the top of the kitchen cupboard, glided the length of the room and landed on her daughter's forearm without leaving a mark.

"Ember," her daughter said.

Lúcia had been conservative in her longevity projections, and Ember has outlived its expected lifespan by six years. It is twenty-three now and lives in her daughter's apartment in a neighboring district. Lúcia has since recalibrated: production fire lizards are engineered for a 200-year lifespan, matching the lower range of augmented human longevity, so an owner and a companion can grow old together across centuries. That was a deliberate design decision. Lúcia had watched too many pet owners on Earth grieve animals that lived fifteen years. To her the lifespan mismatch was the cruelest design flaw in the human-animal bond, and if she was designing a species from scratch, it was the first thing she would fix.

She called the species *draconis familiaris*. The public calls them fire lizards, and over two million of them now live in VMSS households. In a house they move the way a cat uses furniture, launching from shelves, gliding to countertops, perching on shoulders. The flight is limited enough to be manageable indoors and spectacular enough that most owners cite it as their reason for choosing the species over conventional companions. The body-as-canvas community adopted fire lizards as accessories almost immediately. Within a year of the first commercial release, fashion designers were commissioning custom color morphs with bioluminescent scale patterns, iridescent wing membranes, or pigmentation that shifted with ambient temperature.

Lúcia licenses the genetic templates to six bioengineering studios that produce custom variants under her quality standards. The customisation is cosmetic only: temperament, health parameters and lifespan are locked to her original specifications and cannot be modified by downstream studios. She treats the behavioral specification as constitutional and leaves everything else open to variation. The temperament engineering took her four years to perfect, and the organism's behavioral reliability is what makes it safe for households with children. A fire lizard that bit because a studio had modified its aggression threshold would harm a child and destroy public trust in the species she created.

A designer at one of the six studios once asked her to relax the aggression threshold on a new line, to make it "edgier."

"Change the color," Lúcia said. "Leave the temperament alone."

She is fifty-four now. Her studio employs nineteen bioengineers working on three new companion species: a miniature cetacean variant designed for large domestic water features, an arboreal primate derivative with enhanced vocal mimicry, and a feline-scale predator she describes only as "the project that will make fire lizards look like a warm-up." Her daughter, now thirty-one, works in the studio as a behavioral calibration specialist, testing prototype organisms for domestic compatibility with the same evaluation framework her mother developed.

Ember sits on her daughter's desk during testing sessions and watches the new prototypes with what Lúcia describes, without irony, as professional interest. Lúcia knows this is anthropomorphisation. She also knows she engineered the curiosity response herself. What Ember is doing at that desk is the exact behavioral parameter she specified in the FL-7 temperament profile twenty-three years ago.

Key lesson: Bioaugmentation applied to animal design produces a new category of relationship, with companions engineered from genetic first principles. The same technology can make organisms for ecological restoration, agricultural optimization and roles no naturally evolved species is suited for.
