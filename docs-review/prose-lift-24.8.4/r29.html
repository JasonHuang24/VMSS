Resource 29: The Monetary Silo

An Economics Textbook Excerpt — Cross-Layer Transfer, the Retirement Mechanic, and the Circulation Question

VMSS operates four siloed currency economies stacked along the layer gradient. +1 Sanctuary and Main Layer share a common currency; -1, -2, and -3 each maintain separate currencies that do not convert upward under any circumstance. Downward conversion is permitted only through authorized channels and only under a retention schedule that retires origin-layer currency at the conversion point. The architecture raises design questions the civilization’s economists return to periodically: why retire currency at the conversion boundary rather than recirculate it, what the retirement mechanic produces across the aggregate economy, and whether the design reflects necessary architectural commitments or a legacy preference the civilization could reopen without damage. This resource examines the monetary silo architecture from the ground up. It treats the retirement-versus-recirculation question as open, because both sides have real arguments and the civilization’s choice is defensible without being uniquely correct.

The Architectural Problem Siloing Solves

A currency that circulated freely across every layer of the civilization would open an arbitrage channel the layer system cannot survive. A resident who earned purchasing power in Main Layer conditions (robust institutional supply, market-priced goods, extensive fabrication infrastructure) could carry it into -3 Terminal, where the same currency commands two-and-a-half to four times the real consumption. The wealth gradient would invert the punitive gradient. Lower-layer residence, which the architecture positions as a restriction of institutional benefit, would become economically advantageous for any resident with accumulated upper-layer capital, and the layer system’s moral geometry would collapse under that pressure.

The same failure runs in the opposite direction. If lower-layer residents could convert their currency upward at face value, the -3 economy, with its Earth-analogous capitalism, equity markets, private banking and absence of VMSS circulation oversight, would become a staging ground for moving capital into Sanctuary and Main without passing through the civilization’s anti-concentration instruments. The Savings Circulation Mandate, the tiered progressive taxation, and the property ownership caps all operate on the upper-layer currency. An unrestricted upward channel from a layer that does not honor those constraints would let capital accumulate at the rate the constraint-free economy allows and then enter the constrained economy at face value, bypassing every anti-oligarchy instrument the architecture runs.

Currency siloing closes both failure modes at once. Each layer’s economy is self-contained, and purchasing power accumulated within a layer cannot be exported at face value to a layer where it would mean something different. Residents are not prohibited from moving; their value simply does not move with them except through the channels the architecture specifies. That is how the silo protects the layer system’s moral and institutional geometry at the economic level.

The Mechanics of the Silo

The architecture runs on four distinct currency units. +1 Sanctuary and Main Layer share a single currency; the two upper layers are functionally one monetary zone because their populations move freely between them and the institutional infrastructure is continuous. -1 Noncompliance, -2 Violent Offense, and -3 Terminal each maintain separate currencies that exist only within their own layer economies. The central bank, the only genuinely new institution VMSS introduces with no direct Earth analog, is sole issuing authority for all four currency units and operates as infrastructure rather than policy. It does not set interest rates or engage in monetary stimulus. It maintains the integrity of the silo architecture and executes settlement when currency crosses layer boundaries.

Upward conversion is prohibited without exception. A -3 resident cannot exchange freedom tokens for Main Layer currency under any circumstance: not at the central bank directly, not through intermediated channels, and not through goods arbitrage, because cross-layer goods trade settles in the destination layer’s currency at PPG-adjusted rates rather than through currency exchange. A resident who returns upward from elective residency carries their citizenship upward but not their accumulated lower-layer currency. That currency stays in the economy where it was earned, and the resident arrives in the upper layer economically neutral and must earn upper-layer currency through upper-layer work.

External inconvertibility runs in parallel. VMSS currency does not circulate outside the civilization’s borders, and foreign nationals cannot acquire it by exchange. International trade runs on a goods-for-goods or goods-for-foreign-currency basis, with the central bank as the counterparty: it absorbs foreign currency and releases VMSS currency only into the internal layer economies. An ally nation trading with VMSS delivers goods or foreign currency, receives goods or service access, and never holds VMSS layer currency as a tradeable instrument. The silo is hermetic, externally as well as internally.

Downward Transfer Channels and the Gradient

Three categories of resident can move value downward across the silo boundary. Visitors descending temporarily may convert a portion of their upper-layer assets under the downward transfer retention schedule (1 to 10 percent of net worth, depending on the visitor’s total assets) into destination-layer currency at the applicable purchasing power gradient multiplier. Elective residents, who keep upper-layer citizenship while living in a lower layer, follow the same retention schedule and conversion mechanics. Voluntary permanent residents, who sever upper-layer citizenship entirely to descend, fall under the progressive liquidation schedule, set in federal law under LP-076 with Article III.V holding the principle. They retain 1 to 10 percent of liquidated assets, converted to destination-layer currency. The remainder, 90 to 99 percent depending on the filing wealth bracket, is absorbed into the Automation Dividend Treasury as the civilization’s institutional absorption of the departing resident’s upper-layer capital.

The retention schedule is progressive because the civilization treats large-capital descent as a stronger signal than small-capital descent. A resident descending with modest net worth retains a higher proportion: the absolute value is unlikely to distort the destination economy, and the retention matters proportionally more to the resident’s post-descent life. A resident descending with substantial net worth retains a lower proportion, because even the reduced proportion is large in destination-layer terms and because the architecture treats large-capital descent as potentially strategic, warranting stronger institutional absorption.

The purchasing power gradient at the conversion point is a range that the central bank derives from observed economic conditions within the destination layer, rather than a fixed exchange rate. -1 converts at approximately 1.3 to 1.8 times Main purchasing power, -2 at approximately 1.8 to 2.5 times, and -3 at approximately 2.5 to 4 times. The range accommodates scarcity conditions, institutional withdrawal, and tourism-inflow pressure, which vary across a layer’s districts and produce real economic variance within a single layer. A tourist-heavy -3 district and a remote cooperative district operate under different PPG conditions at the same time, and the central bank’s settlement rate reflects the conditions of the transferring resident’s destination district rather than a uniform layer-wide average.

The retention schedule and the PPG multiplier compound. A Main Layer resident with ten million units who elects voluntary permanent residency in -3 retains, at the middle of the retention schedule, approximately five percent: five hundred thousand units. Those units convert at the -3 PPG into somewhere between 125,000 and 200,000 -3 freedom tokens, depending on settlement conditions. In -3 purchasing power terms the resident arrives with substantial local wealth; in upper-layer terms they leave with a small fraction of what they held. The combination is intentional. The descent is not economically trivial at the destination, yet the upper-layer economy loses almost nothing from the departure, because ADT absorption keeps almost all of the resident’s upper-layer capital in the upper-layer economy.

Retirement and Recirculation — Where Each Operates

The architecture already runs two distinct value-handling mechanisms at the silo boundary, and the distinction is central to the design question this resource addresses.

Recirculation operates on the portion of descended capital that stays in the upper-layer economy. The 90-to-99 percent liquidation under voluntary permanent residency, the full 100 percent liquidation under involuntary descent, the SCM garnishing that activates when district aggregate savings cross the threshold, and the tiered progressive taxation on upper-layer income all flow into the Automation Dividend Treasury. The ADT redistributes these flows as UBI: $10,000 per month per resident in the upper layers, $5,000 in -1, $2,500 in -2, $1,250 in -3, untaxed, unconditional, and paid to every citizen including newborns. The loop is tight and closed, returning capital absorbed by institutional mechanisms to the population as the dividend of the automated economy. Upper-layer recirculation stays in upper-layer currency and lower-layer recirculation stays in lower-layer currency, so recirculation never moves currency across silos.

Retirement operates at the conversion point itself. When a resident crosses the silo boundary with their retained 1-to-10 percent, the central bank retires origin-layer currency equal to the converted value and issues destination-layer currency equal to the PPG-adjusted converted value. The origin-layer currency is extinguished rather than transferred anywhere; no currency unit crosses the silo, because that would violate currency siloing. The destination-layer currency is freshly issued at the applicable PPG rate. The resident arrives in the destination economy with newly issued local currency, and the origin economy loses the corresponding value from circulation at the moment of conversion.

The two mechanisms serve different architectural functions. Recirculation returns capital to the UBI stream, so that the civilization’s institutional absorption becomes every citizen’s dividend. Retirement preserves the silo invariant, so that currency from one economy cannot appear as currency in another. Both operate in canon, and each is necessary where it operates. Since the architecture already uses both correctly, the design question is narrower than a choice between them: it concerns the specific slice of value that moves across the silo at the conversion point.

The Narrow Question

When a resident converts their retained 1-to-10 percent at the silo boundary, the origin-layer currency is retired and the destination-layer currency is newly issued. The value that existed in the origin economy is gone from it, and the value that appears in the destination economy is net new issuance rather than a transfer of existing currency.

A designer examining this mechanism can reasonably ask why the origin-layer currency has to be retired. The central bank is already sole issuing authority for both currencies, and the destination-layer currency is freshly issued either way, because currency units cannot cross. Retiring the origin-layer currency is an architectural choice rather than an architectural necessity. The central bank could instead absorb the origin-layer currency into the Automation Dividend Treasury and redistribute it as upper-layer UBI, while issuing the destination-layer currency at the PPG rate as before. The silo invariant would hold, since no origin-layer currency crosses, and the destination economy would receive the same net issuance it receives under the current mechanism. The only difference is what happens to the origin-layer value: it disappears (retirement), or it flows through ADT to the upper-layer population as UBI (recirculation).

The question points to a design decision rather than a doctrinal flaw or architectural error. Canon has made the decision and could revisit it without violating the silo invariant, changing the retention schedule, altering the PPG mechanics, or disrupting any other element of the monetary architecture. The decision is internal to the conversion-point mechanism, and the two designs produce outputs similar enough that reasonable economists can disagree about which is preferable.

The Case for Retirement

Retirement has a mild deflationary effect on the origin-layer economy. Every cross-silo conversion removes currency from circulation without a corresponding reduction in goods, services, or productive capacity. Over time, if cross-silo conversions outpace central bank issuance into the upper-layer economy, the upper-layer money supply contracts relative to the real economy and purchasing power per upper-layer unit rises. Residents holding upper-layer currency see a small, gradual appreciation in what it commands.

The effect fits the civilization’s anti-concentration posture. A gently deflationary monetary environment rewards savers modestly, strengthens the upper-layer currency’s relationship to the goods it purchases, and slightly compresses the gradient between upper-layer and lower-layer purchasing power, because a relatively stronger upper-layer currency narrows the PPG advantage lower-layer residents experience. A civilization that values stable monetary conditions, and wants mild deflationary pressure to counterbalance the inflationary pressure of the ADT’s continuous UBI issuance, can defensibly argue that retirement is the correct mechanism at the conversion boundary.

Retirement is also definitionally clean. Currency leaves one silo and currency enters the other, and the two are never the same unit. The central bank acts as pure infrastructure at the conversion point, performing issuance and retirement symmetrically. No ADT routing is required, and cross-silo conversion volume affects no monthly UBI distribution, so the monetary plumbing stays decoupled from the fiscal plumbing. A central bank that retires at conversion has a narrower operational remit than one that also routes origin-layer conversions through the fiscal stream, and VMSS’s design philosophy values institutional narrowness.

The Case for Recirculation

Recirculation at the conversion point would redirect the origin-layer value into the Automation Dividend Treasury, from which it would flow to the upper-layer population as UBI. The currency still does not cross, so the silo invariant holds. Instead of being extinguished, though, the origin-layer value stays in the origin-layer economy, redistributed to every upper-layer citizen as a fractional increment to their monthly UBI.

The aggregate effect is small, since cross-silo conversion volume is a small fraction of total upper-layer economic activity, but it is positive-sum for the upper-layer population in a way retirement is not. Retirement benefits upper-layer currency holders diffusely through mild deflation. Recirculation benefits the entire upper-layer citizenry directly through a fractional UBI uplift that compounds across every cross-silo conversion. Each descending resident’s conversion would add a tiny UBI contribution to the population they are leaving, instead of removing that value from circulation.

The coherence argument runs the other way here. Recirculation is already the dominant institutional absorption mechanism: the 90-to-99 percent liquidation, the involuntary-descent 100 percent liquidation, the SCM garnishing, and the tiered taxation all flow to the ADT. Under the current design, the retained 1-to-10 percent slice is the only category of descending capital that bypasses ADT. The architecture does not reject recirculation for this slice; the silo mechanics simply happen to retire the currency at the conversion point. Recirculation at conversion would make the institutions consistent, with every channel through which capital leaves the upper-layer economy flowing to the ADT without exception, which is what the Treasury is architecturally for.

The deflationary counter-argument weakens under examination. The upper-layer economy does not lack deflationary pressure. The SCM, the tiered taxation, and the property ownership caps already operate as anti-inflationary instruments at scale, and they, not currency retirement at the margins, offset the ADT’s continuous UBI issuance. The mild deflation retirement produces is a second-order effect, below the threshold at which any resident or institution would observably notice it, and the civilization’s monetary stability does not depend on it.

Why the Effect Is Second-Order

The aggregate volume of cross-silo retained-slice conversions is small relative to the upper-layer money supply. Descending residents are a minority of the population in any given year, and the retained slice is at most 10 percent of their assets. The upper-layer money supply includes UBI at the scale of $10,000 per month times approximately 3.3 billion residents, continuous ADT injection, the full accumulated savings of Sanctuary and Main combined, and the monetary circulation of the entire productive economy. Against that aggregate the retained-slice volume is a rounding error: fractions of a percent annually, possibly smaller depending on descent rates in any given period.

At this volume, neither design produces an effect any individual resident could observe. No resident detects a difference in purchasing power from retirement’s deflation, and none detects a UBI increment from recirculation’s injection. Price levels, interest dynamics, savings behavior, and consumption patterns are unchanged at the resident-visible level under either design. The question is one of architectural elegance rather than economic consequence.

Evaluated at the upper-layer aggregate level, the two mechanisms produce outcomes statistically indistinguishable at resident-experience scale. The difference shows only in institutional bookkeeping: what the central bank does at the conversion point, whether the ADT receives a line item from conversion volume, and whether the fiscal architecture runs a few more transactions per descent cycle. The difference is real but small, and it does not compound into any observable macroeconomic outcome at the horizons the civilization operates on.

The Doctrinal Response

Canon operates retirement at the conversion point, and the design has been stable across the civilization’s major monetary reforms. The central bank’s operational architecture is built around retirement-at-conversion, the §12.2 wording specifies retirement explicitly, and the mechanism integrates cleanly with the other silo invariants the architecture depends on. The decision reflects a preference for narrower central bank operational scope, cleaner fiscal-monetary separation, and the mild deflationary counterweight retirement provides against the ADT’s continuous injection pressure.

The decision is also defensible against its alternative. If the civilization reopened the question and chose recirculation-at-conversion, the resulting economy would be statistically indistinguishable from the current one. Its institutional architecture would be slightly more consistent (every capital departure routes to the ADT), and its central bank would have a slightly broader operational scope (conversion transactions would include fiscal routing as well as monetary issuance). The alternative is a different defensible landing on a design question whose outcomes converge at the aggregate level.

The civilization’s economists treat the retention-versus-recirculation question as a legitimate subject of ongoing scholarship rather than a settled matter. Published analysis engages it, and academic economics conferences in VMSS Advanced institutions periodically return to it. The doctrinal position has three parts: retirement is canon; recirculation is a coherent alternative whose adoption would require revisiting §12.2 and §12.3 in modest detail; and the architecture’s monetary stability does not depend on which mechanism operates at the conversion boundary. The question stays open by choice, because the civilization treats questions whose outcomes are architecturally similar as suited to continued analysis rather than rigid closure.

The Student’s Error

The student who concludes that currency retirement is wasteful has misread the aggregate. The retained slice volume, the deflationary effect, and the purchasing-power increment recirculation would give upper-layer citizens are all small, and no resident experiences the difference. Calling retirement waste requires a frame in which the extinguished currency is the primary analytical object, but currency is not a resource in the way goods are. The central bank can issue currency at any time. What matters is the monetary environment the issuance produces, and under retirement that environment is stable, mildly deflationary, and operationally clean. Retirement is a different architecture from recirculation, with a different set of second-order effects, none large enough for waste to be a meaningful characterization.

The student who concludes that recirculation is obviously superior because it returns value to the population has overstated its benefit. Divided across the upper-layer population, the recirculated value produces a UBI increment too small for any resident to detect against the monthly UBI payment they already receive. The gain exists in aggregate but not in any individual’s experience. A policy whose benefit is real at the statistical level and invisible at the experiential level is not obviously preferable to an alternative with symmetric properties in the opposite direction.

The student who concludes that the retirement-versus-recirculation question is unimportant has missed what it reveals about the architecture. Its economic outcomes barely differ, but its framing exposes how the civilization’s monetary architecture integrates with its fiscal architecture at the silo boundary. Working through it requires a precise understanding of what currency siloing requires, what the central bank’s operational scope is, how the ADT interacts with cross-layer movement, and why the layer system’s moral geometry depends on the silo invariant holding without exception. The question is pedagogically valuable even when its answer is practically inconsequential.

The deepest observation belongs to the student who notices that the monetary silo is the architectural element VMSS most closely inherited from Earth-era central banking and most substantially transformed. Every Earth-era central bank manages issuance, retirement, and circulation for one economy within a single currency zone. VMSS’s central bank operates four currency zones simultaneously, manages issuance and retirement across silo boundaries, executes settlement at conversion points, and keeps economically separate populations who share citizenship but not monetary geography. The institution is recognizable as a central bank, but its operational architecture has no Earth-era analog. The retirement-versus-recirculation question exists only because of that architecture; no Earth-era economy had silo boundaries at which currency could be retired or recirculated as design alternatives. The civilization inherited the institution and rebuilt its mechanics to support the layer system’s moral geometry, and reopening any question within the rebuilt mechanics, this one included, is part of its ongoing refinement of an institutional category it transformed beyond what its Earth-era predecessors could have imagined.
