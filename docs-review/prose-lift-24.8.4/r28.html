Resource 28: Backup Vessel Architecture

A Technical and Philosophical Studies Textbook Excerpt — Continuity as Doctrine, the Captured-Cognition Problem, and the Questions the Architecture Leaves Open

Backup vessel infrastructure is the civilization’s continuity mechanism. Every Charter provision involving revival, every layer’s institutional character and every enforcement posture’s proportionality depends on the architecture working reliably enough to support the doctrinal commitments built on it. The Charter establishes the mechanism, the whitepaper specifies the five failure modes, and the Academy’s Q22 A+ response identifies the captured-cognition problem the mechanism surfaces but does not resolve. This resource extends those foundations. The civilization’s official position is that revival preserves continuity, but the architecture that produces that continuity raises questions the doctrine has not fully closed, and this resource takes them up.

Continuity as Doctrine, Not Metaphysics

Whether a revived citizen is “the same person” admits competing answers under every framework that examines it seriously. Teleporter paradoxes, parfit-style identity puzzles, replica-and-original thought experiments: the tradition is long and the arguments do not converge. The civilization does not try to resolve the philosophical question. It makes the question operationally irrelevant.

Continuity-not-innocence is a doctrinal commitment and makes no metaphysical claim. The civilization takes no position on whether revival preserves the numerically same consciousness or produces a consciousness continuous enough with the original that the distinction fails to matter. It takes a position only on how it will treat the revived person. The ledger, the STI score, the layer assignment, the SAD stack and the criminal record all persist. Every institutional fact that identified the resident before death identifies them after revival, with no interruption, no reclassification, and no procedural step that would register revival as a discontinuity. A Sanctuary resident who dies on Tuesday and revives on Wednesday returns to their apartment still a Sanctuary resident, still holding their RIL and CCD memberships, still owing on their contracts and still carrying their conduct record. Because the civilization treats them as the same person, they are the same person for every purpose the civilization adjudicates.

The approach deliberately separates the philosophical question from the operational framework, and it involves no metaphysical sleight of hand. The framework produces every effect that would follow if the metaphysical claim held true, without having to defend that claim on its own terms. A civilization committed to the metaphysical claim would have to defend it against every philosophical tradition that challenges it. One committed only to the operational treatment can accommodate, within the same doctrinal framework, residents who believe revival preserves consciousness, residents who believe it produces a continuous copy, and residents who think the question has no determinate answer. Each resident’s personal view of what revival means to them is private cognition the civilization does not touch. The civilization’s position concerns what it will do rather than what is true, and that distinction lets continuity function without consensus on the underlying question.

The Sync Architecture

Mind-state synchronization is continuous rather than episodic. This is doctrinal. The implant captures neural state in real-time streaming rather than in periodic snapshots, and the captured data flows to the backup vessel’s maintained mind-state reservoir without meaningful lag under ordinary operating conditions. A resident’s backup vessel at any given moment holds a mind-state that trails their live consciousness by at most the latency of the capture and transmission layer — measured in fractions of a second, not minutes or hours.

Under continuous sync, the Academy’s Q22 47-minute gap is structurally anomalous. A backup vessel revival that leaves a resident missing 47 minutes of experience is a glitch: the sync layer fell out of continuous operation during the window in question, the captured data was incomplete or lost before transmission, or the revival drew from an earlier stable mind-state because the current state was unavailable. The glitch framing moves the 47-minute gap out of the category of doctrinal features requiring philosophical resolution and into the category of engineering failures requiring technical attention. The revived Yara’s missing experiences are what the system produced when it failed, for specific technical reasons that the fabrication proxy’s post-revival diagnostics document for investigation.

Continuous sync captures the full neural state, which is broader than the resident’s conscious experience. Unsent messages, unexpressed thoughts, physiological stress responses and draft communications the resident composed and decided not to send all exist within the captured mind-state, because the capture does not distinguish thought the resident chose to act on from thought the resident did not. The sync captures the substrate, and the substrate holds both, since at the neural level thought consists of patterns the resident may or may not choose to consolidate into action.

Q22’s A+ response calls this territory the captured-cognition problem. The Charter’s privacy architecture commits to cognition being non-public: thought produces no consequence, only outward expression does. Continuous sync captures cognition as part of its ordinary operation. The capture is not an attempt to police thought. Sync that separated thought-to-be-expressed from thought-to-be-private would need a prior judgment about which category a given neural state belongs in, and the capture layer cannot make that judgment without knowing the thought’s subsequent trajectory. The architecture captures everything and leaves classification to the layers above the capture. The tension between capture and privacy is structural, and the civilization has not fully resolved where the line between them should fall.

The Five Failure Modes

Whitepaper §17.1.2 enumerates five failure modes beyond terminal layer severance, and notes that these push effective death rates 10 to 50 times higher than the published revival failure rates would suggest in isolation. The enumeration is canonical. This section asks how the modes interact and what the 10–50x multiplier means for residents’ actual mortality exposure.

Implant removal is the most straightforward failure. A resident who voluntarily removes their implant severs the backup vessel link. The vessel may continue to exist in whatever state the resident’s last complete sync produced, but no further synchronization occurs, and revival eligibility depends on the pre-removal state rather than the resident’s current condition. Voluntary implant removal is rare in the upper layers, where the implant is mandatory for residency, and more common in lower layers, where removal carries fewer structural consequences. A resident who removes their implant in -1 or -2 and later dies faces a revival process operating from whatever pre-removal state the vessel holds, with all intervening experience unrecoverable.

Economic inability captures the cases where a resident cannot afford the backup vessel’s ongoing maintenance costs. Vessels require biological substrate, fabrication resources, and continuous infrastructure allocation. The upper layers absorb these costs through institutional infrastructure and lower layers deliver them through fabrication proxy installations. In marginal cases, though, the maintenance may not be continuous: residents in deeply economically distressed situations, residents whose local cooperative infrastructure has failed, or residents whose commitments to private providers lapsed. A vessel whose maintenance has lapsed is not guaranteed to be revival-ready when needed.

Fabrication glitches are defects that emerge during revival itself. The vessel is fabricated from feedstock and assembled at the proxy installation, and the fabrication process operates at tolerances where occasional undetected defects compound. The defect may be biological, such as organ systems that fail under the stress of transfer initiation or neural architectures with subtle imperfections the pre-transfer inspection does not catch. Or it may be procedural, such as transfer timing off by microseconds in ways that affect integration. Most fabrication glitches manifest during revival and produce the binary failure the doctrine documents: the resident does not wake up, or wakes up and deteriorates beyond the system’s recovery capacity.

Supply chain shortages affect the fabrication proxy’s capacity to deliver revivals on schedule. Fabrication technology does not make a civilization immune to distributional failures in feedstock availability, energy allocation, or fabrication facility throughput at peak demand. A revival queue that backs up during a mass casualty event has consequences for residents: revivals delayed past viability windows, vessels held in stasis longer than the architecture was designed to support, and facilities running at capacities their engineering margins do not accommodate.

Biological rejection is the irreducible floor. Some percentage of mind-state transfers fail at the neural integration step regardless of the fabrication’s quality, the sync’s completeness, or the infrastructure’s availability. The vessel’s architecture rejects the incoming mind-state the way an immune system rejects a transplanted organ, through a fundamental incompatibility between the new architecture and the patterns being integrated into it rather than through any correctable error. Biological rejection rates are low but not zero, and no technological improvement the civilization has deployed has reduced them to zero across centuries of operation.

The 10–50x multiplier emerges from the compound operation of these modes. A resident whose revival failure rate would be 1 in 1,000,000 under pure biological rejection may face an effective death rate of 1 in 20,000 when all five modes are factored in across a lifetime of exposure. The multiplier is not uniform across the population. Residents in upper layers with continuous implant coverage, economic stability, and access to the highest-maintenance infrastructure experience death rates much closer to the biological floor. Residents whose situations stress several failure modes at once (intermittent implant coverage, economic distress, lower-layer infrastructure) experience rates at the higher end of the multiplier. The civilization publishes the biological floor as the revival failure rate because that is the rate the architecture itself produces. Residents’ actual exposure is meaningfully higher, and honest publication also acknowledges the multiplier.

Visitor Revival and the Origin-Layer Rule

A visitor’s backup vessel synchronizes to the fabrication infrastructure of their origin layer rather than the destination layer. This is doctrinal (Whitepaper §17.1.1), and it has consequences. A Main Layer citizen visiting -2 retains the Main Layer revival rate of approximately 1 in 1,000,000 because their backup vessel is fabricated at Main Layer facilities, not at the -2 fabrication proxy installation. If they die on -2, Main Layer infrastructure revives them; they wake up in their Main Layer vessel and arrive back in Main with continuity preserved. The origin-layer rule is what makes visiting a layer a different experience from residing in it: visitors carry their origin-layer revival architecture into the lower-layer environments they enter, with the -3 exception described below.

The rule switches at reassignment or voluntary permanent residency. Punitive reassignment to -1 or below severs the origin-layer link at the hardware level and automatically binds the citizen to the destination layer’s fabrication proxy installation at the corresponding failure rate. Voluntary permanent residency does the same by election. The citizen’s vessel is relocated, rebuilt, or reinitialized at the destination layer’s infrastructure, and later revivals occur under the destination’s conditions rather than the origin’s. Elective residency, which preserves origin-layer status, does not trigger the switch: the elective resident retains origin-layer revival access indefinitely, which is part of what makes elective residency a different civilizational category from permanent reassignment or filing.

The rule has specific implications for -3 visitation that the continuity parity doctrine in R27 addresses. Under the origin-layer rule alone, a Main Layer visitor entering -3 would retain their Main Layer revival architecture. Article XXV.VI federal law closes this exploit. Visitors must disclose their backup vessel status, and under LP-004.2 backup vessel coverage does not cross the -3 boundary: entry requires documented vessel-link suspension, the link stays severed for the duration of the visit, and it is restored on exit. Death inside -3 is final for visitor and resident alike. The origin-layer rule operates as the default, and the suspension operates as the targeted exception that keeps the default from producing visitor exploitation of -3’s mortality-priced economy.

The Refusal Option

The civilization commits to continuity without compelling it. Whitepaper §17.2 establishes that a citizen may refuse revival: the system preserves the option but does not impose it. Removing the implant and choosing not to continue is an exercise of the autonomy the architecture is specifically designed to preserve.

This has resident-level implications the bailout problem analyzed above does not fully capture. A resident in the upper layers facing the optimization paradox, terminal illness the augmentation infrastructure cannot resolve, or simply the felt exhaustion of a centuries-long life has the architectural option to refuse further revival. Refusal involves neither reassignment nor -3 descent. It is terminal non-continuation for a resident whose layer placement does not require it and whose conduct has not forfeited continuity, a death the civilization accepts because the citizen has chosen it. The choice is numerically rare, but its availability matters: the civilization’s commitment to continuity presumes the citizen wants continuity, and the refusal option preserves room for the cases where that presumption does not hold.

Refusal differs from -3 descent because it is an exercise of autonomy rather than a consequence of conduct. At the moment of the choice, a resident who refuses revival remains a resident of their current layer with their current record, and their death occurs under the architecture that applies to that position. The vessel’s subsequent disposition (decommissioning, archival, or eventual repurposing) falls into the custodianship gap analyzed earlier in this resource, because the civilization has not specified doctrine for vessel disposition following deliberate non-continuation. The gap is one of the architecture’s unresolved edges, and the first case on vessel custody after refused revival to reach the Supreme Court’s novelty filter will establish doctrine the current framework leaves open.

Terminal severance, in context. Whitepaper §17.3 frames -3 terminal severance as the civilization’s heaviest structural consequence. It removes continuity itself, which weighs more than incarceration or economic penalty. The civilization preserves life for everyone who has not crossed the terminal threshold and removes the guarantee for those who have. The framing matters for the architecture’s coherence because it ties the backup vessel system’s central commitment to a concrete function: it is the commitment that makes terminal severance meaningful as a consequence. A civilization without backup vessel architecture would have nothing to remove at the terminal threshold, and -3 would become a different kind of layer entirely. The continuity infrastructure the upper layers depend on and the terminal severance the -3 architecture depends on are one commitment seen from opposite ends: continuity is available, and removing it is what the civilization does when conduct crosses its sharpest line.

The Captured-Cognition Problem

Q22’s A+ response identifies the tension between the privacy architecture (cognition is non-public, no consequence) and the continuity architecture (full-fidelity mind-state capture, which necessarily includes unexpressed cognition). The A+ response treats this as a genuine constitutional novelty awaiting Supreme Court adjudication under Article XXI’s novelty filter, and infers that the Court would rule in favor of the privacy principle to keep the implant from becoming a thought-policing device through architectural side effect. The inference is defensible. The next step is to ask what the ruling has to do, and what it cannot accomplish however it rules.

The ruling must classify captured-but-unexpressed cognition as cognition for consequence purposes. A draft message composed and not sent remains cognition. A witnessed event the resident did not intervene in remains cognition about the event rather than outward action. Physiological stress responses, emotional states and reasoning patterns the resident did not consolidate into action are all cognition, and the privacy architecture’s commitment applies to them. The captured data exists, but it cannot produce consequences the doctrine does not attach to cognition generally.

What the ruling cannot do is keep the data inaccessible. The captured substrate resides in the backup vessel’s mind-state reservoir and in the fabrication proxy’s archives during the revival process. Access to this data is technologically possible for any actor with sufficient institutional standing, and the architecture preserves the data because revival requires it. A ruling that the data must not produce consequences does not make it disappear. It sets the norm but cannot make violating the norm architecturally impossible. A future administration, a future corrupted institution, or a future actor with access to the proxy installation’s archives could in principle retrieve captured cognition and use it for purposes the doctrine forbids. The ruling defines the violation without preventing it.

Stated plainly, the captured-cognition problem is this. The doctrine can forbid using captured cognition against residents, but it cannot prevent the capture, because the capture produces the continuity the civilization is committed to. Preventing captured cognition from existing would mean giving up continuous sync, and revival would then produce residents with gaps, the Q22 scenario the doctrine treats as anomalous failure. The civilization cannot have both zero captured cognition and full-fidelity revival. In this specific respect the architecture holds continuity above privacy, and the Supreme Court’s ruling on the privacy principle compensates for that choice without resolving the underlying tension.

Q22’s A+ closes with an observation about Yara’s right to access her own captured cognition. If granted, that right becomes the resident-facing side of the same architectural tension. A resident who can retrieve their own captured cognition can also share it voluntarily, which makes the cognition recoverable for any purpose the resident consents to. Employers could require it as a condition of employment, though the Charter’s consent provisions would likely strike this down. Partners could request it as a trust signal, with unpredictable relational consequences. The civilization does not typically face the question of what residents will do when given access to data the doctrine classifies as private. The backup vessel architecture is where the question arrives, and the answer is not yet settled.

The Vessel Custodianship Question

A backup vessel is a physical object. It sits in a fabrication proxy installation somewhere, maintained by infrastructure the resident does not typically see and funded by economic flows the resident does not typically manage. Current doctrine is not well developed on who owns the vessel, who controls it, and what happens to it across the resident’s life transitions.

The simplest case is a resident in the upper layers with continuous implant coverage, whose vessel is maintained through institutional infrastructure. The vessel exists throughout the resident’s life, receives continuous sync, and is available for revival when needed. The resident does not own the vessel in any property-law sense; it is institutional infrastructure operating on the resident’s behalf. Nor does any specific institutional actor own it in a way that would allow the actor to dispose of it independently. The vessel belongs to the resident in function but not in property.

Complications arise at life transitions. When a resident descends to -1 or -2 through punitive reassignment, their vessel’s maintenance transitions from upper-layer infrastructure to the lower-layer fabrication proxy. Does the vessel physically relocate? Is a new vessel fabricated in the destination layer? Does the original persist in its original location while a new sync relationship forms with the lower-layer infrastructure? The doctrine does not specify, and the implementation likely varies with the specific transition’s conditions. For most practical purposes the resident does not experience the transition, since their continuity carries through, but the architectural details matter when transitions cascade or interact.

The sharper case is terminal reassignment to -3. The implant severs the backup vessel link at the hardware level. The resident is not going to use the vessel again, so its later state is not their concern. But the vessel itself continues to exist somewhere, because the fabrication proxy’s infrastructure does not immediately destroy every vessel whose owner’s link has been severed. What happens to a severed vessel is not well-documented. Options include immediate decommissioning (total material loss), archived preservation (cold storage, kept for some period), or repurposing (biological feedstock reclaimed for future fabrications). The civilization’s practice is likely some combination calibrated to the specific facility’s capacity and institutional framework. The resident has no say.

The still sharper case is a resident who dies without being revived. The mind-state reservoir contains their pre-death state, and the vessel exists in revival-ready condition. What prevents the vessel from being revived against the resident’s wishes (if they had left an explicit do-not-revive instruction) or without any specific authorization (if they had made no arrangements)? The Charter does not establish a mechanism for last-will specification of vessel disposition. The default is revival when the conditions trigger it, and the civilization has not developed the consent architecture that would allow residents to specify otherwise. A resident who wanted to die permanently without descending to -3 has no architectural path to achieve this, because the vessel will be revived regardless of their preference.

The custodianship gap is a genuine doctrinal hole. Most residents are not currently aware of it because their circumstances do not force the question. The first resident to contest the default, by filing a formal request for terminal non-revival without accompanying -3 descent, will bring the question to the Supreme Court’s novelty filter, and the ruling will establish doctrine the current architecture has not specified.

The Bailout Problem

The layer -1 sociology acknowledges that a resident experiencing unbearable circumstances may choose to die deliberately, triggering backup vessel revival and resetting their immediate physical circumstances. The architecture supports this: the implant ledger records the deliberate termination, the fabrication proxy completes the revival, and the resident wakes up in a new vessel with their continuity preserved. The bailout works as an escape valve from circumstances the resident finds intolerable.

The doctrine does not clearly specify what stops iteration. A resident in -2 experiencing ongoing torment from local conditions has, in principle, the option to bail out. Nothing guarantees different circumstances after revival: they are still in -2, they still face whatever local situation produced the torment, and the bailout has reset only their immediate physical state. If the local situation persists, the resident may bail out again, and again. The architecture does not currently specify an iteration limit.

This produces several interrelated problems. First, high iteration counts strain the civilization’s commitment to continuity. A resident who has bailed out twelve times within a decade has accumulated twelve revival events, twelve vessel fabrications and twelve sync restorations, and repeat bailouts absorb the fabrication proxy facility’s capacity in ways the infrastructure was not designed for at scale. Second, the resident’s own relationship to continuity may degrade under high iteration. Each revival resets physical circumstance but preserves cognitive continuity, so the resident remembers bailing out previously and arrives with the accumulated experience of the pattern. At some point the continuity itself becomes the circumstance the resident wishes to escape, and bailout cannot resolve that, because bailout specifically preserves continuity.

Third, and most important for the architecture, bailout as a pattern produces gameable behavior that the consequence architecture does not account for. A resident in -2 facing a specific bad actor who cannot legally be stopped (the layer permits non-lethal harm) can use bailout to escape immediate torment, but the bad actor’s presence persists. Repeated bailout becomes the resident’s adaptation to a layer feature the consequence architecture expected residents to accept as their condition. The architecture did not prohibit non-lethal harm in -2 in order to produce a population using revival as a workaround. It prohibited non-lethal harm in -2 to express the layer’s withdrawal from protection against it. Bailout at scale degrades the expression.

The civilization’s current practice on bailout is permissive, with no iteration limits, no consequences and no institutional friction. It reflects the assumption that residents choosing deliberate termination are exercising legitimate autonomy and should not be obstructed. The assumption holds at low iteration counts and begins to break down at high ones, and the architecture does not yet have a mechanism to distinguish a resident’s one-time escape from intolerable acute conditions from systematic use of bailout as a chronic management tool. The bailout problem is one of the architecture’s unresolved edges, and the civilization will eventually need to address it at the level of doctrine rather than practice.

The Student’s Error

The student who reads this resource and concludes that backup vessel architecture solves mortality has misread the architecture. It relocates mortality from the continuous background condition of biological existence to a constrained set of failure modes whose combined rate is much lower than the biological baseline but is not zero. The civilization’s residents still die. They die much less often than Earth-era residents did, and when they die the probability of revival is high enough that most residents do not experience death as terminal. But the 10–50x effective multiplier is not a small number, and across centuries of lifespan the cumulative exposure produces a meaningful population of residents for whom the architecture failed. The revival failure rate published in the layer documentation is the biological floor, and the rate residents actually face is higher.

The student who concludes that continuous sync resolves the identity question has misread the doctrinal commitment. The question remains philosophically open. The architecture renders it operationally inert: the civilization treats the revived resident as the same resident regardless of what view the resident or anyone else takes of the underlying metaphysics. That is a doctrinal choice rather than a philosophical answer. A civilization that made the opposite doctrinal choice, treating revival as producing a new resident requiring fresh classification, would produce different institutional effects without altering the metaphysical question at all. The architecture relies on keeping the operational position and the metaphysical position separate.

The student who concludes that the Q22 scenario is a philosophical puzzle has misread the technical framing. The 47-minute gap is a technical glitch. The backup vessel architecture under continuous sync does not produce gaps of that magnitude as its ordinary output. Yara’s experience is what happens when the system fails, and the failure conditions that produced it are the subject of post-revival diagnostics at the fabrication proxy installation. The gap does raise genuine philosophical questions (captured-cognition, consent to recovery, what identity consists of), but the scenario surfaces them; the architecture was not designed to produce them. Treating the gap as normal operation misses the point: the civilization commits to continuous operation, and the cases where continuity fails are the edge conditions that reveal where the doctrine has not yet fully specified its commitments.

Finally, the student may notice that the rest of the civilization’s doctrine depends on backup vessel architecture without adequately addressing it. Pre-intervention enforcement assumes revival is available for the rare cases where it fails. Post-intervention enforcement assumes revival is available to restore victims. Layer reassignment as consequence assumes the resident continues to exist to experience the reassignment. Voluntary permanent residency assumes continuity through the descent. Even the -3 Terminal hardware-severance design depends on backup vessel architecture existing in the layers above it, because the severance is meaningful only as the removal of a commitment the other layers honour. Every other architectural commitment the civilization makes rests on this one, and the captured-cognition problem, the custodianship gap, and the bailout question are its unresolved edges. Future doctrine will have to address them. When it does, the answers will likely reshape the surrounding architecture as well. The continuity infrastructure is a project the civilization is still building.
