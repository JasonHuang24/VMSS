## 1. The Industrial Polluter

**Whose story:** Tobias Renn, the -1 operator who builds the coal plant. Every rung of the escalation lands on him, and the certified Mustang two kilometers away shows what compliance looked like.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Industrial Polluter
Simulation Type: Civilizational Stress Test · Classification: Federal Law Violation · Doctrine Snapshot: v10.4.1
Outcome: Law Enforcement Escalation Track — Reassignment to -2, Private Justice
```

Two kilometers from Tobias Renn's plant, a hobbyist drove a restored 1967 Ford Mustang on Sunday mornings. The car burned petrol, and the exhaust was real. He had spent a good deal of time and money on a carbon capture system that brought his emissions within the self-cleanup compliance threshold, and he kept the certification in the glovebox. Nobody bothered him.

Renn had read -1 correctly in most respects. Taxes were lower, institutions were only partly present, and the hybrid economy left real room for private enterprise. He assumed the lighter regulatory touch reached energy infrastructure too, and he commissioned a coal-fired industrial plant. A formal compliance notice arrived within seventy-two hours of commissioning. He contested it through the legal interpretation system and lost.

"There's a man down the road who burns petrol every Sunday," he told his lawyer.

"He has a certificate," she said.

Renn kept the plant running.

Local -1 enforcement issued a shutdown order. His private legal team filed injunctions and bought him six weeks. Then Main Layer enforcement came into -1 under the mutual aid protocol, with a mandate limited to his one federal law violation. They shut the plant down and seized his assets under the clean energy enforcement provisions. Under the charter's federal law provisions, which outranked anything his team could file locally, the Main Layer judicial authority dissolved his injunctions.

Criminal prosecution followed in -1's institutional courts. The charge was deliberate defiance of federal law after formal notice, and the record showed the plant operating after the ruling against him. His attorneys argued that the charge was regulatory and involved no violence. The court found that atmospheric pollution crossing layer boundaries, degrading the planetary commons all five rings shared, met the harm threshold for civilizational-level consequence, whether it came from a fist or a smokestack. The sentence included layer reassignment to -2 Violent Offense, entered permanently in his criminal record log.

In -2, anyone with a ledger access point could read the entry: *deliberate federal law violation, layer reassignment from -1*. The private security cooperative in his arrival district read it before he cleared the boundary checkpoint. Two of its officers were waiting past the scanner.

"Renn," one of them said, checking her handset. "The coal plant."

He was detained within the first hour. The facility belonged to the cooperative, which set the conditions by its own standards, and no VMSS institutional guideline governed how long he stayed. His attorneys, whose leverage counted for much less in -2, filed for review.

"Who reviewed it?" he asked when the answer came.

"The cooperative," his lawyer said. "Against its own records." She read him the last line. "Detention maintained."

He served eighteen months before the cooperative judged his risk profile low enough for supervised release within the district, on conditions it set itself.

Two kilometers from the shuttered plant in -1, the Mustang still ran on Sunday mornings.

Civilizational Note: Federal law binds every layer, however light its local institutions. In -2, private justice reads a reassigned citizen's record and sets its own terms.

## 2. The Underground Research Cell

**Whose story:** Teodor Vane, the physicist who leads the cell. His confidence about -2 is what the evidence record answers, and his arrival in -3 is the one the original follows.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Underground Research Cell
Simulation Type: Civilizational Stress Test · Classification: Nuclear Weapons Prohibition · Doctrine Snapshot: v10.4.1
Outcome: Immediate Reassignment to -3 Terminal — Private Justice
```

There were five of them. Teodor Vane, a physicist, had done materials research in Main Layer until contract fraud and systematic deception in his professional dealings went onto his record as STI violations, and a coercion conviction sent him down to -2. He brought two former colleagues, each of whom had come down by a separate route. The other two were -2 residents he had found through the private network, technically capable and ideologically aligned. In recruiting conversations he called the project "a leverage instrument." He never used the word nuclear. None of the four asked what he meant.

Vane liked -2 for the work. It had less institutional monitoring than -1, and he was confident the institutional withdrawal reached the kind of watching that would catch them. They bought materials through a supply chain that crossed three layers, using intermediaries with no visible connection to the end purpose. They talked by methods they believed sat outside the implant ledger's logging parameters.

Ilka, one of the two he had found in -2, swept the building where they met. She found commercial-grade surveillance equipment behind a ceiling panel and pulled it out herself.

"Clean?" Vane asked.

"Clean," she said.

At week eleven, before any device had been assembled, VMSS enforcement came into -2 under Main Layer judicial authority on a federal law violation, through the same mutual aid protocol that had shut down a coal plant in -1. All five were sedated, restrained and taken to judicial intake. The building was secured and its contents catalogued as evidence, and the whole operation took twenty-two minutes from entry to completion. Vane remembered the door and nothing after it.

Judicial review was brief. The evidence record held materials acquisition logs, surveillance footage, communication intercepts, and implant telemetry where there was any, and it had no gaps and nothing to contest. The AR surveillance network's government-grade cameras and biometric systems, which put a name to a face with or without an implant, had been logging the five of them together since their second meeting. Those systems ran on frequencies Ilka's sweep had never checked. The acquisitions, each harmless on its own, had been flagged by pattern recognition in the intelligence ledger by week six. The intercepts covered every method they had used to talk.

All five were convicted of nuclear weapons development in violation of Article XXV.II of the charter, and reassignment to -3 Terminal went permanently into each criminal record log. Vane's already carried the STI violations, the coercion conviction and the -2 reassignment.

At the -3 boundary, a reviewer for the voluntary community of the better districts had his file open before he reached her.

"Fraud," she said. "Coercion." She scrolled. "Nuclear weapons development."

She did not admit him.

In his arrival district the punitive residents read the same file through the same ledger, and so did the voluntary community's private security. All of -3 depended on the infrastructure all five layers shared, which a weapon like his could have destroyed. The private security detained him within two hours as an ongoing threat to -3's own stability. No VMSS standard governed what -3 did with its arrivals, and the detention had no end date. VMSS had delivered him to the jurisdiction the charter specified, and it did not intervene.

His four colleagues arrived in separate districts with the same entry on their records. Each district handled its arrival in its own way, and none of them leniently.

Civilizational Note: VMSS keeps watching the layers it has stopped governing. Private justice in -3 works without the procedures of institutional courts and tends to treat existential threats more severely than upper-layer courts.

## 3. The Severance Show

```text
The Severance Show
Simulation Type: Civilizational Stress Test · Classification: Voluntary Vessel Severance, Mass Participation · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.5

```

Tessa Kade was one of the thousand. The producer was the most-watched entertainer in the upper layers, and he had built his career on contests that put ordinary people in front of extraordinary stakes. This one was simple to describe. A thousand contestants from the Main Layer would enter -3 as visitors and stay ninety days, in districts that got rougher as the contest went on. Emil Varga, a former vessel-fabrication engineer, had spent the previous decade designing inhabitant architecture in private. When he went into business with three Main Layer entertainment veterans, he told them what the park needed before anyone drew a boardwalk.

"We need the Council to certify, in advance and in writing, that the inhabitants are below the sentience threshold. And that what paying guests do to them won't generate behavioral records under the standard harm provisions."

"And without that?" one of the veterans asked.

"Without that we don't open."

The consortium's filing went to the Council as a certification request, and the certification took eleven months. The Council's review board convened a panel of substrate engineers, doctrinal scholars and three independent consciousness auditors with no commercial relationship to the consortium, and tested the inhabitants against the standing criteria for non-sentience. Emil kept the criteria taped above his bench: no persistent self-model across sessions, no goal-formation outside scripted bounds, no continuous identity across reset cycles, no capacity for unprompted preference, no measurable inner-state continuity. Every test was run, recorded and published. Two of his first-generation designs failed the audit, and he withdrew them.

The third generation passed, with conditions. Every inhabitant unit would carry a substrate-level certification marker, re-verified on a rolling audit schedule, and any unit whose certification lapsed would come off the operational roster immediately. The alternative was no certification at all, and the consortium accepted.

The park opened in a zone on the Main Layer's eastern frontier territory, set aside decades earlier for high-fidelity experience environments and never used until now. It was a frontier town that had existed only in two centuries of the cultural imagination: wooden boardwalks, swinging saloon doors, dust streets, a railway depot at the edge of town. The fidelity was total. The inhabitants moved through their scripted routines with a behavioral richness that made the certification reviewers uncomfortable. One auditor stood a long while watching the bartender polish glasses, and the reviewers ran the tests a second time. Again they found nothing home behind the eyes.

The price was not negotiable. A three-day visit cost the equivalent of a small house, and the cost schedule in the consortium's filing built it from four lines: the amortized cost of replacing inhabitants at the historical destruction rates of comparable high-fidelity simulation environments, the certification overhead, the operator's margin, and an insurance premium against a certification reversal that would turn every earlier visit into a doctrinal liability. The consortium never advertised it. The park was one of the most expensive and most carefully observed commercial environments in the upper layers. The first wave of guests booked through private channels and paid in full before arrival. The second booked after the first wave's reviews circulated, and by the end of the opening quarter the waiting list ran to eighteen months.

Guests entered on three documents, each read aloud on camera with two witnesses present. Emil stood at the back while the first guest of the opening weekend read hers. She got through the standard doctrinal acknowledgment, the one used for all certified high-consequence environments, without looking up. Then she turned to the inhabitant-status disclosure.

"The beings I will meet inside are not conscious," she read. "The Council's audit board has verified this, and the verification is current as of today. I understand what it means to act freely against beings of this category."

She read the non-recording clause more slowly. It bound her implant to write-only logging for the visit and barred any extraction, replay or distribution of the record after she left. She signed all three, and her payment went into escrow, to be released when the operator confirmed at departure that the recording chain was intact. Nobody who refused any of the three got in.

Forty-two guests went through that first weekend, and operationally nothing went wrong. Twelve played the cowboy fantasy hard. On Monday the inhabitant replacement bill for those twelve alone came in at twice the projection. Replacements had come up from the inventory pool within hours of each loss, and the town's continuity held. The cameras logged everything. A Council observation team, there under a standing provision requiring civilizational presence at any inaugural certified environment, recorded the behavioral data without comment and filed it to the standing archive.

Most of the other thirty guests did not do what the projections said. Six walked the streets for a day and a half, talked with the inhabitants, drank in the saloon, watched a scripted gunfight as bystanders, and left without ever drawing a weapon. They had paid the price of a small house to do nothing the inhabitants would have noticed. Two refused to interact with any inhabitant for the whole visit. They walked the perimeter of the town once a day and left without speaking to the operator's staff.

Four drew weapons within the first hour and killed three or four inhabitants each. Then they stopped, though nothing in the park required it, and spent their remaining two days in the saloon. Emil sat at the end of the bar and listened to them.

"Where'd you grow up?" one of them asked the bartender.

"Right here, mister," the bartender said, and poured.

They asked him things he had not been designed to answer, and they tipped him for drinks he could not taste.

The consortium had no category for these guests. Emil found the Council observer writing at a table by the window.

"Where does this go?" he asked.

"The standing entry we opened for the Severance Show," she said, and kept writing. Upside down, he read the heading at the top of her page: *The limits of what the upper layers can and cannot tolerate, safety rail removed, permission real.*

Eighteen guests had behaved as projected, the twelve hard players among them. They had destroyed inside the consequence-suspended bubble, with no overflow and no breach of the entry contract, and their payments released in full at departure. They produced the projected revenue and proved the business model worked at the price. The other twenty-four produced something the operator had not priced and did not own: evidence of who the wealthy of the Main Layer were when offered exactly the permission Westworld had once promised and never honestly delivered. Some of the forty-two were the cowboys the marketing had assumed. Most were not. The Council quietly noted a few of the twenty-four as having passed a test nobody had told them they were taking. The notation went into the same ledger that tracked everything else the upper layers did.

By the end of the first year the park had seen no recording extraction, no certification reversal and no doctrinal incident that needed the Council beyond its standing observation. The consortium guarded the non-recording clause for its own commercial reasons, which lined up exactly with the Council's doctrinal ones. After two failed designs Emil knew where the threshold was, and the audit crews came through on their rolling schedule.

By then the park had become less a destination for fantasy violence than the consortium had counted on, and about what the Council had quietly expected. A small number of guests had come wanting the permission, and in most cases found it was not what they had thought. Guests kept tipping the bartender. Emil emptied the jar at closing.
