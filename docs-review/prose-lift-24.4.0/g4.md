## 1. Earth Introduction (Alternative)

**Whose story:** Owen Tuller, an Austin tech founder. He is the rich applicant the lifestyle catalogue pulls in, he bids for priority, and he stands in the same evaluation hall as a garment worker from Dhaka, so every stage of the demand inversion reaches him.

**Rewrite**

```text
Earth Introduction (Alternative)
Simulation Type: Civilizational Scenario · Classification: Founding Era — Demand Inversion · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.0
Outcome: Premium-driven migration, bidding wars for entry, brain drain from the top
```

Owen Tuller came out of the ImmersionTube demo in Austin and couldn't remember where he had parked. He had $200 million in a streaming company. Every platform on Earth, his included, offered a slice of what that booth had just given him, and on the drive home it came to him that he owned a black-and-white television.

That night he read the doctrine, which had been published before anyone applied. It described a cooking show where you tasted the dish, extreme sports with real death in them and no permanent consequence, and dream libraries that archived subconscious experience as a public resource. The UBI figure was on the first page. He had scrolled past it.

Within months of the offer going public, the news ran the application counts as two lines on a chart. The first was the one the analysts had predicted: billions of people from the Global South, drawn by the UBI floor. The second was millions of well-off people from the United States, Western Europe, Japan, South Korea and the Gulf States, who had read the same pages Owen had. His feed filled up with their application receipts. Most of the people posting them had bigger audiences than he did.

"You're on the waitlist with everyone else," his lawyer, Marcy Dunn, told him that spring. "They take applications in order, and they don't formally auction priority. They just can't process several billion people at once."

By then Marcy was taking calls from brokers who sold early-wave places anyway.

Owen made two offers. He would pay for a processing wing at the gate, and he would bring the whole company across, its engineers, its equipment and everything it knew, if VMSS admitted them in one batch. The batch got an April date.

By April the intake looked less like a refugee line than a high-end music festival, with priority access and corporate sponsorship tiers. A friend of Owen's in Palo Alto had been waitlisted and hadn't left his house in a week.

"Nobody's going to stop you," Marcy said when he signed the last of the relocation papers. Her immigration files were open on the desk, tabbed at the rules that stopped poor people arriving or leaving. "There's nothing in them about you."

The priority lounge at the gate was full of people Owen half recognized. Some were founders he knew from Palo Alto, and two tables over, portfolio managers from Wall Street were comparing waitlist stories. A hedge fund manager from London sat down next to Owen and read him the Gamers Domain description off his phone: gaming at civilizational scale with institutional legitimacy, 20-hour work weeks, and a Selective Ascension Domain of its own.

"That's the retirement I actually want," he said.

By the coffee a Hollywood director was telling a Premier League midfielder that ImmersionTube had made film a dead medium. The midfielder was nodding. He wanted to play for crowds that felt every tackle. A woman from a K-pop agency in Seoul showed Owen a spreadsheet. By her numbers, the first year of neural-diving concerts, attended from inside the performer's nervous system, would earn more than the agency's entire touring model on Earth. Across the table a cellist from the London Philharmonic was reading about a sensory-art piece whose emotional arc ran across every human sense at once. She had looked for anything like it on Earth and found nothing.

"I've written for one sense my whole life," she said. "They've got seven."

The evaluation hall was shared. Owen's batch went through the same doors as the applicants who had waited their turn. Ahead of him in line was Rokeya Begum, who had sewn garments in Dhaka, and two places behind him was Priya Sethi, the venture capitalist from Manhattan who had led his last funding round. Every one of them sat the same behavioral evaluation.

The officer's screen listed three things for Owen: his criminal history, his STI-equivalent behavioral record and his demonstrated patterns. Under them it said *Main Layer (0)*.

"What did the processing wing buy me?" he asked.

She checked. "Your April date."

Rokeya's screen said Main Layer too. Their first UBI payments landed the next morning, the same amount to the cent.

That autumn he paid his first taxes into the treasury that funded Rokeya's UBI and his own. The founding plan had phased the infrastructure over decades, and taxes from companies that had moved across whole, like his, were building it now.

Today everything he earns above $10 million is taxed at 50%, and the other half is his to put where he likes. He has put a good part of that half into a studio for sensory composers two floors above his office. Most afternoons he goes up to listen.

Key lesson: The lifestyle catalogue draws the applicants who have a choice, and their capital helps pay for the founding. Placement still follows each applicant's record.

## 2. The Cradle Offensive

**Whose story:** Anneke Roos, a mother in the coordinating -3 community. She bears the ten children, works for one of the corporations the levy hits, loses her contract, security and credit, sends the "come home" message, and takes back the child who answers it.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Cradle Offensive
Simulation Type: Civilizational Scenario · Classification: Economic Exploit · Doctrine Snapshot: v14.5
Outcome: Treasury levy cascade, corporate aggro, pariah status
```

Anneke Roos had come down to -3 for the low taxes and the open markets. She was on a bench in the grain hall the night Tobias Wren, who believed he had found a structural exploit, handed round his page of figures.

The page began with the head count, 100,000 residents, and then the gap: Main Layer's tax escalation curve penalized excess childbearing, but it stopped at the -3 boundary. Further down, underlined: a child born in any layer kept the constitutional right to relocate to Main Layer autoparenting facilities, and every child who went drew $10,000 a month in UBI, untaxed.

"Ten children a family, over five years," Tobias said. "Every one of them goes up to Main immediately. That's a million children and a hundred and twenty billion a year in UBI, and none of us ever paid into the treasury it comes out of."

Someone near the back asked whether Main would come after them for it.

"Come after us how?" Tobias said. "They'd have to tax us down here, and they don't come down here."

Anneke put up her hand with the rest.

Over the next five years she had ten children, and she took each of them to the gate herself. The week after the first one went, a caretaker from the autoparenting facility in Main called her on the cross-layer channel and read her the intake line: clean slate, neutral STI, an independent AI legal advocate, full UBI.

"Did anyone ask why she was sent?" Anneke said.

"We don't ask. She's feeding well, if that helps." The caretaker sounded tired. They were adding a wing to the nursery that month, she said, and another after it.

Tobias kept count. By the fifth year his page said nearly a million children had gone up, and the Automation Dividend Treasury was paying out $10 billion a month on them.

Anneke worked dispatch for a freight operator. That spring a treasury notice came through the office. Main's treasury had traced a population spike to a single -3 district, theirs, and the inter-layer treasury levy had landed on the -3 layer treasury. The -3 treasury ran on a 6.25% tax, and the layer's average income was modest. The first $10 billion cycle all but emptied it. The notice ended with an emergency corporate tax escalation, effective immediately.

Her manager read it twice. The freight company got a tax hike and a margin call for liquidity that same morning, and so did the security firms, the extraction companies and the fabrication contractors. By the end of the week the corporations had the source district marked on a map.

Her contract came back renegotiated at terms she couldn't live on, and Tobias lost his job outright. The security firm that patrolled the district withdrew coverage from every coordinating family, and there was no other security to hire. The bank froze their credit. The power cooperative revoked its service agreement, and the freight company billed Anneke a penalty under a clause she had never read.

Prices rose across the layer, because the corporations spread their costs over every customer they had. At the fuel depot in the next district, the owner recognized her and put the nozzle back on its hook.

"My fuel's up because of you people," he said.

She drove farther each week to find someone who would sell to her. Inside a thousand kilometers she found no one.

In the autumn the freight company's owner came to the grain hall himself. Every child who came home, he said, would take that child's share off the levy. Tobias stood beside him and said nothing.

Anneke recorded her message that night and sent it to all ten over the cross-layer channel: *Come home. Your parents need you. The community is suffering.*

Pim, who cried at the end of every call, answered the next day. His advocate had talked to him first.

"It showed me our house," he said. "It said nobody guards it now. It said I didn't have to come."

He came home anyway, in the spring. He had given up $120,000 a year in untaxed UBI to do it, and every protection that came with living in Main. The other nine stayed, as most of the million did.

Within three years the coordination had collapsed. Tobias sold his truck and stopped calling meetings, and nobody asked him to start again.

The levy is smaller by Pim's share and the shares of the few others who came home, and the prices at the fuel depot have begun to ease. The levy will come down further as the children in Main settle there for good and start working and paying tax. Anneke's other nine are growing up in Main and building lives there, and the older ones are in school. She doesn't expect many of them back. When they call, Pim sits close to the screen.

Key lesson: VMSS levies the cost on the -3 treasury. The layer's own market then passes it to the families responsible.

"Then I'll sit on the Meritboard."

"Then you'll need the credentials."
