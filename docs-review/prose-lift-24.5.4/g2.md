## 1. The Last Half Point

**Whose story:** Dario Mthembu. Every tenth of a point reaches him on his own score, the refusal lands in his kitchen, and the 86.1 is waiting in his queue.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Last Half Point
Simulation Type: Resident Story · Classification: Sanctuary Phasing / STI Opacity · Doctrine Snapshot: v20.5
Outcome: Phasing at 86.1 — Reached While He Wasn't Checking
```

Dario Mthembu is a water-reclamation engineer in Main Layer, forty-one years old, STI 84.2. The woman he loves lives on the other side of a wall he is half a point too untrusted to cross.

Asha phased up to Sanctuary three years ago. She didn't campaign for it; one day the eligibility notification was simply in her queue, the way it happens. She cried, and he was happy for her with his whole chest and with something underneath it that he didn't name.

She visits every month, riding the gate transit down like it costs nothing, because for her it costs nothing. He cannot visit up. At the gate she always says the same thing.

"Don't check it tonight."

"I won't."

He watches the wall take her back, fifteen kilometers of composite blade against the sky, and goes home and checks his score.

84.1. 84.4. 84.2. He lives on the plateau for years and does everything a reasonable man would do. Part of the score is public rating, the layer's own population rating what it experiences, so he volunteers where volunteering is visible. He joins the district civic association, attends every meeting and says useful things. He mediates a neighbor dispute he would rather have walked past, is scrupulously fair, and checks afterward whether fairness moved the number. When it is settled, the older of the two neighbors shakes his hand.

"Hope that counts for something," she says.

The score breathes in tenths and goes nowhere. He cannot ask it why: the formula is proprietary, classified and dynamic, and nobody gets to know it.

So when the STI Trajectory Advisory Mandate starts gathering signatures, Dario signs it the hour he hears of it. The petition asks AI governance to tell each citizen, privately, what would actually move their own number. Millions of others camped on the same plateau sign too. It becomes the largest citizen-origination federal filing in the register's history, and it dies at the first gate, filibustered at 51 percent.

The Meritboard's published opinion lands in every signatory's feed, and he reads all of it standing in his kitchen. A formula that tells you what it wants, the opinion says, stops measuring character and starts measuring obedience. Any advisory specific enough to be useful is specific enough to reconstruct the formula. It closes on the line from Article II, quoted back at two hundred million people at once: *the formula's opacity forces genuine conduct as the only reliable strategy.* He reads it as bureaucratic cruelty.

Then his father gets sick, the long way, and the score stops existing.

Two years. He is at the hospice three nights a week, then five. The facility's water reclamation is failing and the maintenance queue is slow, so he fixes it on his own hours. The night orderly is exhausted and the orderly's kid is sharp, so on Sunday afternoons Dario teaches the kid math. He misses civic association meetings. He stops volunteering anywhere visible. He stops checking the number. There is no room.

His father dies in spring. It is three weeks before Dario thinks to look at anything in his queue that isn't condolences, and the eligibility notification is sitting there, already weeks old. 86.1. He chased that half point for six years. He laughs alone in his kitchen, one short, broken sound.

At Sanctuary orientation, a man his age asks how he finally moved his number.

"My father got sick," Dario says.

The man waits for the rest of it.

Asha meets him on the Sanctuary side of the gate. For the first time in three years, she has no transit to catch.

Key lesson: LP-058 was refused because a score that explains how to raise it ends up measuring compliance. The STI registers what a citizen's conduct is for, as well as the conduct.

## 2. The Line That Held

**Whose story:** Mara Ellison. The whole response happens to her in the underpass, the petition's one line is hers, and the missing point reaches her on the stairs from a neighbor who voted no.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Line That Held
Simulation Type: Resident Story · Classification: Post-Intervention Outcry / LP-062 Origin · Doctrine Snapshot: v20.5
Outcome: LP-062 Fails at 79% — the Coalition Redrafts
```

Every part of the system did its job. That is the part Mara Ellison cannot get past.

She was assaulted in a Main Layer transit underpass at the wrong hour, by a man whose failsafe was disabled. That was his right in the layer where the failsafe is yours to configure. The implant logged the act at neural resolution as it happened. It did not stop him, because in Main the default is post-intervention: harm may complete, and the civilization answers afterward with its full weight. It answered with its full weight. The medical drone reached her in under a minute. The nanite injectors closed the physical injuries before she had finished understanding what had happened, and the neural-therapy module began its work at the scene. Within the hour the three-axis evaluation had run: severity, pattern, and the reversibility axis, which read the harm as irreversible regardless of the body's restoration. The man was through the wall to -2 before the bruising she no longer had would have surfaced. The architecture had better evidence than her memory from the first second, and nobody asked her to testify. There was no trial.

The act still happened to her.

She had read all of it: Main as the proving ground, the layer of lived trust, where agency is preserved, including the agency to do harm. She had believed it in the abstract way a person believes the architecture of the world they were born safe inside. She did not believe it anymore.

From her district she could see the wall, fifteen kilometers of composite blade, and behind it Sanctuary, where her case is not a category that exists. There the Threshold Inhibition Protocol runs mandatory and continuous: a raised hand stops mid-motion, the muscles simply cease to answer, and the act does not complete. No citizen of Sanctuary becomes the victim of another; it is the second founding line. The civilization already knew how to make her assault impossible, and for Main it held that protection on the far side of a threshold she had not yet earned. She started attending the coalition meetings the month her therapy tapered.

The petition was not hard to surface, which surprised the upper-layer commentators and nobody in the coalition. TIP already exists in Main as an opt-in; anyone can switch on the coverage Sanctuary gets by default. Make it mandatory, the petition said, and non-disableable, for this one category: the one harm the architecture's own doctrine calls irreversible no matter how whole the body comes back. The signatures came fast.

At the third district meeting Mara stood up. "It costs nothing to anyone who would never do it," she said, and sat down again. The line ended up in half the campaign material.

The expert panel scoped the trigger carefully: overpowering force, incapacitation, refusal-then-continuation, nothing that could mistake intimacy for assault. The dual-key challenge that tried to bury it in the amendment gauntlet was denied; the petition was the layer's own to decide. It went to the ballot clean.

It failed at seventy-nine percent, one point short of the eighty it needed. The missing point came from people who could not bring themselves to make the implant a thing that overrides its bearer with no switch to turn it off. The same population had voted survivors the right to release the memory of it only months before. Mara sat with the number for a long time.

Her neighbor Signe told her on the stairs that she had voted no. "I'm sorry," Signe said. "I didn't want us turning into Sanctuary one category at a time."

Mara understood. The switch the man in the underpass had used was the switch Signe was keeping. On the worst-honest days she even agreed with a sliver of the dissent.

The coalition is already redrafting. Mara has the new text open on her table: an opt-out-able default. Every Main resident would be enrolled in the coverage at birth, and disabling it would be a deliberate, logged, friction-heavy choice instead of the silent opt-in. It would catch almost everyone the mandate would have caught and leave the switch in the bearer's hand.

Theo, who drafts for the coalition, asks whether she thinks it will pass.

"In a few years," Mara says. "It should." She turns a page. "He switched his off on purpose. Only the one we lost would have stopped him."

She has decided to spend the next decades of her long life on that difference.

Key lesson: By design, Main answers harm after the fact, including harm that cannot be reversed. In LP-062, Main's voters would not give up control of their own implants to change that.
