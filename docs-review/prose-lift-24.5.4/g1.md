## 1. The Therapist Who Descended for Research

**Whose story:** Lena Solari. The fieldwork is her idea, the patients' trauma lands on her, and the turn is the day she takes the patient's chair.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Therapist Who Descended for Research
Simulation Type: Resident Story · Classification: Extended Visitation / Professional Curiosity · Doctrine Snapshot: v17.9.8
Outcome: From Observer to Patient in -2 — The Fieldwork That Bit Back
```

Dr. Lena Solari was one of Sanctuary's sharpest trauma therapists. She had read every study, run every simulation and treated hundreds of reassigned residents through neural feeds. She knew what the lower rings looked like on paper.

She filed for an extended visitor permit to -2.

"It's the ultimate fieldwork," she told her colleagues.

"You're insane," they told her.

She smiled, packed her bags and went anyway.

She arrived in -2 with a clean record and a high STI. The first few months felt exactly as she had expected, intense but manageable. Then the real patients started showing up, people whose trauma made her Sanctuary case files look like gentle bedtime stories: enforcers who carried ghosts in their shoulders, women who had lost entire families in territorial disputes during times they had no backup vessel access.

Her own stress levels climbed fast. One evening an enforcer stayed in his chair after the session.

"People are saying the polished Sanctuary therapist is starting to fray at the edges," he said. "They're right, Doc."

By month nine she was the one booking sessions with another therapist in the district.

"What brings you in?" he asked.

Lena sat down on the other side of the couch.

Her visitor permit eventually ran out, and she returned to Sanctuary changed. She understood now why some residents never make it back alive, and she had seen the horrors on the faces of those who do.

"How was the fieldwork?" one of her colleagues asked.

"Long," Lena said.

Key lesson: A lifetime of study from above can miss what a lower layer is like. Some understanding only comes from living there.

## 2. The Right No One Mentioned

**Whose story:** Koa Maren. The block, the cough, the question and the four days are all hers, and every Article VIII fact reaches her on the day she needs it: in Wen's answers, on the clerk's screen and in her new file.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Right No One Mentioned
Simulation Type: Resident Story · Classification: Child Relocation / Organic Discovery · Doctrine Snapshot: v18.3
Outcome: Federal Extraction from -3 — Standing Right Exercised
```

Koa Maren was eleven years old and had never left Block Fourteen. She was born in -3 Terminal. Her mother had been reassigned there for second-degree murder. Her father had followed her down on voluntary permanent residency, because he loved her more than he loved continuity.

On flood days Koa took the dry corridors to Wen Garza's workshop. The private water main failed every three weeks, because the cooperative that owned it was in a territorial dispute with the cooperative that owned the pumping station. She passed adults who carried their weapons openly and others who kept them concealed, and she gave more room to the second group, who were more dangerous. Four months earlier her best friend's older brother had been stabbed over a water debt. Death was permanent here. He didn't come back, and nobody had expected him to.

Wen was sixty-three and had lived in Block Fourteen for nine years. He had come down from Main Layer after his wife died, as a voluntary permanent resident, because he'd decided he would rather live somewhere that matched the way the world felt to him. He sealed the ceiling, took the liquidation hit and converted what was left to freedom tokens at the purchasing power gradient. He didn't advertise the relocation right, and he didn't hide it either. He had built a quiet life running a small tutorial service for block kids who wanted to learn mechanical skills.

Koa had had a cough for two months. One afternoon it doubled her over a pump housing, and she put the wrench down.

"I wish I could see a doctor about this," she said.

"In Main Layer the medical infrastructure's automated," Wen said, without looking up. "It's free."

"How do you get there?"

"You apply."

"Do my parents have to agree?"

"No."

That night her mother came home from a sixteen-hour shift at the salvage processor with chemical burns on her forearms. Nobody treated them; the nearest private clinic charged more per visit than her weekly income. Her father stayed late at his small repair shop, where the block respected him for fixing things without gouging. Koa didn't mention Wen's answer to either of them. They had never told her any of it, and the block didn't discuss it.

She spent two weeks thinking about it. Then she walked to the federal services point at the edge of the district, a small, reinforced facility staffed by federal infrastructure personnel, and told the clerk she wanted to relocate to Main Layer. He confirmed her eligibility in ninety seconds and turned his screen toward her.

*Article VIII: standing right to relocate to Main Layer at any age. Parental consent not required. Federally facilitated. Full UBI. Access to autoparenting infrastructure.*

"You also have an independent AI legal advocate, assigned from birth," the clerk said. "It never initialized. The connection requires the hardware."

"I don't have an implant," Koa said. "My mother refused it when I was born."

Nobody in Block Fourteen had questioned that.

The process took four days. A temporary implant was installed at the facility for transit purposes: identity verification, a medical baseline, backup vessel initialization. For the first time in her life, Koa had continuity. The advocate activated eleven years late and began compiling her file at once. Its first page came up on the clerk's screen:

*Record: clean (clean-record doctrine). Inherited flags: none. Status: neutral.*

Her mother found out on day three. She stood in the doorway of the federal facility.

"You're going to be okay," she said.

Her father stood behind her and said nothing. Koa's new implant detected elevated stress hormones in both of them but no coercive intent, and raised no three-axis flag.

On day four she was transported through the mega-wall. She had never seen it from this close. The transit shaft was smooth, fast and silent. When the doors opened on the Main Layer side, the first thing she noticed was the air, which didn't smell like chemical runoff. The second was a small ambient glow near the shoulder of every person walking past: STI halos, visible in the AR overlay her new implant rendered by default. Greens and light blues were everywhere. She had never seen a green halo.

She was processed into an autoparenting facility in a mid-ring residential district. Her UBI activated at $10,000 a month. At eleven years old, she was wealthier than both her parents combined.

Three weeks later she sent a message to her father through the federal communication relay. It read: "The cough is gone."

Key lesson: The doctrine's strongest child protection works only once the child knows it exists. Where institutions have withdrawn furthest, discovery is organic, and some children go years without it.

## 3. The Sealed Notation

**Whose story:** Tamsin Reyes. The statute reaches her as a notification and a text, the calibration is her own choosing, and the aftermath is what she no longer recognizes.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Sealed Notation
Simulation Type: Resident Story · Classification: Survivor Election / LP-060 Aftermath · Doctrine Snapshot: v20.5
Outcome: Elective Excision in Main Layer — The Ledger Remembers So She Doesn't Have To
```

Tamsin Reyes is thirty-four, lives in a mid-ring Main Layer district, and for seven years has carried an October.

The man who gave it to her was through the wall before her bruises finished coming up. The act completed, the implant ledger read it at neural resolution, the three-axis evaluation ran, and the reassignment to -2 was processed while she was still in the medical drone's care. Nobody asked her what happened. Her memory of the worst hour of her life was never evidence, because the architecture had better evidence than she did from the first second, and the consequence was delivered without her testimony, her presence or her permission. At the time, half-sedated under a nanite injector, she took this for coldness.

VMSS never asked her to remember anything. She has read how it worked on Earth: conviction needed testimony, appeals needed re-testimony, and survivors maintained the wound for years because the justice system was load-bearing on their ability to keep remembering in front of strangers. Her memory has sat in her skull, serving no one. In seven years of therapy the neural modules took the jaggedness off the edges. The center kept its shape, a room in her house she paid for and never entered.

When the Survivor Elective Memory Excision Act clears its last gate, her advocate surfaces the notification with its usual lack of ceremony.

"It passed," the advocate says. "Statute text attached."

She reads it twice. *Victim-initiated only. Scoped to the ledger-documented event. The ledger record itself untouched. By design, excision gives the offender nothing, so no one has a reason to pressure a survivor toward it.*

"And his file?" she asks.

"Not one character of it changes, whatever you elect," the advocate says.

She files the same week.

In the deliberation window the therapeutic panel never once asks her to justify wanting it gone; the statute forbids the question. The sessions are boundary calibration. Memory is not a file with clean edges, and the work is mapping what is entangled with the event: the street, the season, a song that was playing somewhere and has ambushed her out of café speakers for seven years.

"Autumn," one of the panel says. "Keep or release?"

"Keep."

"The song."

"Keep," Tamsin says, and it surprises her. "It was mine before it was his."

She releases the hour.

The clean version of the procedure, in which she would wake with no knowledge that anything was ever removed, was struck from the law before enactment, and she is glad. A citizen must always be able to know that she chose not to know. What Tamsin keeps, permanently and by statute, is the sealed notation. The morning after, she opens her record. One entry is sealed and dated to that October, its date-range beside it, and a line underneath reads: *Summary held in escrow by your advocate. Retrievable at any time on request.*

Afterward, the world has a texture she slowly learns to read. The doctrine does not reach other minds. Her sister keeps a copy of the October that Tamsin set down, and at dinner she says a name Tamsin no longer recognizes, flinches, catches herself and changes the subject. A coworker is unaccountably gentle with her for one week every fall. Tamsin does not know what the kindness is for. She accepts it anyway.

Somewhere below the wall, a man remembers what she doesn't; his reassignment is forever, and his public ledger carries the act permanently. Tamsin walks the streets of her own district without knowing which one holds it. She takes the shortest way home.

Key lesson: Under categorical evaluation, a victim's memory serves no institutional function. That leaves it hers to keep or give up.
