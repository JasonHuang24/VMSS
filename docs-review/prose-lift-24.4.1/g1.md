## 1. The Proxy Network

**Whose story:** Maren Solke. The culture is hers, the directors' gains reach her conglomerate, and the attribution lands on her own ledger.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Proxy Network
Simulation Type: Civilizational Scenario · Classification: Network Attribution · Doctrine Snapshot: v14.7
Outcome: Conglomerate head reassigned to -1 on beneficiary pattern analysis
```

Maren Solke ran a logistics conglomerate in Main Layer, and fourteen regional directors reported to her. Over three years she had built an organizational culture in which aggressive contract enforcement was rewarded with bonuses, promotion and social access. Directors who lost market share were quietly demoted, regardless of method. She had never given an illegal order.

At the spring review Teodor Glass, who ran her eastern region, reported that Harrow Freight had taken the Ostvik contracts.

"They came in under us on every lane," he said.

"I expect results," Maren said.

He waited in case there was more. She went on to the next line of the review.

Over the next eighteen months seven of her fourteen directors picked up STI violations: for contract intimidation, for supplier coercion and, once, in Teodor's region, for a physical threat against a competing firm's logistics manager. Taken individually, each incident was a one-axis event, moderate in severity, isolated and reversible. Each director took an STI hit, and none crossed a reassignment threshold. Within weeks of every incident the gains reached her conglomerate: market share, contract victories, a competitor withdrawing. Harrow Freight pulled out of Ostvik, and Teodor got his bonus.

Maren's ledger stayed clean. She had never threatened anyone, or instructed anyone to.

The reassignment order ran to two pages. The first cited Article XVIII, network attribution. The AI governance system had run beneficiary pattern analysis across her network and found one actor, Maren Solke, consistently benefiting from harmful acts committed by seven separate associates across four districts over eighteen months. It called the correlation statistically anomalous. The second page evaluated her against the aggregate harm her network had produced:

*Severity: high, sustained economic coercion across multiple victims. Pattern: undeniable, seven directors, eighteen months, four districts. Reversibility: limited; competitors driven from markets do not recover their position. Three axes. Qualifying event. Reassignment to -1.*

She called her general counsel, Ruth Anselm, up from the floor below and opened her own implant logs on the desk screen between them. They held performance reviews, strategy meetings and compensation decisions, and nothing else.

"I never gave an order," Maren said.

Ruth read both pages. "It doesn't mention orders."

"Then what does it look at?"

"Who benefited. How consistently. Whether the correlation exceeds chance." Ruth turned back to the first page. "It does."

Teodor and the other six directors stayed in Main Layer, each with their own STI violations and a damaged trust score. Maren went to -1, permanently, and the bonuses, the promotions and the social access left Main Layer with her. The network dissolved.

Key lesson: A leader who consistently profits from harm her associates commit carries the pattern on her own ledger. No conspiracy trial or judge is needed to find it.

**Whose story:** Dav Orent. Every filing is his, every flag comes back to him, and the pattern he builds lands on his own ledger.

Dav Orent had been reassigned from Main Layer to -1 Noncompliance nine years earlier, for a pattern of financial fraud, and in -1 he rebuilt: private contracts, a crew of forty, three district-level building projects. He was a district construction magnate now, and wealthy by -1 standards.

At a contractor meeting, in front of thirty witnesses, a disgruntled worker named Asha Mirin leaned across the table and spat in his face. The ledger logged it as a minor assault: low severity, isolated, fully reversible. One axis. Asha's STI took a hit.

"It's corrective," Pell, his site manager, said that evening. "A fine, some stigma. Maybe she loses her current contract."

"She spat in my face in front of thirty people," Dav said.

In the morning he filed charges with Oren Tace, one of the district's private court operators. They made up a network of arbitrators who resolved commercial disputes for fees, and affiliated private security firms enforced their rulings. Tace had adjudicated six of Dav's commercial disputes in the past two years and depended on his continued business. He sentenced Asha to three hundred years in a private detention facility run by Dav's security affiliate.

"It meets minimum -1 standards," Tace said. "Basic rations, restricted movement, no physical harm."

Then the affiliate's duty officer called.

"It's flagged," she said. "Unenforceable."

"Flagged by what?"

"The AI's proportionality check. It runs automatically, on every consequence in -1. Private ones too."

"On a private court's sentence?"

"Three hundred years against a one-axis offense," she said. "The axis profile doesn't match."

Asha's consequence went back to the proportional one: the STI hit, the fine, and whatever the market decided about her contracts.

Dav went to a different arbitrator, who framed the charge as breach of professional conduct and gave her fifty years. That sentence came back flagged and unenforceable, like the first. The third time he filed through a commercial mediation channel and asked for her indefinite exclusion from all district construction contracts. He controlled most of the work in the district.

This time the AI read the three filings together: three attempts to impose disproportionate consequence on one person for one minor offense. The pattern axis activated on Dav's own ledger.

Six months later the notice came. His accumulated pattern of coercive private court manipulation had crossed the two-axis threshold. It went beyond the Asha case: the network attribution system now correlated a history of similar filings against contractors, suppliers and former employees.

*Severity: sustained economic coercion of multiple individuals. Pattern: documented across years.*

He faced formal multi-factor evaluation for -2 reassignment.

Key lesson: In -1 every consequence, private ones included, has to fit the offense. Pushing for more builds a pattern on the filer's own ledger.

## 3. The Revival Loop

**Whose story:** Dren Vasic. She watches the extraction, hears the loop from Kolev, and the finding lands on her: she is weighing -3 as the better place.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Revival Loop
Simulation Type: Civilizational Scenario · Classification: -2 Private Justice · Doctrine Snapshot: v14.7
Outcome: Debtor submits after nine forced revivals; his neighbor weighs -3
```

Dren Vasic lived in -2's eastern industrial corridor, inside the eleven blocks the Reth Syndicate controlled. The Syndicate ran resource distribution, private lending and territorial security for about 9,000 residents there, with an enforcement arm of forty operators. Dren paid its territorial fees and had never borrowed from it.

Her neighbor Kolev Rast had. He owed the Syndicate $340,000 in accumulated lending principal and territorial fees, and he could not pay. He had no crew affiliation, no district standing and no leverage. One night Dren watched from her window as two Syndicate operators took him from his unit. He was still talking as they walked him off. "End of the month," he kept saying. "End of the month."

Every resident of the eastern corridor knew what happened in block seven. Three years earlier the Syndicate had acquired portable backup vessel fabrication capacity in a private deal with a -2 tech broker. The units were crude, with revival fidelity of approximately 97.5% per event, lower than VMSS fabrication proxy installations, but they were mobile and private. The Syndicate did not advertise what it used them for.

Eleven days later Kolev came back down the lane pushing a Syndicate distribution cart. His hands were whole. That evening Dren took him soup.

"The basement in block seven," he said, before she could ask. "They broke both my hands the first night. When the pain got unbearable, I died. On purpose."

A bailout. Dren knew the backup vessel system should have revived him at the nearest VMSS fabrication proxy installation and reset his physical circumstances.

"Their portable unit intercepted the revival. I woke up in the same basement," Kolev said. "Same operators. Hands intact." He flexed them. "The debt was still there, so they started again. I died a second time, and a third. Same room every time."

"How many times?" Dren asked.

"Nine. Their unit fails one revival in forty. That was the only way out I had, and at three revivals a week, the odds said it would take months of sustained torture. So I stopped resisting and took their terms."

"What terms?"

"Eighteen months unpaid on their distribution network. The debt's forgiven when I finish, and I'm bound to the eleven blocks for five years." He nodded at the cart. "It's better than the basement."

In the corridor debts were paid on time, and territorial fees went uncontested. The forty operators hardly ever came down Dren's lane.

Dren had no debts and a clean -2 record. She also had no crew, no district standing and no guarantee that the Syndicate's lending terms wouldn't find her eventually. She began researching voluntary permanent residency in -3.

What drew her was one thing. In -3 death was final, and no one would own her continuity. The Syndicate couldn't follow her there; its operators would lose revival access the moment they crossed into Terminal. She went over it every night: lower UBI, no institutional floor, no revival if she was killed. No basement, and no one reviving her into the same room.

She hasn't decided yet. Most mornings she watches Kolev take the cart out.

Key lesson: -2 sets no institutional limit on nonlethal harm, and a private revival unit can make that harm indefinite. Voluntary permanent residency in -3, where death is final, exists for that reason.
