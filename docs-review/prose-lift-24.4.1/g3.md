- **Footnote boxes (new in this group).** All four cards end with a "Footnote — …" box after the Key Lesson. No earlier group had one, and the style guide doesn't cover it. Each box is kept with its original label and rewritten in plain prose: no reversals, no aphoristic ending, and hedges frozen. This could go either way. - **Word counts.** A count covers the body plus the Key Lesson (label included), excluding metadata. The footnote box is counted separately (label included). They exclude the section headers, which the rewrites drop (37, 36, 28 and 37 words).
- **Ledgers.** Each ledger is checked against the original, one row per doctrinal or factual claim. Claims from the original's lesson box start "KL:", claims from its footnote start "FN:", and row O is the Outcome line.
- **Quote marks in ledgers.** Double-quoted fragments are verbatim from the rewrite (10 words or fewer, italic asterisks ignored). Single-quoted fragments are original wording that was cut. The claim column uses no double quotes.
- **Sweep rulings.** No doctrine-sweep ruling applies to these four cards.
- **Invented names** were checked for clashes against `simulations.html`, `simulations-*.html`, the layer pages, `world.html`, `index.html`, `faq.html`, the pilot and the four 24.4.0 group files. None of them is used there. The other 24.4.1 group files did not exist when this was written, so cross-group clashes are unchecked.
- **Timeline.** All four originals give their mechanics in year bands, and every event in the rewrites stays inside the band its fact came from. ## 1. The Elected Ring

**Whose story:** Odile Ferrand, a founder who becomes Main Layer's Prime Minister. Every campaign asks her what she will change, she drafts the review boards, the lower-layer bloc outvotes her on the Federal Council, and the twenty-year border review lands on her kitchen table.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Elected Ring
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Governance Variant Stress Test · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.2
Outcome: Elected officials add discretion to a system built to eliminate it; structural erosion within two decades
```

Odile Ferrand had sat in a parliament before she emigrated, and she was one of the founders who signed the sovereign treaty that created the new civilization on a contested landmass. It opened with 380 million people, and the projections said 900 million within a century. The founders were political theorists, constitutional scholars and former parliamentarians like her, who had emigrated from democratic nations. They took the VMSS stack whole, from the implants, the STI ledger and the AI governance system to the mega-walls, the backup vessels, the kill switch and the nanobot plumes. The layers, the technology and the consequence model were identical. The charter changed one thing: each layer would elect its own government, through political parties.

Early on, the VMSS alliance admitted them as a treaty partner. The five layers mapped one to one, and when Odile first crossed into VMSS, the border read her and let her through. The VMSS official who met her on the other side, Arvo Keld, asked her why they wanted parties.

"Your Meritboard is chosen on measurable achievement, and your President is drawn from it," she said. "They answer to competence metrics. The people they govern never get a vote on them."

"And yours?"

"Ours will answer to voters. We're calling it Democratic Gradient Governance."

At home she stood for a Main Layer seat. Each layer elected its own parliament by universal suffrage, one representative for every million residents in districts drawn the way VMSS drew them, and elections came every four years. Odile's party won in Main, and the parliament's majority elected her Prime Minister, the layer's chief executive. That gave her a seat on the Federal Council, which governed cross-layer federal policy, with the Prime Ministers of the other four layers. The five of them elected a President from among themselves by majority vote, and they confirmed the Supreme Court justices the President nominated. In VMSS the justices came from the Meritboard's legal-interpretation ranking. Below the parliaments nothing had changed: the implants recorded behavioral data, the ledger tracked trust, and the AI delivered consequence automatically.

The first electoral cycle produced competent governments in every layer, run by high-caliber officials. The founding population had chosen to come, and it ran disproportionately to the motivated and the idealistic, people invested in the project's success. Sanctuary's parliament filled with philosophers, scientists and institutional designers who would have qualified for any Meritboard. Odile's benches held entrepreneurs, engineers and civic leaders. Even -1's parliament, smaller and rougher, sent representatives with genuine knowledge of the people they served. The parliaments worked on regulatory law, zoning, resource allocation, public infrastructure and cultural programming, while the AI delivered consequence without interference. Citizens moved freely between the two civilizations. Odile cited the early figures in every speech as validation, and when Arvo told her at the five-year mark that they looked like a well-run VMSS founding, she cited that too.

The second campaign brought the model's first structural tension. Her strategist, Corvin Halloran, asked what she was running on.

"It works," she said. "All of it."

"The other side is promising to change things. What are you going to change?"

"Nothing's broken."

"Then find what people are unhappy about."

Her platform was economic populism: tax adjustments, UBI supplements, PJS expansion and regulatory relief for particular industries. She won, and her parliament passed a series of industry-friendly regulations at 52%, a simple majority on party-line votes. In VMSS, citizens would have petitioned, experts would have drafted, and the population would have had to ratify at 80%. None of these regulations would have got there. The AI ignored the campaign and enforced the charter.

In Sanctuary the parties ran philosophical refinement campaigns over cultural policy, arts funding, neural diving regulation and SAD governance. The stakes were low, the voters trusted one another, and the campaigns had substance. In -1 every candidate's platform came down to the same promise, and -1's Prime Minister, Bram Ostrow, said it aloud once, at the Council table: "I will make it easier to get back to Main." His voters wanted ascent more than anything. His parties competed on promises to advocate for relaxed behavioral thresholds, faster remediation timelines and expanded pathways to Main.

"Does anyone down there run on the system working?" Odile asked him once.

"On telling voters the system works and -1 is the intended consequence of their conduct?" Ostrow said. "That loses every election."

He couldn't touch the AI's enforcement thresholds, which were charter-level. His parliament could pass regulatory law, and it did: bureaucratic friction around enforcement, review processes that delayed consequence delivery, and funding for legal advocacy programs that contested STI evaluations through every available channel.

Before the third campaign Corvin had a different question. "What have you built that a voter can point to?"

She drafted the Contextual Review Boards herself, and her parliament passed them. They were elected panels that reviewed flagged STI evaluations and sent the AI non-binding recommendations. She worded the regulation carefully, so the boards advised and never overrode, and nothing in it contradicted the charter. On the campaign stage she said, "I established a process that gives citizens a voice in the system that governs them." The hall stood for her.

The first citizen whose evaluation a board flagged went into a queue and waited while the board met. The AI read the recommendation, found it non-binding, and delivered the original consequence, later than it would have arrived without the board.

After the fourth election, -1 had chosen leniency candidates three cycles running. Its remediation programs were funded lavishly, and its legal advocacy had become an institution. Every behavioral breach drew an automatic review request, filed by the resident's own elected representative. The AI processed each one and delivered the consequence anyway, and processing time had tripled. Ostrow brought the figure to the Council.

"Tripled," he said. "My people finally feel heard."

-2's Prime Minister spoke for people reassigned for predatory violence. Her layer's representatives campaigned on survival infrastructure: medical access, economic development, personal safety inside the layer. Those were legitimate governance concerns, and -2's two parties channeled them through ideology. One argued -2 was too harsh and wanted expanded institutional presence. The other wanted -2 more autonomous, with reduced federal oversight. Odile read both platforms. Neither mentioned what -2's residents had done.

Between the fifteenth and twentieth years the Council split, and federal enforcement policy deadlocked. The Sanctuary Prime Minister's voters wanted the system preserved, and Ostrow's wanted it softened. Both had been elected and both sat at the same table, so every federal policy discussion became a negotiation between a population that had earned its position and one placed there by consequence. In VMSS, -1 petitioned like every other layer, and no elected advocate at the federal level had a career in making it more comfortable. Here, on enforcement, Ostrow and -2's Prime Minister voted as a bloc, and -3's Prime Minister, who spoke for the terminal layer, joined them when it suited him. That made three of the five votes, all from punitive layers. Odile and the Sanctuary Prime Minister were outvoted on enforcement-adjacent policy.

The bloc couldn't override the charter, which the AI enforced whatever the Council voted. It could block federal regulatory proposals, and it did. It defunded cross-layer enforcement coordination and directed federal resources to remediation infrastructure in the lower layers, which effectively subsidized consequence mitigation there with upper-layer tax revenue.

"That's Main's tax money," Odile said after one vote.

"It's federal money," Ostrow said. "We voted."

The Supreme Court stepped in twice. It struck down a -1 regulation that effectively created a shadow appeals process for layer reassignment, and it struck down a Council budget allocation that redirected enforcement funding to legal advocacy. Both rulings were correct on the charter, and both were politically catastrophic. The President had nominated those justices and the Council, Odile among its members, had confirmed them, and now they were striking down the Council's own policies. Ostrow campaigned for re-election on judicial reform.

In the twentieth year the alliance conducted its routine border calibration review, and Odile read the report at her kitchen table the night it arrived. The findings were stark. The first page said her civilization's STI distribution had shifted measurably, and the lines under it said where:

*Main Layer: average STI drifting downward. Cause: procedural delays let borderline conduct accumulate before consequence arrives. Citizens not behaving worse.*
*-1: population grown disproportionately. Remediation has made the layer more habitable and reduced the urgency of behavioral correction. Ascent not accelerated.*
*Sanctuary: population plateaued. Pathway unchanged on paper; cultural signal shifted. Same sustained non-harmful conduct required, amid comment periods, review boards and advisory recommendations in place of zero-latency consequence.*
*Border entry denials, citizens entering VMSS: 3% rising to 11% over twenty years.*

Beside each finding the report printed VMSS's own at the same twenty-year mark. VMSS showed no comparable drift. Its laws still came from petitions on genuine regulatory gaps its population had identified, ratified at 80%, and its AI delivered consequence at the same latency as on its first day. A note on the last page called her civilization's drift passive and persistent, like the Softer Ring's, and entirely produced by its own governance choices.

She called Arvo in the morning.

"What does VMSS want us to do?"

"Nothing," he said. "The thresholds are the ones you crossed the first time."

Her citizens were fed, housed, safe and governed. The layers were intact, the walls stood, the kill switch and the plumes still worked, and the AI still enforced the charter. By any Earth-historical standard it was an extraordinary success: a five-layer gradient civilization with universal abundance, functional consequence delivery and democratic representation at every level. That afternoon Corvin sent over the first draft of the next platform.

Key lesson: Elected officials need discretionary authority to justify their office, and VMSS is built to keep discretion out of consequence. Below Sanctuary, every constituency wants that discretion used for leniency.

Footnote — What the Honeymoon Proves: The first electoral cycle produced competent, high-caliber governments in every layer, so merit-based selection is not the only way to produce competent leaders. Elections can produce excellent leaders, just not consistently across generations. The first generation is self-selected for idealism, the second inherits party infrastructure, and the third inherits incumbency. The Meritboard selects on fixed criteria, measurable achievement, so it does not degrade from one generation to the next.

## 2. The Recovery Gradient

**Whose story:** Hanne Vell, reassigned to -1 for fraud in the civilization's third year. She lives by the fifteen-year clock, watches her neighbor's clock reset a month before her own review, passes, and settles in the same district as the woman she defrauded.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Recovery Gradient
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Alliance Divergence & Consequence Architecture · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.2
Outcome: Upward reassignment transforms permanent consequence into a timed sentence — the deterrent erodes, the layers destabilize, and the victims pay the cost
```

Hanne Vell was reassigned to -1 for fraud in the civilization's third year. It was an allied civilization, founded twelve years after VMSS, with 1.6 billion people in five rings, and it ran the full VMSS architecture with one structural change. The cover of her arrival packet called that change the humane completion of gradient governance: people change, and the Recovery Gradient asked them to prove it. It offered earned redemption, to a standard higher than original placement had ever required. The first page set it out. A -1 resident who kept an STI above 78 for fifteen continuous years, with zero qualifying breaches, became eligible for review for recovery to Main Layer. The AI governance system ran the review as a multi-factor evaluation: STI trajectory, behavioral consistency, peer-signal analysis, and a victim-impact assessment that gave the original victim formal input. Meeting the threshold earned a review, and the review could still deny recovery. Below that, in smaller type: *-2: STI above 72 for twenty-five years, zero qualifying breaches, eligible for review for recovery to -1. -3: terminal, no pathway.*

The man who came down on the same transport, Rudi Selk, had been reassigned for a DUI at thirty. He read the page on the platform.

"In VMSS this would be for good," he said. "Backup vessels, augmentation, you're effectively immortal. That's three hundred years for one DUI at thirty." He folded the page into his jacket. "Here it's fifteen, if you can prove it."

Every quarter the bulletin in Hanne's stairwell reported STI distributions, population flows, enforcement latency and behavioral metrics indistinguishable from VMSS's. One issue carried the alliance's notice admitting the civilization as a treaty partner with clean border protocol, and the result of VMSS's layer equivalence mapping: no calibration issues. Hanne read each issue on her way to work and saw nothing in it to suggest the deterrent had weakened. The fifteen- and twenty-five-year minimums had not run, and through the civilization's first decade the count of residents qualified for review stayed at zero.

Hanne kept her STI above 78 and her behavior impeccable by every metric. She checked her score every morning and rented her flat by the month. In fifteen years she never signed a longer lease.

The first cohort became eligible in the fifteenth year, and the results went up in the canteen. Of the roughly 3.2 million residents who had entered -1 in the civilization's first two years, about 340,000, just over 10%, had met the STI and zero-breach criteria. Of those, 220,000 passed the review. Victims had blocked about 40,000, and the other 80,000 were denied on behavioral trajectory or peer-signal grounds despite meeting the STI threshold. The 220,000 recoveries were processed over six months. The woman across Hanne's landing was one of them, and she left with one bag.

Within two years of the first recoveries, Main's qualifying breach rate rose 8%. It was no crime wave. The young man who moved into the empty flat across the landing was no hardened offender. He had run the calculation first.

"Fifteen years," he told Hanne. "Not the rest of my life."

The recoveries split -1 in two. Residents on the clock, like Hanne, kept their scores high, avoided every risk and optimized for the fifteen years. The rest had failed the criteria or never meant to pursue them, and now they lived beside neighbors with something to lose. Hanne's bicycle was stolen that spring, and the AI recorded the theft and processed it through standard enforcement like any other. A man from the ground floor took to stopping her on the stairs and raising his voice, to see whether she would raise hers in public. Later he asked to be paid to stop.

Rudi had held 79 for fourteen years and eleven months. In month 179 the same man followed him into the canteen, and Rudi turned on him, and the argument got loud enough that the canteen went quiet. It wasn't a qualifying breach, only a conflict the STI could see. Rudi's score dropped to 77, and his fifteen-year clock reset. Hanne had wanted to shout at that man for two years. She never had.

Her own fifteen years ended the next month. The review approved her. She entered Main Layer with a clean layer status and a fresh economic position: no asset restoration, a rebuild from the UBI baseline. The AI would not weight the notation on her STI ledger in any future evaluation, and the population could weight it however it chose. The notation was permanent and public, a factual record of prior -1 residency and recovery.

Work moved her twice in her first years in Main, and the second move put her in the same district as Mirela Stoica, whose savings she had taken. The AI placed no restriction on where recovered residents settled. Mirela read the notation in the market before she recognized Hanne's face.

"They wrote to me," Mirela said. "I hadn't thought about you in fifteen years. Then a letter told me you were eligible to come up to Main and asked for my input."

"I'm sorry," Hanne said.

"It was a very respectful letter." Mirela put the pears back on the stall and walked away.

In the twenty-fifth year the first -2 cohort became eligible. They had been reassigned for predatory violence: assault, sexual assault and attempted murder. -2 was populated by people whose qualifying breach had involved direct physical harm to another person's body. The review was rigorous and the standard genuinely high. Their victims opposed recovery at nearly three times the rate of victims of -1-level offenses, and the AI denied the vast majority on behavioral trajectory grounds. Fewer than 4% of the eligible cohort passed, fewer than 8,000 people in a civilization of 1.6 billion. In -2 people began to organize their lives around the twenty-five-year clock, as -1 had around the fifteen. Rudi wrote that two of the recovered had taken flats in his building. He was partway through his second fifteen years.

By the thirtieth year the re-contact notices had produced a formal protest movement in Main. Hanne met its march in her own district on the day the alliance published its thirty-year border calibration review, and the market screens carried the figures. Main's qualifying breach rate was 14% higher than VMSS's, all of it attributable to marginal deterrent erosion. -1 had split into recovery-track and non-recovery populations, with measurably different social structures and conflict patterns, and -2 had begun the same split. The civilization was measurably softer than VMSS on the one metric VMSS considers non-negotiable, the permanence of consequence for qualifying harm. Its calibration drift was slower than the Softer Ring's or the Elected Ring's, because the thresholds were demanding, but it ran in the same direction, and its border entry denial rate at VMSS had risen to 9%.

None of these costs was catastrophic. At the same mark VMSS showed none of them: no deterrent erosion, no victim re-contact, no recovery-track population and no STI gaming. There, a punitive layer's STI decided a resident's district, contracts and standing among peers but never whether they left, and a -1 resident who had lived quietly for thirty years had a home in -1.

The marchers carried a banner that read PERMANENCE IS A VICTIM'S RIGHT, NOT JUST A PUNISHMENT. Mirela was holding one end of it. Hanne waited on the curb until the march had passed, then crossed the street behind it.

Key lesson: A way back out of a punitive layer changes how its residents live there and brings their victims back into the case. A behavior score cannot certify a changed character.

Footnote — The Immortality Argument: The Recovery Gradient is the most defensible modification any allied civilization has attempted. Its thresholds are demanding, its review is rigorous, and its victim-input mechanism is structurally unprecedented. Its designers genuinely understood the architecture they were modifying, and they believed that permanence, applied to an immortal population, is a disproportionate consequence for a single qualifying act. Theirs is the one objection to permanence that carries genuine philosophical weight over centuries: with backup vessels and effective immortality, a DUI at age thirty determines a person's civilizational environment for the next three hundred years. The argument has moral weight. VMSS's counter, that the system does not guess, it knows, is mechanically sound. But whether a three-century consequence for a single qualifying act stays proportional over an immortal lifespan is a question the traditional model answers with doctrine rather than data, because it has never needed to produce data. The Recovery Gradient is the only allied nation generating that data. If its 10% recovery rate and demanding thresholds eventually produce a stable equilibrium without deterrent erosion, a possibility the thirty-year window does not confirm or exclude, the argument for permanence may need to be defended on grounds beyond "it works because we never tried the alternative."

## 3. The Drift Vessel

**Whose story:** Ysolde Kaarn, an engineer in the division that built the drift vessel. She signs the drift table, her partner is revived in Main, her brother lives through repeated revivals in -3, and the delegations and VMSS's reviewer all come to her table.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Drift Vessel
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Technological Divergence & Continuity Philosophy · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.2
Outcome: Revival never fails; continuity drift, graduated by layer, compounds with every revival
```

Ysolde Kaarn signed the calibration table the week her division announced the breakthrough. She was an engineer in the fabrication research division of a VMSS-adjacent nation, a sovereign civilization outside the alliance that ran a five-ring gradient model under its own charter. The division had built a backup vessel revival architecture that eliminated revival failure entirely. Every revival succeeded, and no death was permanent in any layer where the backup vessel link was maintained. VMSS's model was binary, full fidelity or failure. Ysolde's replaced the binary with a spectrum: the person who woke was never a perfect copy, and the continuity drift between the person who died and the person who woke could be measured. The table graduated it by layer, following the civilization's own institutional gradient:

*Sanctuary, Main: approx. 99.97% fidelity*
*-1: approx. 0.5% drift*
*-2: approx. 2% drift*
*-3: approx. 8% drift*

At the announcement the division's director set it against VMSS's numbers. "In VMSS, one revival in a thousand fails in -2," she said. "Across millions of people and centuries, that is hundreds of thousands of permanent deaths from a technological limitation rather than a policy choice. Our citizens always come back, in every layer." Drift was the cost, she said, and it was graduated: nearly imperceptible in the upper layers, where institutional investment was highest, and more pronounced in the lower layers, where the consequence architecture was designed to be harsher. The layers that had earned the most institutional trust received the most precise continuity, and the layers the civilization had withdrawn from received less fidelity. Unlike VMSS, her nation kept backup vessel infrastructure running in -3, where Ysolde's brother Tavi lived.

In Main, where Ysolde lived, the effect was almost entirely positive. Under the binary model VMSS still ran, every upper-layer revival carried a one-in-a-million chance of failing, statistically trivial each time. A citizen who expected to live for centuries would die and be revived dozens of times, and the cumulative chance became personally meaningful. Revival anxiety, the low-grade awareness that any death carried a small but non-zero chance of being final, went away. That year's surveys had upper-layer citizens reporting higher satisfaction, lower revival anxiety and a negligible difference in continuity. Her partner Soren drowned sailing in the second year and was revived at 99.97% fidelity. He remembered everything, recognized everyone and kept every skill. The division called the missing 0.03% emotional rounding: slight attenuations in how intensely particular memories felt, minor shifts in how he responded to particular things. Most revived people in the upper layers could not identify what had changed. Months later Ysolde told the story of the night they met, which had always made him laugh, and he smiled and said, "I remember." She noticed a flatness in it she could not have named.

In -1 the drift was noticeable. The division's field interviews came back with the residents' own words for it. "The first two were clean," one man said. "The third was soft. My wife says she can see the drift marks." Revived -1 residents kept their full factual memory, their skills and their identity. They lost something in personality texture: reduced emotional granularity, slight shifts in how they responded to stress, occasional gaps in contextual memory, like forgetting why they had walked into a room. It was noticeable to them and to their intimates, and it disabled no one. Each revival compounded the drift cumulatively, not additively, and a resident revived three times carried about 1.5%, measurably different in emotional texture, stress response and affective depth from the person who had entered -1. Residents called the slow attenuation across revivals thinning.

In -2 the effect was transformative. Revival there was common, because predatory violence produced high mortality, and at 2% per revival a resident revived twice carried about 4%. Factual memory stayed largely intact while emotional memory degraded meaningfully, including their emotional memory of the violence they had committed, of the relationships they had before reassignment and of who they had been on entering -2. Relationships felt thinner. People knew whom they loved and felt it less. Several reported something the researchers had not predicted, reduced remorse. Their remorse was not gone. They knew what they had done and felt less about it. The charter committee reviewed the finding and classified it as an acceptable trade-off. Under traditional technology, its ruling noted, the alternative was permanent death for 1 in 1,000 -2 revivals.

Tavi's first revival in -3 brought back a recognizably similar, emotionally attenuated version of him. His second brought the cumulative drift to about 15%, and his third to close to 22%. He still had his name, his language, his skills and every fact of his autobiography, and he had lost nearly a quarter of what the division's papers called his emotional architecture. He was not brain-damaged; his cognition tested intact. When Ysolde came through his door he looked up and knew her face, and the feeling of recognizing it did not come. He knew she was his sister. He remembered loving their mother the way he remembered her address.

After a decade -3 had a visible demographic of high-revival residents, unprecedented in the VMSS model family: people technically continuous with their original selves who were functionally different people. Their skills worked and their language was fluent. What had degraded was the connective tissue of personality, the emotional responses, affective patterns and experiential depth that made a person feel like themselves to the people around them. By then Tavi had been revived five times and was approaching 35% cumulative drift, and the people in his block called residents like him present but hollow.

The charter committee began to debate whether high-drift revival was meaningful continuity or a different form of death, one where the body survived and the person did not. The woman downstairs from Tavi had been revived once and still felt like herself, and lived like it. The man who fixed Tavi's door noticed his own changes and kept a notebook of things he used to like; some weeks it helped, and lately he got lost more often between what he remembered of himself and what he felt. Tavi was past that. He remembered being someone else and could not reach the experience of having been him.

Delegations came to the division through those years, from across the alliance and beyond. The alliance nations ran traditional backup vessels, and their evaluators did not dismiss the drift vessel; zero revival failure was a genuine advance. A delegate from one of their Sanctuary layers did the arithmetic across the table for Ysolde. He expected forty revivals over three centuries, each with a one-in-a-million chance of failing.

"That starts to be about me," he said.

For upper layers the question was whether near-zero drift was an acceptable trade for guaranteed revival, and the answer was genuinely competitive. At 0.03% drift the drift vessel removed the statistically rare but psychologically corrosive possibility of permanent death in the safest environment, with no meaningful continuity concern, and for upper layers that was the better outcome. Multiple alliance nations opened formal technology-sharing negotiations to deploy it in their upper layers while retaining binary revival for their lower layers. Below Main, their consensus ran, the calculus reversed. Binary revival with elevated failure rates preserved perfect fidelity for everyone who survived the process, and they preferred it to guaranteed survival with cumulative personality erosion, particularly in -2 and -3, where high mortality and multiple revival cycles would compound drift into a de facto identity replacement program.

The non-allied delegations came separately. Their nations had no layered consequence architecture and no punitive lower layers where drift compounded across violent deaths, so the offer was straightforward: revival that never failed, with negligible drift, at the standard of care their whole population would receive. Several opened direct bilateral negotiations with Ysolde's government, bypassing the alliance entirely. Her nation became the sole source of a capability every civilization wanted and the alliance's framework did not control, and the drift vessel became a diplomatic asset.

VMSS's Meritboard commissioned its own formal review, the most measured of any, and its reviewer spent a week in the -3 installation with Ysolde. His findings were that in Sanctuary and Main the drift vessel was superior to binary revival by every metric that mattered: zero failure, imperceptible drift, the end of cumulative revival anxiety across immortal lifespans. In -1 and -2 it was inferior by the single metric that defined those layers, the integrity of the person who came back. VMSS's foundational promise was *continuity, not innocence*, and by continuity it meant the complete person, not the survival of a body carrying their memories. On his last day he stood with Ysolde outside Tavi's block.

"We'd have no framework for him," he said. "On paper he's the same citizen."

By the twentieth year -3 had produced a generation of high-drift residents, people with five, eight, twelve revivals and cumulative drift above 40%. They were ambulatory, functional, employable and legally continuous with their original selves. They remembered their lives and did not experience them. The charter committee formally ruled that drift revival constituted meaningful continuity at every level: whoever woke, at any cumulative drift, was the same person who died. The ruling was legally necessary. Without it, the charter had no category for people who were simultaneously alive and no longer themselves. VMSS observed. Its Meritboard review concluded internally that both revival models were honest trade-offs, and VMSS made no public comment.

The ruling did not resolve what the families lived with. That year an account Tavi had written circulated widely. He had 44% cumulative drift, and he described it as "living in the memory of a life that happened to someone who was almost me." Ysolde read it on the tram.

She went down that weekend. He was glad to see her, and said so at the right moment. He poured the tea the way their father used to, holding the pot high, and she watched him remember to do it. When she mentioned Soren he asked after him by name and waited, correctly, for the answer.

"I know I've drifted," he said. "I remember being more. I can't get at what it felt like."

She drank the tea. He held the pot high again for the second cup.

Key lesson: Each revival model publishes its cost, a small chance of permanent death or a small loss with every revival. The drift cost builds up in the person who survives, so it weighs most in layers where people die often.

Footnote — The Hybrid Path: The simulation identifies the hybrid approach, drift vessels for the upper layers and binary revival for the lower, as the likely alliance consensus. VMSS itself would logically adopt the upper-layer half, replacing its one-in-a-million failure rate with zero failure at imperceptible drift. In this case the discovering nation's innovation improves the traditional model rather than competing with it.

## 4. The Glass Mind

**Whose story:** Rhea Castellan, a Sanctuary neuroscientist who voted for the recalibration. Her lab measures the chill while she feels it, her husband's compositions go flat, and she is one of the emigrants whose mind comes back in VMSS.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Glass Mind
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Cognitive Transparency & Surveillance Architecture · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.2
Outcome: Full thought transparency eliminates deception and produces a civilization that cannot think freely
```

Rhea Castellan voted for the recalibration. She was a neuroscientist in the Sanctuary layer of a VMSS-adjacent nation that ran a five-ring gradient model and had never been an alliance member, and she knew the change needed no new hardware. The implants already read cognition at the resolution required. VMSS kept what its implants read apart from what they shared, a deliberate constraint on a capability that existed: their high-resolution neural pattern scanning distinguished intent from mere thought, detected escalating harmful intent for pre-intervention in Sanctuary, and treated all other cognition as sovereign and non-public. They read everything and broadcast nothing, and no thought, fantasy, opinion or internal deliberation carried institutional consequence. Her nation's vote removed the software layer that did the separating, and with it the line VMSS considered foundational, between thought and action.

The charter committee's case had been simple. A civilization that published its architecture, its enforcement rules and its citizens' behavioral records should not stop at the skull. Deception, the committee argued, was the last category of harm the system could not detect until it became action, and full cognitive transparency would eliminate it at the source, because no one could lie.

The night it switched on, every thought became legible and every emotional state visible, and deception collapsed as the committee had anticipated. Each internal reaction, private judgment, fleeting impulse and unspoken opinion showed on the STI ledger and in the AR telemetry of anyone nearby. Rhea and her husband Jonah sat up at the kitchen table reading each other. She learned what he thought of her appearance, her habits, her family and her sexual interests, and he learned the same about her. They were still married in the morning. Her oldest friendship ended within the month, once she and her friend could each see the irritations the other had been absorbing without comment. Jonah, who composed for neural diving, settled his next contract with his studio in one sentence; each side could see the other's reservation price.

Within months crime had dropped to near zero. Crime requires concealment, and every harmful intent now showed before it became action. Her nation had what VMSS achieved only in Sanctuary through pre-intervention: harm that never completed. VMSS's pre-intervention watched for one specific neurological pattern, escalating intent crossing a threshold from ideation to execution planning. A Sanctuary resident in VMSS could fantasize about violence without consequence, because the implant distinguished fantasy from intent. Rhea's nation displayed all cognition indiscriminately, and a citizen there could not fantasize about anything without the neighbors reading it in AR.

Early in the first year Liesl, a postdoc in Rhea's lab, was presenting at the weekly meeting when Rhea felt a flash of rage at her over a slide out of order. It appeared on Liesl's AR display in real time. Liesl stopped talking.

"Four hundred milliseconds," Rhea told her afterward. "I have the trace. It was never going to become anything."

"I know," Liesl said. She sat at the other end of the table for the rest of the term.

The lab documented the change within the first year, and it accelerated through the fifth. Researchers called it cognitive chill: the measurable suppression of spontaneous, undirected and exploratory thought in a population that knew its cognition was being observed. It showed on the lab's coding sheets. At baseline every healthy subject filled the columns for things they would never say or do: intrusive thoughts, dark humor, sexual impulses about inappropriate targets, momentary rage at loved ones, petty jealousies, fantasies of cruelty. None of it was pathological. Left alone, a healthy mind was messy, contradictory and frequently antisocial, the normal output of a brain that generates options faster than it evaluates them. Character lived in the evaluation, the decision not to act, speak or pursue, and in VMSS the implant watched only what a person did with a thought. By the third year every column held less, and the subjects were producing fewer thoughts of every kind.

Rhea felt it in herself. Over thousands of micro-exposures her brain had learned that producing a thought had a social cost, and it was censoring itself at the neurological level. She thought in shorter loops, avoided extended internal deliberation, and carried a persistent low-grade awareness that dampened everything else. The spontaneous wandering that fed creativity, problem-solving, humor, empathy and self-knowledge began to attenuate.

The charter committee had not modeled what came next. Jonah's compositions stayed technically proficient and went emotionally flat. He said he could no longer reach the unselfconscious state his breakthrough work came from, and the artists he knew said the same. The messy, contradictory, socially unacceptable part of the mind that produced novel ideas was the same part that produced intrusive thoughts, dark fantasies and inappropriate impulses, and suppressing one suppressed the other. Artistic output held its volume and lost its originality. The novels Rhea read at night had turned observational, away from the imaginative.

Her own research slowed. A hypothesis starts with thinking absurd things provisionally, entertaining ideas that might be wrong and holding contradictory propositions at once, and now all of that was visible. At a seminar a colleague asked her, in front of the room, why she believed the model she had spent the morning trying on.

"I don't," Rhea said. "I was trying it."

After that she tried fewer things. Her thinking went linear and cautious and less productive, and she reached correct conclusions more slowly.

In the tenth year the lab compared the layers, and the pattern was one the charter committee had not predicted. Rhea pinned the chart over her desk:

*Sanctuary, Main: most severe. High-trust, socially integrated, densely networked. Most exposure to others' telemetry; most to lose from a thought misread by a colleague, partner or neighbor.*
*-1: moderate. Smaller population, thinner networks; peers already in a consequence environment.*
*-2: minimal. Residents reassigned for predatory violence, whose thoughts were already darker than average. Transparency showed what everyone there already assumed.*
*-3: almost no suppression.*

In VMSS the upper layers were the freest: pre-intervention kept them safe, institutional presence guaranteed abundance, and cognitive privacy preserved the inner life. Here the upper layers thought cautiously, stagnated creatively and kept their social harmony through neurological self-censorship. Nobody with social standing watched -3's displays, and its terminal population had nothing left to lose from a visible thought, so -3 was the only layer where cognition ran in its natural, unconstrained state. The -3 traces looked like Rhea's own baseline from before the switch.

The VMSS alliance's statement was unanimous: cognitive transparency was incompatible with treaty membership. The alliance framework required structural compatibility between gradient governance models, and in VMSS cognitive privacy was a charter-level right. The statement quoted Article II, *cognition is non-public and carries no penalties or gains*, and the whitepaper's rights boundaries: *a civilization that monitors conduct must be explicit about what it does not monitor, or it becomes the surveillance state it claims to replace.* Jonah read it aloud at breakfast, and Rhea saw his opinion of it on his display before he reached the end.

Several alliance nations that had kept bilateral trade and diplomatic relations with Rhea's nation suspended them. Its ambassadors carried every strategic thought, reservation price and private assessment on their AR displays, and the alliance nations refused to send diplomats into an environment where a counterpart could read their internal deliberation in real time. Nobody sanctioned or embargoed her nation. It was excluded from every interaction that required one party to think privately: diplomatic exchanges, trade negotiations, intelligence-sharing. Meanwhile several authoritarian states expressed interest in the technology. They had spent centuries trying to learn what their citizens thought through crude surveillance.

Over five years about 11% of the nation's Sanctuary-equivalent population and 6% of its Main Layer population emigrated to allied nations, primarily to VMSS. The papers compared this structural emigration with the Softer Ring's philosophical wave of 4,200. The emigrants were mostly creatives, researchers, entrepreneurs and anyone else whose professional output depended on unconstrained exploratory thinking. Their brains had stopped working properly under observation, and their cognitive function had degraded until they could not do their work, maintain their relationships or sustain their mental health in a transparent environment.

In the fifteenth year Jonah said it at dinner.

"I can't compose here."

His display said it a moment before he did. They left that spring.

At VMSS's border they went through the standard ledger audit and entered at the layer their behavioral records warranted. Most emigrants entered Main or Sanctuary; Rhea and Jonah entered Main. Within weeks the suppression began to lift. Spontaneous thought returned and creative capacity rebuilt. The recovery was reported universally, and the emigrants in their building described it consistently, as thinking in color again after years of grayscale. The chill had been an adaptive response to a surveillance environment, and without the surveillance it reversed. There was no permanent brain damage.

Behind them the recalibration worked exactly as designed. The charter committee, which had built it as a radical extension of transparency and not as an instrument of control, watched it adopted by exactly the governments it least resembled, for exactly the purposes it least intended. Back there, deception was eliminated and crime was near zero. Every citizen knew exactly what every other citizen thought, the social contract was fully transparent, and no one could lie, manipulate or conceal harmful intent. The committee's argument was internally coherent, and the implementation was technically flawless. No authority was watching; everyone watched everyone.
