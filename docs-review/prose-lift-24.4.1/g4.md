## 1. The Wider Door

**Whose story:** Marta Wessel, daughter of a woman who stayed on Earth in 2026 over permanence. She shares the objection, enters through the Recovery Gradient, moves on to VMSS, and is still alive in 2226 when the family's descendants ask the harder question.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Wider Door
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Alliance Diversity & Earth Emigration Dynamics · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.3
Outcome: Earth Emigration Rises Through the Softer Allies, and Migration Within the Alliance Trends Toward the Traditional Model
```

Marta Wessel's mother read the VMSS offer in 2026 and stayed on Earth. When Marta was nine she asked her why.

"I disagreed with permanent reassignment," Ruth said.

In VMSS's first decade there was only the one offer, and it came whole: permanent reassignment, no elections, the kill switch, cognitive privacy, merit-based governance and a 10:1 penalty asymmetry. Join or stay. Ruth's friend Carys had read all of it twice, accepted every trade-off and gone in the first year. Ruth agreed with eighty percent of the model and found twenty percent unacceptable, and that kept her home. A different twenty percent kept Marta's uncle home. "I didn't trust a system without elections," he told Marta. The woman downstairs said, "The kill switch made me uncomfortable."

The government broadcasts of those years called VMSS a single ideological experiment: one architect, one model, one philosophy, untested and extreme. Parts of that were true, and that was why it worked. There was one model, and it was untested at civilizational scale. Permanence, no elections and remote lethality could each be defended alone. Together they gave any skeptic a reason to stay, and Ruth had found hers in the first.

Ruth died at seventy-nine, a year under Earth's eighty-year lifespan ceiling, in a clinic with people sleeping in its doorway. By then Marta had spent most of her working life in a parole office under a wall chart that put the recidivism rate at sixty-five percent.

Sixty years after the Founding Treaty, Marta printed a comparison sheet from an emigrants' forum. It had fourteen columns, one for each sovereign civilization in the alliance, spread over four continents. VMSS was the largest at 4.3 billion. The founding ally ran four rings for 1.8 billion. The other twelve ranged from 200 million to 1.6 billion, on four, five or six rings. The total at the bottom was about 14 billion, roughly 40% of Earth's total human population.

The top rows read the same in every column: layered environments, behavioral consequence, AI governance, implant infrastructure, backup vessels, UBI, a charter-based constitutional framework. Below them no two columns matched. Marta read across looking for permanence. Her eye went past a nation that elected its leadership and one that had removed the kill switch and the nanobot plumes from its military doctrine. It went past six rings, with an achievement layer and a contribution layer as two distinct upper tiers above Main, and past backup vessels carried into a terminal layer with full revival. It stopped at the Recovery Gradient, which had replaced permanent reassignment with a measured recovery pathway, demanding but real. The last line bound all fourteen by treaty to mutual defense and free citizen movement.

Her brother Anselm read the sheet upside down across his kitchen table.

"It's one man's ideology," he said. The government had been saying it since Marta was a child.

Marta turned the sheet toward him. It held fourteen charters from fourteen independent founding populations, and above the enforcement layer all fourteen governance structures differed.

"Permanent reassignment is inhumane."

She put a finger on the Recovery Gradient.

"No democratic accountability, then."

She moved it to the Elected Ring, where elected parliaments sat above the AI enforcement layer.

"Remote lethality is authoritarian."

The Softer Ring had constitutionally removed the kill switch. Anselm left the sheet where it lay.

In the next room his daughter Pia had the evening feed on. She had grown up watching the alliance's civilizations, fourteen of them now, outperform Earth on lifespan, safety, medical access and material abundance at once. The emigration figures ran along the bottom of the screen: about 18 million people a year had left Earth for gradient nations in the decade when VMSS and two early allies were the only choices, and about 31 million a year in the decade since the alliance reached fourteen members with meaningfully diverse models. Almost all of the increase, the anchor said, came from holdouts with one specific objection, people who wanted gradient governance calibrated to the harshness they could accept. Then the feed ran a quiz, *Which gradient nation matches your values?* It had fourteen possible results and no option to stay. Pia took it twice.

Marta had VMSS's consent form open on her own screen that night. It still said *permanent layer reassignment with no appeal*. She closed it and applied to the Recovery Gradient.

She lived there ten years. She took the implant and drew the UBI floor, and her STI ledger filled with a decade of her conduct. She learned the rings and what sent people down them. The evening reports there described a -1 split in two: residents on the recovery track, gaming their STI to reach review, and residents who had given up on it. In her fourth year a notice reached Lio, across the hall, with the first word she'd had in years of the man who had defrauded her. He was eligible for recovery review, and she could give input. Lio looked him up. His STI ledger had nothing on it but good marks.

"He was spotless while he was stealing from me," she said, and put the notice in a drawer.

The Recovery Gradient's published results showed the deterrent eroding, with a breach rate measurably higher than VMSS's. Hanne, who had come over on the same crossing, read them and stayed.

"It's the right balance for me," she said.

In Marta's tenth winter the alliance published its internal migration tables, and they pointed the same way they had every decade. About 8% of the Earth emigrants who had entered through the softer nations moved on to VMSS within their first fifteen years. The Elected Ring was losing about 3% of its upper-layer population a decade to other alliance nations, disproportionately to VMSS and the founding ally. The Recovery Gradient lost about 2% a decade from its Sanctuary equivalent, the Softer Ring about 1.5%.

Marta applied to VMSS that spring, for sharper consequence and higher precision, with no discretionary padding. In ten years VMSS had never sent her a word, and every comparison she had read came from somewhere else. At its border she brought a decade of alliance-standard behavioral data, and the ledger audit went through all of it. The officer scrolled without looking up.

"Alliance transfers run better trajectories than people straight from Earth," he said. "Steadier, too."

The audit placed her in Main, the layer her conduct warranted. He slid the consent form across. It was the form she had closed on Earth, word for word, and the thresholds behind it were the ones Ruth had read.

"You understand the reassignment clause," he said.

"Permanent, with no appeal," Marta said, and signed.

By 2226, two centuries after the Founding Treaty, Marta still lived in Main, and a message reached her from Earth. Sefa, one of Anselm's descendants, was building the family record. No Earth lifespan ran the two centuries between Ruth's choice and Sefa's question, and Ruth had not lived to be asked.

*Ruth's answer is in the family file: she disagreed with permanent reassignment. But there were fourteen of them. One had elections. One had non-permanent reassignment. One removed the kill switch. One had six rings with finer gradation. One had full revival in every layer. She disagreed with all fourteen? Whatever she objected to, one of them had answered it at civilizational scale and published the results. I know what was out there. I'm asking anyway. Why didn't she go?*

Marta still had the comparison sheet in a drawer, soft at the folds. She took it out and smoothed it flat on the table before she began to write.

Key lesson: A family of allied variants draws emigrants whom a single strict model does not reach. Some of them move on to VMSS after living under a softer one.

## 3. The Common Coin

**Whose story:** Teodor Vance, a Main Layer furniture maker. He earns his fortune on -2 labor through the shared currency, and when he is reassigned to -1 the same currency carries his money down after him.

**Rewrite**

```text
The Common Coin
Simulation Type: Civilizational · Classification: Economic Architecture Divergence · Doctrine Snapshot: v16.3
Outcome: A Single Currency Lets Main Profit From Lower-Layer Labor and Lets Wealth Soften Reassignment
```

In the Common Coin nation's second year, Teodor Vance, a Main Layer furniture maker, bought a load of timber in -2 for a fraction of its Main price and paid with the same money he spent at home.

The nation was a five-ring ally that had adopted the full VMSS architecture with one economic modification. Its founders' argument was egalitarian: currency siloing created economic castes. Under siloing, a -1 resident's savings couldn't be used in Main, and a Main visitor to -1 arrived economically neutral and had to earn local currency from scratch. The founders saw that as punishment beyond the layer assignment itself, an economic wall stacked on the physical one, so their charter set a single currency, earned, spent, saved and transferred across all five layers without conversion. UBI was still graduated and taxation still layered, and asset liquidation still applied on punitive descent.

That winter Teodor wrote his plan on one page. Main had full institutional presence: regulated markets, high taxation, comprehensive public services, and labor priced by people with a $10,000-a-month UBI floor. -1 had partial institutional presence, lighter regulation, lower taxes and a $5,000 floor. -2 had minimal institutional oversight, a $2,500 floor, and people whose economic behavior the harshness of their environment had shaped. The same currency bought at three price levels. Labor cost less in -2 than in Main, because the floor was lower and people were more desperate, and materials made in -2 cost less because the regulatory burden was lighter. A chair made in -2 and sold in Main would carry both savings.

He rented a workshop in -2, sent money down the shafts for a crew at -2 wages and -2 timber, and sold the chairs that came up in Main at Main prices. His margin was the gap between people earning $2,500 a month and people earning $10,000.

His adviser, Benedek, had grown up in VMSS. "You couldn't do this there," he said over the first quarter's figures. "Main money is no good in -2."

By the tenth year the Main feeds were running profiles of residents who had built fortunes on lower-layer labor arbitrage without breaking a law, and one of them was Teodor.

Benedek spent those years spreading Teodor's money out: a trust, the business signed over to his daughter Juno, stakes in cross-layer ventures, all in the one currency.

"If anything ever happens," Benedek said, "they can only take what they can identify."

In the twelfth year Teodor drove home drunk and was reassigned to -1. The standard liquidation took his accounts, his house and everything else in his name. It could not fully unwind what Benedek had spread across entities and relationships. Within a month Juno was sending money down every week, and the trust's income was reaching him through indirect channels.

Benedek called at the end of the month.

"In VMSS they'd have taken a hundred percent," he said. "You'd have landed with nothing, in a currency you'd never held, and learned the economy from zero. Nothing from Main would have converted."

Teodor took the top floor of a building on Fenn Street in -1. Across the road Aurek, reassigned the same year for fraud, lived on the $5,000 UBI in two rooms over a laundry. He had come down with nothing behind him. More residents like Teodor arrived every year, and -1 had an economic elite of its own. Aurek carried his shopping home from the market. Teodor's was delivered.

By the fifteenth year the charter committee had named what it was seeing: pre-VMSS Earth colonial economics. Its report described a wealthy layer that took labor and materials from a poor one, sold finished goods back to it at premium prices and kept the difference as profit. The founders, it noted, had set out only to remove what they saw as unnecessary economic segregation, and siloing existed to stop layer stratification from turning into economic colonialism. Teodor read it on Fenn Street and sent it to Benedek without comment.

By the twentieth year the lower layers ran as extraction economies. In -2, the labor force, raw materials and lighter regulation had drawn sustained capital investment from Main entities, all of it legal across the shared currency. Rafe's crew in Teodor's old workshop, now in Juno's name, earned -2 wages in a Main-owned enterprise. No one coerced them, and the workshop ran efficiently. The profits went up the shafts in the same currency and stayed in Main, and the crew stayed in -2. That spring Rafe bought one of the crew's chairs for his mother, at the Main price.

The committee took up reimposing siloing that year. The businesses it would disrupt came to about 12% of Main Layer's GDP, Juno's workshop among them. Juno called the hearings a witch hunt. The political will for reimposition did not materialize.

Teodor watched the last hearing from his top floor on Fenn Street, with Juno's transfer for the week already in his account.

Key lesson: Separate currencies keep a richer layer from profiting on a poorer layer's cheaper labor. They also keep wealth from softening a punitive reassignment.
