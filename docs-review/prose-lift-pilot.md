## 1. Where the Maps End

**Whose story:** Calla Dren. Who private order protects is settled on her ledger column and her nine days, and the old man is seen through her.

**Rewrite**

```text
Where the Maps End
Simulation Type: Resident Story · Classification: -3 Outskirts / Kidnapping & Private Order · Doctrine Snapshot: v20.5
Outcome: Ransomed in Nine Days by Her District Association
```

Calla Dren chose -3 the way some people choose the sea. She filed for voluntary permanent residency at twenty-nine, took the liquidation hit, and spent the next eleven years building a salvage-materials business in one of the eastern voluntary districts, the kind of place upper-layer documentaries call surprisingly orderly. Her district had paved roads, a market association, solar cooperatives that billed on time, and a perimeter walked by private security on contract. Four established members of the association vouched for her. Her contract-fulfillment ratio was published, and it was excellent.

The patrol contracts ended at the boundary markers, and so did the maps. Calla had been past the markers a dozen times on day trips, armed and careful, and somewhere in those eleven years she had stopped noticing when she crossed them.

The site was a pre-reassignment chemical works two ridges beyond the perimeter: condenser coil and tantalum fittings, a haul that would pay for a quarter if she reached it before the scrap crews did. She stayed too long in the cracking shed weighing fittings. When she came out into the late light, five people stood between her and her vehicle.

None of the five had bothered to cover their faces. No drone was coming; the layer ran no monitoring. Her sidearm was on her hip. In -3 a death was final, hers or theirs, and she left it where it was.

They did not kill her. Within the hour their leader, a flat-eyed woman the others called Sorrel, was checking Calla's name against the regional reputation ledgers. Calla watched her read. Partway down the second screen, Sorrel's shoulders came down.

"Four guarantors," Sorrel said. "Eleven years of trade. Member in good standing." She turned the handset around so Calla could see her own column of numbers, the one that decided whether the market would sell to her. "You'll be fine."

"You don't know that."

"I know your association." Sorrel was already drafting the note. "They'll pay. They can't have the market hearing they didn't."

The note went out that evening to the district association, payable in freedom tokens.

The crew was eight people living in the shell of a pumping station. The two who cooked still argued about stall prices like traders; their voluntary districts had thrown them out. A heavyset man on the night watch had come down on a -3 ledger, and so had the two who played cards with him. Nobody asked them what for. The other three had no records anywhere, and never talked about it.

A man was chained at the far end of the pump hall. He was sixty-some, with no implant, a punitive arrival from years back. He hauled water and fed the generator, and as near as Calla could tell he had been doing it for two years. He slept eight meters from her. That first night she asked him whether they had ever sent a note for him.

"Who to?" he said.

"I'm owed money, though," he said after a while. "Federal. Goes into an account every month."

"Where do you draw it?"

"Distribution point." He lifted the chain an inch off the concrete and let it drop. "Two ridges that way."

She lay awake a long time. Her association's budget had a line headed *Persistent threats*. It paid bounties to the retrieval contractors, and for crews that graduated from robbery to killing district members it paid for places in the private supermax the eastern associations kept between them. She wondered whether Sorrel had ever seen it.

They fed her, mostly. On the second night she tried the door, and a crewman hit her once across the face before Sorrel said something short to him. Nobody touched her again.

On the ninth morning they walked her to a dry creek crossing. Two association security vehicles were parked on the far bank. A contracted negotiator came halfway across and read the token transfer aloud, figure by figure, until Sorrel nodded.

"Tell your association we fed you," Sorrel said.

Calla walked the last forty meters herself. Her legs held until she was inside the vehicle.

Nobody was arrested; past the markers, nobody could be. The association logged the crew, raised the outskirts advisory rating for the eastern ridges, and added nine percent to her recovery cost as a member-services levy, paid off over four quarters. The market reopened to her that same week. Her vouchers held.

She went back for the old man. It took her a season to talk the association into it.

"There's no levy line for this," the officer in member services kept telling her. "Who's the member? Who's the counterparty?"

In the end she funded the retrieval contract herself, out of the profit the tantalum job was supposed to bring.

The contractors found the pumping station empty. The crew had moved on the week after the exchange and taken their labor with them. The contract came back with a report and a refund of the unused completion fee.

She still works the salvage trade, and she doesn't cross the markers anymore. New arrivals at the market talk about the ridges the way she used to. When they ask whether she regrets coming down, she says no. Then she unfolds the map and shows them where the patrol contracts end.

Key lesson: In the -3 outskirts, protection from other people is private and commercial. It reaches only those someone will pay to get back.

## 2. Maren Solvik

**Whose story:** Maren Solvik. The new medium starts as her complaint about what a recording leaves out, so it is told through her ear and her working choices.

**Rewrite**

```text
Maren Solvik
Simulation Type: Lifestyle · Classification: Sensory Composer · Doctrine Snapshot: v15.6
Outcome: Main Layer — Founder of Sensory Composition
```

Maren Solvik was a working pianist before VMSS: session recordings, accompaniment gigs, two decades behind instruments in rooms where someone else was the reason the audience had come. She entered Main Layer at forty-six and took the implant expecting continuity insurance and the neural diving preview she had read about in the entry materials, and not much else.

The preview was another person's memory of hearing a symphony. She sat through it with her eyes closed, following the emotional arc and the orchestration, aware of the audience around her. When it ended, the technician asked what she thought.

"Where's the rest of it?"

"The rest of what?"

"I couldn't taste anything. I couldn't smell the hall. I had no idea where my own hands were."

"It's a memory of a concert," he said. "Sight and sound. That's what people keep."

"So it's a field recording." She stood up. "A good one."

She walked out and spent the next six hours writing notes on what a composition would feel like if it used everything.

That week she went through ImmersionTube looking for anyone composing that way. The platform could carry every sense at once, and it was full of recordings: a chef's afternoon, a mountain climb, a surgery seen from the surgeon's side of the table. She couldn't find one piece that somebody had written.

Her first piece took eleven months. She built it in a neural diving composition suite, a layer at a time, the way a recording engineer builds a track. First the arc: warmth building slowly over four minutes, a plateau of contentment, a sharp spike of vertigo at the six-minute mark, then a slow descent into calm. Taste came next: honey for the warmth, copper at the spike, clean water for the calm. For smell she used cedar on the plateau and rain just before the vertigo, and nothing during the spike itself, because she wanted the copper to carry that moment alone. The body went in last. You rise through the warmth, the ground goes out from under you at the vertigo, and at the end your weight settles back in.

The piece had nothing to see and nothing to hear. It ran fourteen minutes, felt from inside the implant with the eyes closed. She called it *Displacement* and uploaded it to ImmersionTube expecting a few hundred downloads from the experimental art community.

Two weeks later the counter said four million. Messages came from a crane operator, a woman who ran a noodle stall, a retired nurse who wrote that she had never sat through a whole piece of music in her life. One had been forwarded so many times it reached her with a long tail of names on it:

*I've been to this place. I know it doesn't exist, I looked. But I can tell you what it was like to stand there: rain coming, and then the floor went.*

An interviewer asked her what she would call the thing she had made. "I don't have a word for it yet," Maren said. "I'll find one when there are more of them."

She is sixty-three now, age-pinned at forty-two, the age she felt most physically alert. She has published thirty-one compositions. Her AGI assistant sorts the collaboration requests that come in every day from other sensory composers. Three of her former students have published work she considers better than her own early pieces.

She teaches the Sanctuary Academy of Sensory Arts master class from her studio in Main Layer, and twice a week her Sanctuary students come down to her. Her STI clears the Sanctuary threshold, so she could move up whenever she chose. She stays in Main. One afternoon a student came in late and rattled, complaining about the tram: a man shouting at the driver, a child crying the whole way, somebody's elbow in her ribs.

"I ride that tram on purpose," Maren said.

Her latest piece took three composers. They merged their neural diving sessions through collaborative consciousness and built it together. It runs forty minutes and has no title yet. One of the early testers wrote that it was the most complex thing she had been inside on the platform.

Partway through there is a smell of burnt sugar. Maren didn't put it there, and neither of the other two remembers choosing it.

After the first playback she asked the other two to leave it in.

She worked alone for seventeen years before this. She has played that passage more often than anything she made by herself.

Key lesson: Neural diving was built for empathy training and therapy. Sensory composition runs on the same infrastructure.

They decided to keep the eight billion out and hope it was enough.

On day ninety-one it crossed $100 billion anyway.

Ines came that week to sign the finished piece. Standing under it, she told Petra it was the best-paid job of her working life and that she had put most of it into savings. Petra's maintenance contractor had done the same.

The cycle activated, and the coalition's remaining savings, Petra's among them, were garnished at 10%. That evening she ran the figures for all forty-three. The ninety days of spending had cost them more than the garnishing would have.

The next quarter, three members left the call, and nobody argued with them. By month six nobody was scheduling Thursdays, and the network had dissolved. The installation is still in Petra's atrium. She has grown fond of it.

Key lesson: Holding a 90-day rolling average down means spending the money for ninety days, which is what the mandate wants. The system never has to detect the cartel.
