**Conventions (same as the pilot and the 24.4.x / 24.5.0 groups)**
- **Metadata blocks.** Title, Type, Classification and Snapshot are unchanged. All three Outcome lines are unchanged too: each already names the layer result, and the Full Spectrum cards share one 'placement — phrase' pattern (see the Cobain flags for the one that could go either way).
- **Word counts.** A count covers the body plus the Key Lesson (label included), excluding metadata. A word is any whitespace-separated token that contains a letter, digit or `$`. The originals were counted from `simulations.html` the same way.
- **Ledgers.** One row per doctrinal or factual claim in the original, in the original's order. Claims from the original's lesson line start `KL:`, and row O is the Outcome line.
- **Quote marks in ledgers.** Double-quoted fragments are verbatim from the rewrite, metadata block included (10 words or fewer, italic asterisks ignored). Single-quoted fragments are original wording that was cut or changed. The claim column uses no double quotes. In the flags, too, double quotes hold rewrite wording only.
- **Real people.** Kurt Cobain is the only real person in this group. He is never given a direct quote: the original's spoken lines ('He said: yes. Then he said: that's partly the point.') and his archive sentence are reported indirectly. Only the invented minor characters (Brisa Tamm, Orsolya Lindahl, the screener) speak in quotation marks. No real-world claim is added; "his Earth work" is the original's only reference to his life before VMSS.
- **Sweep rulings.** DS-38 and DS-42 (Kurt Cobain) are applied; see his flags. Neither Elias Varro nor Seren Okafor carries a score band, a floor for staying in Main, or an ascension review, application or consultation.
- **Charter check.** Every VMSS-side statement was read against the current Charter. One non-numeric conflict outside the sweep was found and fixed (Seren Okafor, Art. III.IV currency transfer). One item is flagged without a fix (Elias Varro, a Meritboard review of a VPR application). **Holds: none.**
- **Invented names** (Brisa Tamm, Orsolya Lindahl, Corwin Holloran, Hesper Kett, Noor Saleh) were checked against every `.html` page at the repo root and every `docs-review` `.md` file, `.claude/worktrees` excluded. Other 24.5.1 group files did not exist when this was written, so cross-group clashes are unchecked.

## 1. Kurt Cobain

**Whose story:** Kurt Cobain. The intake note, the three months in Sanctuary and the screening question all land on him, and Brisa Tamm, Orsolya Lindahl and the screener are invented minor characters around him.

**Rewrite**

```text
Kurt Cobain
Simulation Type: Full Spectrum · Classification: Philosophical Departure · Doctrine Snapshot: v9.0
Outcome: -3 Terminal (Voluntary) — Creative Necessity
```

Kurt Cobain was placed in Main Layer without deliberation; his behavioral profile was not complex. His STI intake score was 74. The one unusual thing in his file was a line in the psychological screening summary, added by a human assessor rather than the automated evaluation: *this subject's relationship with safety may be fundamentally different from the civilizational norm. Monitor for voluntary permanent residency inquiry.* The note was logged and filed, and the system did nothing with it.

He made no music in his first months. The log recorded that without interpretation, and it raised no flag; many artists need time to orient. By month four he was working in the neural diving infrastructure, building sonic architectures in audience mode. The work was technically accomplished, and the cultural record noted it with interest. It was not what the people who remembered his Earth work were waiting for.

Brisa Tamm, who ran his dive sessions, was one of those people. After a playback she sat with her hand flat on the console.

"It's beautiful," she said. "It's just not..."

She couldn't say what it wasn't. He didn't say it for her.

His STI climbed to 81 by month six and kept climbing. There was nothing to apply for. By month ten it had crossed 85, the Sanctuary phasing floor, and he phased up to +1.

He spent three months in Sanctuary with the Threshold Inhibition Protocol active around him continuously. The pre-intervention safety he had heard described was exactly what people said it would be: absolute. No ambient vigilance was required of him, and nothing that came next was ever genuinely uncertain. Within a week he had stopped watching doorways.

He made nothing there. He had the most technically advanced creative infrastructure the civilization offers, and he was surrounded by people whose work he genuinely admired. One of them, a composer named Orsolya Lindahl, looked into his studio near the end of the second month.

"Anything?" she asked.

He told her no.

After three months he went back down to Main. In his personal archive he wrote one sentence: that the music he made required a room where something could go wrong.

He filed for voluntary permanent residency in month fifteen. The psychological screening took four sessions rather than the standard two, because the screener wanted to be certain he understood what he was choosing. He did. His understanding was unusually precise.

"Do you fully understand that death in -3 would be permanent?" the screener asked.

He said yes. Then he said that was partly the point.

The screener logged the response without comment. The application was approved, his assets were liquidated per the voluntary permanent residency schedule, and the retained portion was converted to -3 currency. He departed Main Layer on a Thursday morning with no ceremony. The moment he crossed the terminal boundary, his implant severed his backup vessel link, as it does without exception for every resident regardless of how they arrived. He had known that when he filed.

The Freedom Layer was real and unmanaged in ways the upper layers are not. Within eighteen months of arrival he had made the most significant work of his VMSS life, and this time the cultural record used words like essential and irreplaceable.

His ceiling sealed when he filed, and nothing he makes in -3 will reopen it. In the layer's context his STI is not a meaningful metric; -3 has its own standards. He has not looked at it. He is working.

Key lesson: Some artists cannot do their best work where safety is guaranteed by design. The civilization lets them choose -3, where death is final.

## 2. Elias Varro

**Whose story:** Elias Varro. The filing, the first year's unpaid work and the default in year seven are his, and the clients who come after the default reach him through Hesper Kett.

**Rewrite**

```text
Elias Varro
Simulation Type: Full Spectrum · Classification: Voluntary Libertarian · Doctrine Snapshot: v10.1
Outcome: -3 Terminal — Wealthy Self-Made Life in the Freedom Layer
```

Most people in -3 had arrived through consequence, and they assumed Elias Varro had too. He told them otherwise before they could ask.

The distinction mattered to him. -3 is sparing with admiration, but once people knew he had assessed the situation and chosen it, they watched him with the particular attention that precedes respect in frontier environments. "I filed," he would say. "Nobody sent me."

He was thirty-four when he filed for voluntary permanent residency from Main Layer, after eighteen months of research. His notes kept coming back to what -3 offered in a combination no other layer did: 6.25% top-marginal taxation, no regulatory infrastructure, no institutional interference in economic activity. The only constraints on what he could build would be his own capability and the informal social order that had emerged to fill the institutional void.

The Meritboard review took five days. Punitive reassignments are immediate, but voluntary permanent residency in -3 triggers additional psychological screening. He passed in two sessions. The examiner noted in the file that his understanding of the -3 environment was unusually detailed for a voluntary applicant.

He had structured his affairs carefully. The liquidation left him a retained portion of his assets, converted to -3 currency at filing, and the compound he had identified and purchased through a -3 intermediary was waiting for him when he landed. He had pre-positioned himself with twenty-four months to spare before the filing date, so no question of asset shielding arose under the pre-positioning lookback the Central Banking Authority administers under LP-076.

He spent his first year building relationships rather than revenue, because -3 runs on personal trust in the absence of institutional enforcement. He attended every local market and paid every obligation early. He made himself useful to three established operators, in ways that cost him time and resources and returned him nothing immediately visible except, slowly, a name as someone whose word held.

By his second year he had identified the gap that would become his primary enterprise. -3 had no reliable financial infrastructure: no lending, no credit, no mechanism for capital allocation beyond personal relationships. One of the three operators, Corwin Holloran, ran a fabrication shop with a waiting list.

"I could fill a second shop," Corwin said, "if I could pay for one."

Elias built a private lending operation, a capital allocation business that ran entirely on reputation and contract. He lent to operators he had assessed personally, at rates that reflected the genuine risk of operating without institutional backstop, on terms negotiated directly. By year four it was the most significant source of private capital in two districts. Businesses that could not have grown without access to capital were growing, and his returns were substantial.

By year five the compound had expanded into what would register as a small estate by any civilizational standard: discreet from the outside, because -3 rewards discretion, and genuinely comfortable within. He kept a private chef. His vehicle collection included two Lamborghinis, which he drove on the private roads of the gated district that had developed organically around the voluntary residents and successful operators who had gravitated toward the same area. He had been one of its early architects without intending to be.

In his seventh year a voluntary resident he had lent to defaulted significantly and left the district. The loss was material and public. Elias absorbed it without aggressive pursuit. He documented the default clearly and made the documentation available to anyone who asked.

Within three months two new clients approached him specifically because of how he had handled it. They had been watching to see whether he would respond with intimidation economics. The first, a glassworks owner named Hesper Kett, had read the documentation twice.

"You didn't send anyone after him," she said.

"No."

She put her own books on his table.

He is fifty-one now. The operation has expanded into four districts, and he employs 28 people directly, most on 20-hour qualifying schedules that unlock the primary job subsidy and leave them genuine discretionary time. His ceiling sealed the day he filed, and nothing he builds here reopens it. He finds the question uninteresting.

He built his life here, among the relationships that matter to him, in an economy suited to his specific combination of risk tolerance, institutional skepticism and capital allocation instinct. That economy exists nowhere else in quite the same form. He chose this, and it turned out to be the right choice. On Sunday mornings, when the private roads are quiet, he takes one of the Lamborghinis out.

Key lesson: -3 was built as the terminal layer of consequence, and a meaningful number of its residents chose it deliberately. People who were always going to leave institutional authority behind one way or another can build a life there worth having.

Seren Okafor had lived in +1 Sanctuary for eleven years, and her STI held at 91. She was a materials researcher, one of the roles that qualifies cleanly for the Primary Job Subsidy in Sanctuary, where the definition of critical infrastructure includes the science that keeps the civilization's foundational technologies advancing. She qualified for three SADs simultaneously. On a Tuesday afternoon she decided to visit -3, and told no one.

The decision was not impulsive. For six months, as part of a broader research interest in how environments shape cognitive and behavioral patterns, she had been reading the recovered implant logs from -3 reassignment transitions. They described an environment she could not model from the inside. Sanctuary had given her eleven years of pre-intervention safety, post-scarcity abundance, and social interactions calibrated to the highest trust density in the civilization. She understood -3 the way she understood any system she had studied, accurately and comprehensively, and entirely without the felt knowledge of what it was like to stand inside it. She wanted the felt knowledge. She filed the visitation request.

She had read the doctrine on cross-layer economics carefully before filing. She knew her Sanctuary currency would be inert, and she had transferred none of it, so she crossed the boundary economically neutral.

At the first gated district she came to, a private security operator read her public ledger off his screen.

"Sanctuary. Ninety-one." He looked up. "So you're no threat, and nobody sent you down. What are you paying with?"

She had nothing to pay with.

She would have to earn local currency through work or receive it from someone who had it. She wrote to the two voluntary residents whose testimony she had read extensively in the logs. One of them, Noor Saleh, wrote back.

Noor put her in touch with a construction operation in one of the voluntary community's better-maintained districts, and Seren spent her first two weeks on a materials assessment contract. She identified stress tolerances in composite materials for private infrastructure projects, work closely adjacent to her Sanctuary research and well within her capability. By the local economy's standards she was a skilled worker with rare expertise, in an environment starved of it. Within the first week she had earned enough in -3 currency to cover her accommodation and basic needs.

In her third week a dispute broke out in the district over access to a water recycling facility. It was a territorial negotiation between two crews, and the voluntary community's private security was managing it without intervention from either side. In Sanctuary the institutional infrastructure resolves resource disputes before they reach territorial negotiation. She watched from a distance for two hours. The resolution was organic and slow, and it ran entirely on the credibility of the parties involved.

Near the end of the second hour the security lead looked from one crew head to the other.

"That's it, then?"

Both nodded. No one had been physically harmed. Sanctuary's formal dispute resolution system would never have produced a process like it, and it had worked.

That evening she wrote eleven pages of notes.

She stayed six weeks rather than the two she had planned. On the day she crossed back, her Sanctuary currency reactivated and her implant reconnected to the full institutional network. The ambient awareness of pre-intervention infrastructure, the background knowledge that nothing can complete here, returned immediately. She noticed it as a physical sensation before she had fully processed it as a thought. She was safe in the specific way Sanctuary makes its residents safe, and she found, to her own surprise, that she had not missed it.

Fourteen months after the visit she published a research paper on cross-layer environmental cognition. In the introduction she acknowledged that she could not have written it from inside Sanctuary alone: the essential data came from six weeks spent economically neutral, in a layer where her status meant nothing and her work was the only currency that transferred. It was one of the more cited works in the Sanctuary academic record that year.

She returned to -3 twice more in the following three years. Both times she went back to work.

Key lesson: A Sanctuary resident who brings no money into -3 has only their skills to trade. That levelling is one of the few things the upper layers structurally cannot provide, and for some residents it turns out to be what their work required.
