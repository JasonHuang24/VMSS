Recurring habits

1. Gloss after the beat. All four. A gesture, a number, or a reply lands, and the next sentence says what it meant. This is the habit that most hurts readability: it stalls the moment the reader has already understood.
2. Thesis speech and staged quotations. Pieces 1, 2, and 3. Kidnappers, artists, reviewers, and crew leaders talk in lines that could be moved into the key lesson. Piece 4 does not do this.
3. Lectures and matched catalogues where a scene should keep moving. Pieces 1, 2, and 3. The sense-by-sense grid, the one-law essay, the three depths of road, and the bailout manual mid-grab are the costly ones. Piece 1's crew census is a lighter pass. Piece 4's figures belong to a page the characters are holding.
4. Endings that retie the theme out loud. Pieces 1, 2, and 3. Piece 4 stops at the sculpture.
5. Stock phrasing and cinematic pauses. Pieces 2 and 3. "In equal measure," "the wounded man and the wind," "emotional architecture," and "no precedent" are the ones a reader hears immediately.
6. Key lessons written as balanced policy sentences. All four. They are fenced off as morals, so they damage the stories less than the in-scene glosses. Pieces 2, 3, and 4 are still hard to say aloud, and each repeats a point the story already made.

One rule

Once a gesture, a number, or a line of dialogue has made the point, stop. Do not add a sentence that names the meaning. Save abstract wording for the Key lesson line.
