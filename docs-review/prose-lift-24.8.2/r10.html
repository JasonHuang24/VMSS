Resource 10: Memory, Repair, and Moral Lag

A Behavioral Philosophy Textbook Excerpt — The 10:1 Ratio and the Architecture of Slow Trust

The STI operates on a 10:1 penalty-to-recovery ratio: trust is approximately ten times harder to rebuild than it is to lose. This number is one of the doctrine’s most emotionally contested design choices. Critics call it punitive and defenders call it realistic. The Charter calls it “calibrated to how trust actually operates.” This resource examines why the ratio exists, what it produces, what it costs and whether the cost is worth paying. It approaches these as a structural analysis of what trust is and how consequence-based systems must handle it, rather than as a policy debate.

Why Trust Is Asymmetric

The 10:1 ratio formalizes an observable truth about how trust operates in every human society that has ever existed. It was not chosen as an arbitrary policy parameter.

Trust accumulates slowly because it is built through repeated observation. A person who behaves reliably once has given you one data point; a person who behaves reliably a hundred times has given you a pattern. Trust is made of that pattern: the aggregate of acts over time, which produces a prediction about future behavior. Building the pattern takes time because each new act adds incrementally to an existing record. A year of reliable conduct builds a year’s worth of trust, and ten years builds ten years’ worth. The accumulation is approximately linear, with each act of positive conduct adding a roughly equal increment to the trust prediction.

Trust collapses fast because a single act of betrayal does more than subtract one data point from the pattern. It reframes the pattern. A person who was reliable for ten years and then betrays you has lost far more than one-tenth of your trust. The betrayal tells you their reliability was not what you thought it was. The pattern you observed was either incomplete (they were always capable of this), conditional (they were reliable only under certain circumstances you did not identify) or deteriorating (something changed and you did not detect it in time). The betrayal contaminates the entire prior record, because an outcome the record did not predict has invalidated its value as a predictor.

The asymmetry follows from how information is processed, and it carries no moral judgment. The 10:1 ratio formalizes the informational asymmetry between building a predictive pattern (slow, incremental, roughly linear) and invalidating one (fast, discontinuous, retroactively contaminating). The ratio is calibrated to how trust actually operates, rather than to how the civilization wishes it operated.

The Boundary-Riding Closure

The 10:1 ratio serves a second architectural function beyond formalizing trust: it closes the boundary-riding exploit.

Boundary-riding is the strategy of sustained low-level harm that stays below any single reassignment threshold. Consider a citizen who commits one minor harassment per month, each individually clearable and each individually below the severity threshold for escalation. The system must eventually recognize the resulting pattern as meaningful. Without the 10:1 ratio, the citizen could clear each infraction and reset to baseline, maintaining an indefinitely clean active record while producing sustained harm at the community level.

The 10:1 ratio prevents this by making clearance slower than accumulation. A minor infraction costs trust at the penalty rate; clearing it and demonstrating corrective conduct restores trust at one-tenth the penalty rate. If the citizen commits another infraction before the first is fully restored, the second penalty compounds on top of the incomplete recovery from the first. The trajectory slopes downward even though each individual act is clearable, because the recovery between acts is structurally insufficient to restore the baseline before the next act arrives.

The boundary-rider’s strategy produces a slow, compounding decline that eventually makes their behavioral pattern legible to the system. They are not punished for any single act; they are scored on a trajectory that the 10:1 ratio ensures they cannot flatten through selective clearance. Over time the asymmetry turns a potential exploit into a losing strategy. It does so without detecting the strategy, which would require reading intent the doctrine does not penalize. Under the ratio’s recovery constraint, the mathematical dynamics of repeated low-level harm are inherently self-defeating.

What the Ratio Costs

The 10:1 ratio produces the outcome the doctrine intends: slow trust rebuilding, boundary-riding closure, and a score that reflects the informational reality of how trust operates. It also produces costs, which the doctrine acknowledges but considers acceptable.

Cost one: moral lag. A citizen who commits a serious breach, takes full responsibility, makes restitution and demonstrates sustained behavioral correction for years will still carry a depressed STI score long after their actual conduct has returned to pre-breach levels. The score lags behind the reality. The citizen’s present character may be exemplary, yet their score still reflects the breach, because the 10:1 recovery rate has not yet restored the trust the breach destroyed. The citizen and the people around them can see the gap between who the citizen is now and what the score says, and the gap feels unjust because the score describes a historical debt rather than a current reality.

The doctrine’s response: the STI formula is weighted on best outcomes across the behavioral record (§5.11 — trajectory weighting). A citizen demonstrating sustained improvement is scored on the trajectory rather than the trough. Trajectory weighting does not eliminate the moral lag, since the 10:1 ratio still governs the recovery speed. It does prevent the lag from compounding into a trap, because the system gives credit for the direction of movement as well as the current position. A citizen whose trajectory is upward is treated differently from a citizen with the same score whose trajectory is flat or declining.

Cost two: the perception of double punishment. A citizen who has already experienced the social consequences of a breach (public visibility, relationship damage, professional exclusion, community judgment) may feel that slow STI recovery is a second punishment on top of what they have already absorbed. The score continues to penalize them for something the social environment has already processed. In the social sense, the citizen has “done their time”; in the metric sense, the score says they have not.

The doctrine’s response: STI is a reliability prediction and does not function as a punishment mechanism. It measures whether enough positive data has accumulated to restore the prediction to pre-breach confidence, and whether the citizen has suffered enough plays no part. A citizen who has been socially forgiven has been forgiven by the people who know them. The STI serves the people who do not know them: the stranger evaluating whether to enter a contract, the employer evaluating whether to hire, the district evaluating whether to accept a new resident. Those parties lack the social context that produced forgiveness and have only the score. The score must therefore reflect the informational reality of the breach rather than the emotional reality of the forgiveness, because the score’s audience is different from the forgiveness’s audience.

Cost three: the repair asymmetry. A citizen who makes extraordinary repair (public admission, private restitution, community accountability, sustained behavioral correction) receives no extraordinary credit in the STI. The 10:1 ratio has no “repair bonus” that accelerates recovery for citizens who do everything right, so a citizen who repairs and a citizen who merely stops offending recover at the same base rate. The trajectory weighting gives credit for sustained improvement, but it does not distinguish between improvement through active repair and improvement through passive cessation.

The doctrine’s response: this is a known design limitation, and a deliberate one. A repair bonus would create a gaming vector. Citizens could commit breaches and then perform dramatic public repair for accelerated recovery, producing a cycle of harm-then-performance that the system rewards. The 10:1 ratio’s uniformity prevents this exploit. Genuine repair receives no metric premium, and neither does performed repair. The metric cannot tell them apart, so it treats both identically to avoid incentivizing the performed version. The social environment, through the public signal component, can tell the difference, and the public signal input accelerates the trajectory accordingly. The base rate remains 10:1 regardless of repair quality.

The Philosophy Beneath the Ratio

The 10:1 ratio is ultimately a statement about the relationship between conduct and time. The civilization claims that consequence follows conduct, and the ratio specifies how long consequence follows it: much longer than the conduct itself took to commit.

A betrayal that took five seconds to commit takes years to recover from in the STI. This temporal asymmetry is deliberate rather than bureaucratic. It formalizes a truth the doctrine considers foundational: the damage a harmful act produces outlasts the moment of the act. The damage radiates forward in time through the victim’s experience, through the community’s recalibrated trust, and through every future interaction the actor has with people who know or learn about the breach. The STI’s slow recovery rate mirrors the slow dissipation of those downstream effects. The score returns to baseline approximately when those effects have dissipated enough that the pre-breach trust prediction is again defensible.

This is what the Charter means by “calibrated to how trust actually operates.” The ratio is calibrated to the informational reality of how the scoring audience, the population that relies on the score as a trust prediction, processes the evidence of a breach. What feels fair to the person being scored does not set it. The population’s trust in the actor does not return to pre-breach levels the moment the actor stops offending. It returns slowly, act by act, as new data gradually outweighs the contaminating effect of the breach on the prior prediction. The 10:1 ratio formalizes the speed at which that recovery occurs in observable human social systems.

The Deeper Question

The doctrine leaves one question open, because the civilization must answer it through lived experience rather than constitutional text: is moral lag a feature or a cost?

The feature argument: moral lag ensures that trust is earned rather than declared. A citizen cannot announce “I have changed” and receive immediate metric restoration. They must demonstrate change over time, under observation, at the pace at which the population’s trust naturally rebuilds. The lag is the system being honest that trust is a trajectory, and trajectories take time to establish.

The cost argument: moral lag penalizes the present for the past. A citizen who has genuinely changed is still scored as if they haven’t, because the system’s recovery rate has not caught up to their actual behavioral improvement; the system does not disbelieve them. The citizen lives in the gap between who they are and what the score says, and that gap comes from the ratio’s design rather than from the citizen’s conduct.

Both arguments are correct, because the feature and the cost are the same thing: slow trust recovery protects the population from premature restoration and, at the same time, penalizes the individual for historical debt. The question is which effect the civilization values more.

The doctrine chose the protection, and the 10:1 ratio is the formal expression of that choice. The civilization decided that the cost of penalizing genuine change (moral lag for the individual) is lower than the cost of premature restoration (vulnerability for the population). The ratio is load-bearing because the trust prediction the STI provides is load-bearing: it gates contracts, partnerships, domain access, and phasing eligibility. If the prediction resets too fast, the audience cannot trust it, and then it serves no one, including the citizen it describes.

A student who finds the ratio too harsh has noticed something real, because moral lag is a genuine cost. A student who sees that cost as the price of a trust prediction the population can rely on understands why the doctrine pays it. The resource was designed to bring the student to a further question: should the civilization periodically re-examine the ratio through the Article XI gauntlet, testing whether the 10:1 number is still calibrated to observed trust dynamics as the civilization matures and the population’s experience of trust evolves? The ratio can be re-evaluated, but because it is load-bearing, it cannot be changed casually.
