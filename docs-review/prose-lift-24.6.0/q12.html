Question 12
Creative Simulation Writing (Meritboard Rules)
"Creative writing session, you're going to write a simulation. One catch, meritboard rules!"
Medium to write / Extreme to win Any level · Meritboard rules scale the difficulty automatically · Session 2 is harder than session 1

Grade-Tier Responses

F: The student writes fan fiction: a character has adventures in VMSS, with cool action scenes in -3 and a love story in Sanctuary. The narrative is entertaining but doctrinally weightless. It tests no mechanism, reveals no interaction between systems and surfaces no edge case; it reads like a Star Wars script set in VMSS, and the professor's first-week filter has just caught a student who survived it. This is the most accessible assignment in the course and also the most ruthlessly competitive. Every student can write a story, but not every student can write one that functions as case law.
D-grade response: The student writes a story that references doctrine without stress-testing it. "A resident commits a crime and gets reassigned. Life is hard in the lower layer. They reflect on their choices." The mechanisms (STI drops, reassignment triggers, UBI reduction) are set dressing that bears no structural load in the narrative; remove the VMSS terminology and the story could be set anywhere. The assignment required VMSS as the subject, and the student used it as a setting. The difference between set dressing and structural integration is the difference between mentioning gravity and writing a story where gravity is the plot.
C-grade understanding: The student picks a single mechanism and illustrates it. Perhaps a story about the 10:1 STI penalty-to-recovery ratio, where a resident makes one mistake and spends years rebuilding, with the math of recovery woven into the narrative. Or a story about phasing, where a Sanctuary resident's STI dips below 85 and they phase back to Main. It is doctrinally accurate and mechanically focused, a solid simulation, but it tests only one system in isolation, and the existing dossier already has stories like this. The student is competent with the doctrine's moving parts but hasn't yet shown how those parts interact when they collide. Single-mechanism simulations are necessary, since they built the archive, but they're the floor, not the ceiling.
B-grade response: The student's simulation reveals an interaction between two or more systems that isn't obvious from reading them separately. Maybe the Savings Circulation Mandate activates in a district at the same time a major employer triggers the Overtime Premium Protocol, and the combined economic pressure produces a behavioral pattern the STI system reads as a trend. The student traced the interaction, made it legible in narrative, and produced something the reader couldn't have derived from the Charter alone. The story teaches, which is the threshold between illustration and contribution: it shows what happens when multiple doctrinal systems operate simultaneously on the same population, and the emergent behavior is the discovery.
A-grade response: The student's simulation discovers a genuine edge case, exploit, or tension the doctrine hasn't explicitly addressed. The Deathless Gold Rush discovered the continuity parity exploit and produced federal law. The Immortal Influencer demonstrated the §9.8 regulatory evolution cycle. The Iara Voss simulation dramatized the founding core reframe. An A-grade simulation finds an interaction, gap, tension or exploit that the existing 94 simulations haven't covered, and it pressures the doctrine instead of only illustrating it. The outcome is either "the doctrine handles this through composable mechanisms" or "this is a genuine gap the architect should look at," and both are valuable. A student who surfaces a gap the doctrine can't close has contributed more than one who wrote a flawless illustration of a well-understood mechanism, because the gap is a discovery and the illustration is a demonstration.
A+ / graduate-level response: The student writes a simulation that changes the doctrine without breaking it: it reveals an interaction important enough to demand a new federal law, a new regulatory clarification, or a Charter-level acknowledgment. The story produces doctrine the way the Deathless Gold Rush produced Backup Vessel Parity. The architect reads it and says "that's real — I need to address this." The simulation becomes case law, stamped with a doctrine snapshot version and entered in the archive, and the student has contributed to the constitutional development of the civilization. That has happened exactly three times in the real project: Claude produced the Deathless Gold Rush, Grok produced the Immortal Influencer, and Claude produced the Iara Voss simulation. Each one changed the doctrine, so the trophy contender is competing against AI models that have already done this work. Session 2 raises the stakes. The room has seen each other's work, easy discoveries are taken, and students must show growth and range; a student who follows a -3 story in session 1 with another -3 story in session 2 is one-dimensional. The entertainment factor matters. Doctrinal accuracy is the floor, but the best simulations are gripping narratives that make the reader feel like they lived inside the consequence. New doctrine is one win condition, and a gripping narrative that makes existing doctrine visceral is another. The peer grading under Meritboard rules naturally surfaces it, because a story that grips the room collects signatures regardless of whether it discovered a new edge case.

Where Students Fail Catastrophically

Writing fan fiction: doctrinally weightless narrative that uses VMSS as a backdrop rather than a subject. Inventing mechanisms that don't exist. Four AI models hallucinated citations during pressure testing; don't be the fifth. The doctrine is specific enough that invented mechanisms are immediately detectable, and a student who fabricates a Charter article or whitepaper section to serve their plot has destroyed their credibility in a room full of people who read the same documents. Playing it safe. Under Meritboard rules a flawless illustration of a well-understood mechanism loses to an ambitious attempt that discovers something new, and because the grading is peer-relative, the student who plays it safe is gambling that nobody in the room took a bigger swing. Accidentally revealing a real gap and then papering over it with an invented fix. The gap was the discovery and the invented fix was the failure. The correct move is to name the gap honestly and let the story's outcome reflect the tension; inventing a mechanism to close it trades a genuine contribution for a comfortable ending.

The Deepest Layer

The assignment makes the student do what every simulation author in the project has done: sit inside the doctrine long enough to see where it bends, then write a story that makes the bend visible to someone who hasn't seen it. That is doctrinal scholarship performed through narrative, rather than creative writing. A student who understands this distinction will produce work that belongs in the archive; one who doesn't will produce work for a fiction workshop. The difference is whether the story serves the author's imagination or the architecture's integrity, and the Meritboard knows which is which.
